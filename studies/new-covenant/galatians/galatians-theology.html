Theological Thesis

What Matters Is New Creation

The theology of Galatians is not mainly a doctrine of how an individual gets right with God, though it contains one. It is the claim that at the cross of the Messiah the age to come broke into the present evil age, and that the people this made, Jew and gentile in one family, already have everything the agitators were offering to complete.

The cross has ended one world and begun another, and the Galatians are being talked back into the old one.

The Central Claim

Paul opens by saying that Jesus "gave himself for our sins to deliver us from the present evil age" (1:4) and closes by saying that "neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything, but new creation" (6:15). Between those two sentences, every argument has the same shape: the cross has ended one world and begun another, and the Galatians are being talked back into the old one. Circumcision, the calendar and the divided table are not neutral additions to the gospel. They are the furniture of the age that the cross has already closed.

That is why Paul can treat a surgical rite as apostasy (5:2–4), a withdrawn meal as a denial of the gospel (2:14), and a list of virtues as the sign of a new world (5:22–23). The question of the letter is which age the Galatians will live in.

Theology Proper

The God of Abraham Who Keeps His Word

God is the subject of the letter's verbs. He raised Jesus (1:1), called Paul from the womb, in the prophets' own words (1:15; Isaiah 49:1; Jeremiah 1:5), and revealed his Son in him (1:16), preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham (3:8), sent his Son and then the Spirit of his Son (4:4, 6), and is not mocked (6:7). Galatians does not argue for God's existence or character; it argues from God's faithfulness to a promise made four hundred and thirty years before Sinai.

Gal 3:20

God Is One

The law came "through angels by the hand of a mediator; now a mediator is not of one, but God is one." Whatever the crux of the verse, its logic is the Shema's, as N. T. Wright reads it: the one God has one people, and the promise that he made directly, with no go-between, is the ground on which that people stands.

Gal 1:6; 5:8

God Calls, and Keeps Calling

The Galatians were "called in grace" and are deserting "the one who called you"; the agitators' persuasion "is not from the one who calls you." The call that was given (1:6, an aorist participle) is also a call still going out (5:8, a present participle). Desertion is not a failure of effort but a turn away from a voice still speaking.

Gal 4:8–9

Known by God

"Now that you have come to know God, or rather to be known by God." Paul corrects himself mid-sentence so that the initiative stays where it belongs. The Galatians did not find their way to the God of Israel; they were found, and the thing they are turning back to is "things that by nature are not gods."

Christology

The Crucified Messiah Who Became a Curse

Galatians has one of the New Covenant's most concentrated pictures of the Messiah, and it is almost entirely a picture of the cross. Jesus is God's Son (1:16; 2:20; 4:4), the seed of Abraham to whom the promise was made (3:16), born of a woman and under the law (4:4), publicly placarded as crucified (3:1), the one who became a curse to redeem those under the curse (3:13), who loved me and gave himself for me (2:20), and through whom the world is crucified (6:14).

What was unveiled on the road (1:11–16)

Paul says twice that his gospel came by "revelation" (ἀποκάλυψις): "through a revelation of Jesus the Messiah" (1:12), and God's good pleasure "to reveal his Son in me" (1:16). N. T. Wright's study of Paul's Damascus texts draws six things out of the short passage.

  1. The word. Apokalypsis is the vocabulary of unveiling, something hidden in God's sphere made visible in the human one, and in Paul usually something expected on the last day disclosed ahead of time. Paul chose it to put his gospel beyond any chain of tradition; it says nothing about the kind of experience it was.
  2. A prophet's call. "From my mother's womb" (1:15) is Isaiah 49:1, 5 and Jeremiah 1:5: the first of two points at which Paul's brief account and Luke's fuller one meet, since Luke also narrates the event in the terms of a prophetic vision.
  3. Revealed to Paul as to the others. The passage is a partial parallel to 1 Corinthians 15:3–11: the Messiah was unveiled to Paul as he had been to the apostles before him.
  4. "The Son of God." Its first sense here, as in Romans 1:3–4 and in Paul's earliest Damascus preaching (Acts 9:20, the second meeting point with Luke), is Israel's Messiah rather than the later creedal sense.
  5. "In me." ἐν ἐμοί can mean to Paul or through Paul. The purpose clause, "so that I might proclaim him among the nations," makes "through" the main point, which requires "to," and the pair rules out a merely inward experience "as opposed to an outward seeing."
  6. "The gospel." In 1:11–12 it is not, in the first instance, justification by faith or the inclusion of the gentiles, but the announcement that "Jesus, the Messiah, is risen from the dead and is the lord of the world," which is why the revelation can take for granted what 1:1 states (Resurrection below the waterline).

Wright's caution stands beside all six: the passage shows how Paul interpreted what happened to him, not "what precisely it was that happened"; for that the texts are 1 Corinthians 9:1 and 15:8. Wright's reading: adopted

"Faith of the Messiah" (πίστις Χριστοῦ)

Twice in 2:16 and again in 2:20 and 3:22 the letter speaks of pistis with Christ in the genitive (2:16 also says, in plain words, that "we believed in the Messiah Jesus"; 3:26, "through faith, in the Messiah Jesus," is a different construction). Read as an objective genitive the phrase means "faith in Christ," a person's trust; read as a subjective genitive it means "the faith (or faithfulness) of Christ," his own fidelity unto death. Richard Hays argued the second from the narrative substructure of 3:1–4:11, and Michael Gorman and N. T. Wright build on it; Ronald Fung and most of the older commentary tradition hold the first.

Objective · faith in ChristJustified by trusting him

These pages do not force the choice. Both readings agree that human faith is required (2:16 says "we believed in the Messiah Jesus" in the same breath), and both agree that the ground of justification is the Messiah's death. What the subjective reading adds is that Jesus's own faithfulness, not only his fate, is the thing the Galatians were called to share. Grammar disputed Theology shared

Pneumatology

The Spirit as the Proof and the Power

Gordon Fee's reading of Galatians rests on an observation that is easy to miss under the justification debate: Paul's first and decisive argument is about the Spirit. "Did you receive the Spirit by works of the law or by hearing with faith?" (3:2). The Galatians had an experience, and Paul treats it as evidence: whatever gave them the Spirit is what makes them God's people, and it was not the law. The Spirit appears some eighteen times in the letter, and the second half is built on it. Luke has Peter make the same argument at the Jerusalem council: God "bore witness to them by giving them the Holy Spirit just as he did to us" (Acts 15:8), and the yoke Peter will not lay on them (15:10) is the yoke Paul names in 5:1 (the Context page reads Luke's account on its own terms).

Gal 3:14

The Spirit Is the Promise

"So that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith." The content of Abraham's blessing, for the nations, turns out to be the Spirit. That identification lets Paul say the Galatians already possess what Abraham was promised, and that nothing added later can improve it.

Gal 4:6

The Spirit Makes Sons

"Because you are sons, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, Abba, Father." The sending of the Spirit mirrors the sending of the Son (4:4). Sonship is not a status awaiting proof; it is spoken aloud, in the family's own word, by the Spirit.

Gal 5:16–25

The Spirit Replaces the Guardian

"If you are led by the Spirit, you are not under the law." The agitators' best objection was that gentiles without Torah would have no moral guide. Paul's answer is not a shorter law; it is a Person who leads, opposes the flesh, and grows a character the law could only describe.

Wider scriptural context: the Spirit promised to the seed

Paul does not quote them in Galatians, but two prophetic texts stand behind his equation of Abraham's blessing with the Spirit (3:14). In Isaiah 44:3 God promises, "I will pour my Spirit upon your seed, and my blessing upon your offspring," setting Spirit, blessing and seed in one line; in Ezekiel 36:26–27 he promises a new heart and "my Spirit within you," so that the people will walk in his statutes from within. Read against that background, "the promise of the Spirit" (3:14) and the Spirit who "leads" (5:18) are what the prophets said the restored people would receive. Soteriology

Justification by Faith, Read Three Ways

"Justify" (δικαιόω) occurs eight times in Galatians, six of them in 2:16–17 and 3:8–11, and "righteousness" four times. The verb is a law-court word, "to declare in the right," and the question the letter raises is what court, what charge, and what verdict Paul has in mind. The modern debate has three main answers, and the works cited below include advocates of each.

The classic Protestant reading

Right standing for sinners

Justification is God's verdict of "righteous" on the ungodly, grounded in the Messiah's death and received by faith alone. "Works of the law" means any human doing offered as the ground of acceptance, and the agitators' error is the perennial one of religion: earning what can only be given.

Fung · Keller · the Reformers
The "new perspective"

Who belongs to the people

Second Temple Judaism was not a religion of earning (Sanders), so "works of the law" are the Torah's identity markers, circumcision, food and calendar, which is exactly what Galatians fights over. Justification is God's declaration of who is in the covenant family, and the verdict is "by faith" so that Jew and gentile are one.

Sanders · Wright · Stendahl · Thomas
Allegiance and incorporation

Faith as loyalty to the King

Pistis carries the sense of allegiance to Jesus as Messiah and Lord; and righteousness is "incorporated," ours by union with the one who is righteous (Bird). Justification is then neither a bare verdict nor only membership but the status of those who are in the Messiah and loyal to him.

Bates · Bird · Campbell

What Galatians itself constrains

Whichever reading one adopts, three things in the text hold. First, the "works of the law" actually at issue are circumcision (2:3; 5:2), table fellowship (2:12) and the calendar (4:10): boundary-markers between Jew and gentile, not moral effort in general. Second, the verdict is pronounced on gentiles as gentiles, "God justifies the nations by faith" (3:8), and that is the scandal. Third, the alternative to justification by faith is not justification by sincerity or by trying hard but "the Messiah died for nothing" (2:21): the ground is the cross or there is no ground.

These pages read the letter with the second answer's historical setting and the third's account of faith, while keeping the first's insistence that the verdict is a gift to the undeserving. Matthew Thomas's study of how second-century readers understood "works of the law" supports the boundary-marker reading from the earliest reception. Working synthesis

Participation

Crucified with the Messiah, Alive in Him

The letter's most personal sentence is also its most radical theology: "I have been crucified with the Messiah; it is no longer I who live, but the Messiah lives in me; and the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith of the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me" (2:19–20). Michael Gorman calls this cruciformity: the believer's life is not merely forgiven by the cross but shaped like it. Constantine Campbell's study of union with Christ finds in Galatians every mode of the theme: incorporation ("in the Messiah," 3:26, 28), identification (co-crucifixion, 2:19), indwelling (2:20; 4:19), and clothing (3:27).

Union runs in both directions: the believer is in the Messiah (3:26–28) and the Messiah is in the believer (2:20; 4:19).

3:26–29 as one sequence

The verses the diagram draws on are a single sequence, and its middle term is baptism. "In the Messiah Jesus you are all sons of God, through faith" (3:26); "for as many of you as were baptized into the Messiah have put on the Messiah" (3:27); "there is neither Jew nor Greek … for you are all one in the Messiah Jesus" (3:28); "and if you are the Messiah's, then you are Abraham's seed, heirs according to promise" (3:29). Sonship is through faith; baptism is where the Messiah is put on like a garment; the three pairs lose their rank because everyone wears the same one; and the family is Abraham's because its members belong to the Messiah, the singular seed of 3:16. The agitators had an entry rite in mind. Paul names the one the Galatians had already undergone, the only rite the letter mentions without a quarrel.

What union is not
  • a mystical absorption that erases the self (Paul still says "I live")
  • a metaphor for admiring Jesus's example
  • a private experience with no bearing on the table (2:19–20 is Paul's answer to the Antioch table of 2:11–14)
What union does
  • relocates identity: the "I" that was "in Judaism" (1:13) has died with the Messiah
  • makes the Messiah's faithfulness the shape of the believer's life (2:20)
  • dissolves the ranks of Jew/Greek, slave/free, male/female as grounds of standing (3:28)
  • turns ethics into formation: "until the Messiah is formed in you" (4:19)

Why Paul answers Antioch with 2:19–20

The confrontation with Cephas (2:11–14) runs straight into the theology of 2:15–21 without a seam. That is not an accident of dictation. Paul's answer to a withdrawn table is a death: the Jew who was "in Judaism," with its boundaries, has been crucified with the Messiah, and the one who now lives cannot rebuild what he tore down (2:18) without making himself a transgressor. Union with the crucified one is what makes the one table necessary. N. T. Wright hears the Jewish metaphor of resurrection in the paragraph, Israel's rescue from exile "coming true in a new way in the person of Paul himself," and insists that the metaphor has a concrete referent: a renewed identity, and table-fellowship with all who have likewise "died and been raised," whose "sole badge of identity" is now the faithfulness of the Messiah (Resurrection below the waterline).

Torah

The Law: Holy, Temporary, and Not Against the Promises

Galatians is not an anti-law letter, though it has been read as one. Paul's claims about the Torah are precise. It came 430 years after the promise and cannot annul it (3:17). It was "added because of transgressions, until the seed should come" (3:19). It is not against the promises, but it could not give life (3:21). It functioned as a guardian, a παιδαγωγός, until the Messiah (3:24–25). And it is fulfilled, whole, in one word: love of neighbor (5:14). Taken together: the law had a job, a term, and an end-date, and the end-date has come. E. P. Sanders's formula, that for Paul the solution precedes the problem, fits the letter exactly: Paul reasons from the Messiah back to the law's limits, not from the law's failure forward to the Messiah.

N. T. Wright's Climax of the Covenant reads 3:10–14 as the covenant's own curse, exile, exhausted on the Messiah so that the promise could proceed to the nations.

The count Paul makes

Abraham was counted righteous in Genesis 15:6. He was circumcised in Genesis 17, more than thirteen years later (Ishmael was thirteen that day, 17:25). The law came at Sinai, 430 years after the promise (3:17; Exodus 12:40, whose Greek text counts the years in Canaan and Egypt together). Paul makes the same before-and-after argument in Romans 4:9–12, and draws the same conclusion: whatever circumcision and the law are, they are not what made Abraham righteous or his family a family. In 3:15 the "ratified covenant" is a human will, the analogy; in 3:17 it is the covenant "ratified beforehand by God," the promise to Abraham. The scene most readers have in view is Genesis 15, in which God alone walked between the pieces while Abraham slept, binding himself and asking nothing (Genesis 17 restates the promise with circumcision attached); that is why Paul can say no later law annuls it. The site's Abraham profile sets out the ceremony and Paul's use of the chronology.

What the law was for
  • to expose and confine transgression "until the seed" (3:19, 22)
  • to guard Israel as a minor is guarded, with authority that ends at majority (3:23–25; 4:1–2)
  • to be fulfilled, as a whole, in love (5:14)
  • to be read as Scripture that testifies to the promise (3:8; 4:21)
What it was never for
  • to give life or righteousness (3:21)
  • to mark the edge of Abraham's family (3:7, 29)
  • to be the ground on which gentiles are received (2:16; 5:2–4)
  • to be taken up partially: "he is obligated to keep the whole law" (5:3)

"Ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator" (3:19–20)

Paul's hearers knew that angels had been at Sinai. The Greek Bible's Deuteronomy 33:2 has the Lord coming from Sinai "with angels at his right hand"; Jubilees opens with the angel of the presence dictating to Moses the revelation that begins with creation (1:27–2:1); Josephus has Herod say that Israel learned "the most holy part of our law" through ἄγγελοι, messengers or angels (Antiquities 15.136); Stephen and the letter to the Hebrews repeat it as common knowledge (Acts 7:38, 53; Hebrews 2:2). In the Jewish texts, and in Stephen's speech, the angels are the law's glory; Hebrews 2:2–3 already uses them for a comparison in which the word spoken through angels ranks below the word spoken through the Lord. Paul grants the fact and reverses its value: a law that came through angels and through Moses came at two removes, while the promise was spoken by God to Abraham with no one in between, "and God is one" (3:20). The verse has drawn several hundred interpretations by one classic count that Longenecker reports, but its direction is widely, though not universally, agreed: mediation, which tradition read as honor, Paul reads as distance (a minority hears instead an affirmation of the law's authorized place). The στοιχεῖα and the strangest sentence in the letter

In 4:3 and 4:9 Paul says that "we" were enslaved under "the elements of the world," and then asks the gentile Galatians, who want to take up the Torah's calendar, how they can "turn back again to the weak and beggarly elements." He has put Jewish observance of the law and pagan observance of the gods in a single category. The word may mean elemental principles, the physical elements, or elemental spirits; the commentaries divide, and Richard Longenecker lays out the options. But the effect is clear on any reading: outside the Messiah, the law functioned for Israel as the gods did for the nations, as a guardian for minors, and to go to it now is to go backward in time. Read with the "powers" of Paul's other letters, the elements are custodians whose authority ended at the cross, which is how the site's Second Temple mechanics study places them. Sense of the word: plausible options

"The law of the Messiah" (6:2)

Having said that believers are not under the law, Paul tells them to "fulfill the law of the Messiah" by bearing one another's burdens. The phrase is not a new code. Read with 5:14, it is the Torah as the Messiah fulfilled it: the love of neighbor, now lived in the pattern of the one who "loved me and gave himself for me." Barclay's Obeying the Truth shows how carefully 5:13–6:10 answers the charge that a law-free gospel is a morally empty one.

Ecclesiology

One Family of Abraham

The church in Galatians is a family before it is anything else: Abraham's seed, God's sons, heirs, brothers and sisters, children of the free woman, the household of faith. Paul addresses the readers as "brothers" nine times. Bruce Longenecker's Triumph of Abraham's God describes the letter as a transformation of identity, and the family language is where the transformation shows. The question "who is in?" has become "who are these people to one another?"

Gal 3:28

One in the Messiah

"There is neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor free, no male and female, for you are all one in the Messiah Jesus." J. Daniel Hays reads the verse as the climax of a biblical theology of the nations: the family God promised Abraham is multi-ethnic by design. Paul does not say the differences vanish (he still writes to Jews and gentiles); he says they no longer rank. Nor did the redefinition of Abraham's children begin with him: John the Baptist had warned that God could raise children for Abraham from stones (Matthew 3:9), and Jesus told his hearers that Abraham's children do Abraham's works (John 8:39). The Abraham profile gathers the Gospel sayings.

Gal 4:19

A Mother in Labor

"My little children, for whom I am again in the anguish of childbirth until the Messiah is formed in you." Susan Eastman shows how this maternal image governs 4:12–20: the apostle's authority is a mother's, and the church's formation is a birth, not a certification.

Gal 6:10

The Household of Faith

"Let us do good to all, and especially to those of the household of faith." Joseph Hellerman's work on the church as family explains why this is not favoritism: in the ancient world the household was the unit of survival, and Paul is telling a mixed group of Jews and gentiles that they are now each other's kin.

Gentiles stay gentiles

The family is not formed by gentiles becoming Jews, which is the agitators' model, nor by Jews ceasing to be Jews. Matthew Thiessen's Jewish Paul presses the point that the promise was for the nations as nations (3:8), so that a circumcised gentile would be a contradiction of the blessing rather than its completion. "The Israel of God" in 6:16 has been read as the whole church and as the Jewish believers within it; the pages hold the question open. 6:16 reference disputed

Liberty

Freedom: From What, For What

"For freedom the Messiah has set us free; stand firm, then, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery" (5:1). Freedom-words occur eleven times in Galatians, and almost all of them cluster around the allegory of the two women (4:21–31) and the exhortation that follows (5:1, 13). Paul's freedom is not autonomy. It is release from custody, the law's guardianship and the elements' slavery, into a life whose shape is service: "through love serve one another" (5:13).

Freedom from
  • the curse of the law (3:13)
  • the guardian's custody (3:23–25; 4:1–3)
  • the weak and beggarly elements (4:9)
  • the present evil age (1:4)
  • the yoke the agitators were fitting (5:1)
Freedom for
  • sonship and the cry of "Abba" (4:6–7)
  • "faith working through love" (5:6)
  • serving one another through love (5:13)
  • walking by the Spirit (5:16, 25)
  • the new creation (6:15)

The false alternative the Galatians were offered

The agitators' case assumed that the only alternatives were Torah or chaos: law or license. Paul refuses both. Freedom used "as an opportunity for the flesh" (5:13) is not freedom but a new slavery, and the "works of the flesh" he lists (5:19–21) are mostly the things that tear a community apart. The free life has a form, the fruit of the Spirit, and against such things, he notes with a trace of a smile, "there is no law" (5:23). John Barclay's reading of 5:13–6:10 is the fullest account of how Paul answers the law-or-license dilemma with a third thing.

The Spirit's Harvest

The Fruit of the Spirit

The nine-fold "fruit" of 5:22–23 is the letter's portrait of what a person looks like when the Spirit, not the law, is the guide. Three things about the list matter theologically before the nine are read one by one. The word is singular: one fruit, nine aspects, as against the plural "works" of the flesh. It is grown, not performed: Paul's contrast is between what the flesh does and what the Spirit produces. And it describes God's own character, which is why BibleProject's study of the list begins by saying these qualities "reflect God's character and the behavior of those living according to God's Spirit."

Works of the flesh (5:19–21)
  • plural, and "obvious": a catalogue of what people do
  • fifteen items, more than half of them communal: enmity, strife, jealousy, fits of anger, rivalries, dissensions, factions, envy
  • the life of a church that is "biting and devouring one another" (5:15)
  • "those who do such things will not inherit the kingdom of God"
Fruit of the Spirit (5:22–23)
  • singular: one fruit with nine facets
  • grown by the Spirit in people who "walk by" and "keep in step with" him (5:16, 25)
  • the life of a church that bears one another's burdens (6:2)
  • "against such things there is no law"
"Flesh" is not the body. In 5:16–24 σάρξ names a power and an orientation, the self living from its own resources and against the Spirit, not physical existence as such; in the same letter Paul can say that "the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith" (2:20), where the word means simply mortal life. Most of the "works of the flesh" are social (enmity, strife, factions, envy), and the body is where the Spirit's fruit becomes visible. The contrast is between two powers at work in a person, not between a soul and a body. N. T. Wright calls them "the two spheres of life, character and behaviour in which a human being can live," flesh being for Paul "always corruptible, decaying and often rebellious as well," and the Spirit the gift through which the future inheritance is assured.

Not a shorter law

It is tempting to turn the nine into a checklist and so smuggle the agitators' model back in through the ethics. Paul's grammar resists it. Fruit is the Spirit's (5:22); the believer's part is to "walk" and "keep in step" (5:16, 25), to "sow to the Spirit" (6:8), and to refuse the flesh its opportunity (5:13). Fee's God's Empowering Presence and Barclay's Obeying the Truth agree from different angles that the Spirit in Galatians is not an aid to law-keeping but its replacement as the principle of the moral life.

Staurology

The Cross and the New Creation

Richard Hays proposed three focal images for New Covenant ethics: community, cross, and new creation. Galatians 6:10–16 contains all three within seven verses, and the cross stands in the middle. "Far be it from me to boast except in the cross of our Lord Jesus the Messiah, by which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world. For neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything, but new creation" (6:14–15). The cross is where the old world ended and the new began, and the agitators' whole program was an attempt to keep the old world's marks meaningful.

Cross and crucifixion language occurs seven times in the letter; the resurrection is named once (1:1), and, on Wright's reading below, assumed everywhere.

Community · 6:10

The Household of Faith

The new creation is not only an individual's inner renewal; it is a people: those who do good to all and especially to one another, bearing each other's burdens.

Cross · 6:14

The World Crucified

The cross is a boundary in time. On one side, the age in which circumcision and uncircumcision organize humanity; on the other, a world where they "count for nothing."

New Creation · 6:15

What Matters

Paul's rule (6:16) is the sentence the letter has been building: not a mark in the flesh, but the new world the cross began and the Spirit is growing.

Why the agitators fled the cross

Paul's final diagnosis is that they press circumcision "only in order that they may not be persecuted for the cross of the Messiah" (6:12). A circumcised church would be a recognizable Jewish sect; an uncircumcised one, boasting in a crucified man, was an offense to synagogue and city alike (5:11). The theology and the sociology meet here: the cross makes the church a new thing in the world, and the agitators wanted the church to be an old thing.

Resurrection below the waterline

Galatians names the resurrection once, in its first verse, and N. T. Wright's study of resurrection in Paul opens its pages on the letter with that fact: "Resurrection is not a main theme in Galatians, but neither the overall argument nor the detail is comprehensible without it." Paul's first sentence says that the Messiah gave himself "to deliver us from the present evil age" (1:4), which is the language of a Pharisee who believes the age to come has broken in, and the verse before it names the "something" that made him believe it: God the Father "raised him from the dead" (1:1). The revelation of 1:12 and 1:16 assumes the same thing: the gospel unveiled to Paul was, in Wright's words, that "Jesus, the Messiah, is risen from the dead and is the lord of the world" (above). After that the letter talks about the cross, and Wright's rule for reading it is that "we have to supply a reference to the resurrection … to make sense of each reference to the cross," since a Messiah who stayed dead would have delivered no one (1 Corinthians 15:17). His image is a hull. The resurrection runs "just below the waterline, supporting those other aspects of Paul's thought which protrude more obviously because he needs them for his 'above-the-surface' argument."

One mention above the line; eight places where, on Wright's reading, the argument rests on what it does not say.

Already happened · resurrection in the past tense
  • 2:19–20. Paul "died" and now "lives." Wright takes both of the usual readings to be in view, Paul's conversion and baptism and, behind them, Jesus' death and resurrection, and hears the Jewish metaphor of resurrection, Israel's rescue from exile, "coming true in a new way in the person of Paul himself." The metaphor has a concrete referent: a renewed identity, and a table shared with everyone who has similarly "died and been raised" (Union).
  • 3:10–14 and 3:22. Israel under the curse, then the blessing reaching the nations; everything shut up under sin, then the promise given. The implicit story is "Israel's subjugation and the divine rescue," the story Jewish texts told with the picture of resurrection, and the juxtaposition of negative and positive "embodies the pattern of death and resurrection."
  • 4:1–7. The slaves set free and made children: the exodus retold, as in Romans 8, with "resurrection overtones" Wright calls powerfully present. Set Galatians 4 beside 1 Corinthians 15:20–28 and "substantially the same ideas are involved."
Still to come · resurrection in the future tense
  • 5:5. "The hope of righteousness." Paul "could easily have written" the hope of resurrection; he chose the status believers will have at the last day over the state they will be in, because that is what Galatians is arguing about. Resurrection and justification are linked in his mind "past, present and future" (Justification).
  • 5:21. "Inherit the kingdom of God": language "any first-century Pharisee would have lined up at once with the hope of resurrection," and the present life in the Spirit is "the guarantee of the future inheritance."
  • 6:7–9. Sowing and reaping. Not the sowing-as-dying of 1 Corinthians 15:35–44 but "behaviour and its long-term results"; "eternal life" is life in the age to come, the counterpart of 1:4's present evil age; and the call to keep doing good without tiring matches 1 Corinthians 15:58. The inheritance is "never an incentive to shrug one's shoulders and wait passively for the final divine rescue operation."
  • 6:14–16. Cross, then new creation: a sequence that "has all the marks of resurrection upon it, and moreover resurrection seen precisely as new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17). Redemption is the renewal of creation, not a rescue from it, and the peace and mercy of 6:16 carry Israel's long hope for a world made new.
What this changes about reading the letter. It explains how Paul can treat the cross as the end of one world and the start of another (the thesis above) without ever arguing that it is: the resurrection "gives retrospective meaning to Jesus' death," and the Galatians, who received the Spirit, already live on the far side of it. It also explains the letter's forward lean (the Spirit's future tense). Wright's verdict is that this inaugurated eschatology differs from anything in Second Temple Judaism outside early Christianity (Qumran offers some analogies) yet is "only explicable as a mutation from within the worldview of the second-Temple Jews," and that it is "remarkably well developed in Paul's mind, whenever we date Galatians (but especially if we place it early, as I am inclined to do)." Named once, the resurrection turns out to be the thing the whole letter assumes. Wright's reading: adopted

Literary-Theological Pattern

The Seven Drumbeats of Grace

"Grace" (χάρις) occurs seven times in Galatians, and the occurrences are placed where the argument turns: at the frame, at the call, at the pillars' handshake, at the hinge of chapter 2, at the warning of chapter 5. John Barclay's Paul and the Gift gives the word its first-century weight: a gift was ordinarily given to the worthy and returned in honor, and the Christ-gift in Galatians is given without regard to worth, which is why any condition added to it changes what it is.

Frame

Incongruous, and therefore unconditioned, but not unconditional

Barclay's distinction keeps the letter from two misreadings. The gift is unconditioned: nothing in the Galatians qualified them for it, and nothing they add can. But it is not unconditional in the sense of expecting nothing: a gift creates a relationship, and the Christ-gift expects a return of allegiance and a life "worthy" of the giver, the "faith working through love" of 5:6. That is why Galatians can be, in the same breath, the letter of grace and the letter of the fruit.

Final Synthesis

One Free Family, by the Spirit

The theological center of Galatians can be put in one line: because the Messiah bore the curse and was raised, Abraham's one family is now constituted by faith and filled with the Spirit; therefore nothing may be added to its membership, and nothing short of new creation is its life.

New Creation

Closing Thesis

The theology of Galatians is not primarily a theology of how to be saved as an individual, though it has been fruitfully read that way. It is the theology of a new world that began at the cross and a new family that lives in it. Because the Messiah became a curse and was raised, the gift has been given to the unworthy; because the gift was given to the unworthy, the family is formed by faith and not by a mark; because the family is formed by faith, Jew and gentile eat at one table; and because the Spirit has been given to that table, the law's custody has ended and the fruit has begun. Neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts. What matters is new creation.

The crucified Messiah bears the curse, makes one free family out of Jew and gentile on the basis of faith, and gives that family the Spirit instead of a rulebook.

Interpretive Voices

The Scholars Behind This Reading

This synthesis leans on several interpreters who disagree with one another, and it says where it sides. Full citations are in the bibliography below.

N. T. Wright

Reads Galatians as the climax of the covenant: one family promised to Abraham, the curse of exile borne by the Messiah, the single seed, and justification as the declaration of who belongs. In The Resurrection of the Son of God, the resurrection as the letter's unstated premise: named once, assumed everywhere.

John M. G. Barclay

Supplies the two frames this page uses most: the Christ-gift as incongruous gift, and the ethics of 5–6 as Paul's answer to the charge that grace leaves a church without a moral compass.

Michael J. Gorman

Cruciformity: 2:19–20 as the center of Paul's spirituality, the believer's life conformed to the self-giving faithfulness of the crucified Messiah.

E. P. Sanders & Krister Stendahl

Reset the question: Judaism as covenantal nomism rather than works-righteousness, and Paul's letters as about Jew and gentile before they are about the introspective conscience.

Richard B. Hays

The faith of the Messiah as the letter's narrative substructure; Paul's intertextual reading of Genesis and Isaiah; and community, cross and new creation as the focal images of the moral life.

Gordon D. Fee

The Spirit as the experiential heart of the letter: received by faith, the proof of belonging, the guide that replaces the guardian, and the grower of the fruit.

Matthew Bates & Michael Bird

Pistis as allegiance to the enthroned King, and righteousness as "incorporated": theirs by union with the Messiah.

Ronald Fung & Timothy Keller

The classic reading, pastorally applied: justification as the verdict of "righteous" for the ungodly, and the agitators' gospel-plus as the perennial religion of self-justification.

BibleProject

The three-part map of the letter ending in new creation, and the study of the nine fruits as reflections of God's own character grown by participation with the Spirit.
