How the Letter Is Built — and How It Reads the Torah

Galatians argues twice over: once by its shape, a proof that climbs to a single verse and comes back down, and once by the way Paul reads the Torah party's own Scriptures until they point past themselves. This page is about both: the craft, and the reading.

The thesis in three lines

Not by works, but through faith, because no flesh

Before the architecture, hear the sentence the whole proof exists to support, said three times in one verse:

"…knowing that a person is not justified by works of the law (ἐξ ἔργων νόμου) but through faith of Jesus the Messiah (διὰ πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ), and we believed in the Messiah Jesus, so that we might be justified by faith of the Messiah and not by works of the law, because by works of the law no flesh will be justified." Galatians 2:16 · the last clause echoes Psalm 143:2

Not · but · because. A denial, a replacement, and a Scripture to ground it. The argument of chapters 3–4 is this sentence slowed down, and the ring that carries it is laid out below in the architecture.

✦ Two Ways the Letter Argues

Galatians is the most argued-over of Paul's letters and, on the surface, the least orderly: a narrative, a string of Scripture quotations, an allegory, two vice-and-virtue lists and a postscript in large handwriting. A linear outline catches only part of it. The letter has a design, but the design is not simply linear, and it has an argument, but much of it is conducted in someone else's words. Read those two things together and Galatians opens up.

Half One · The Craft

The shape is the sermon.

A deliberative letter with the classical parts in place; a proof arranged in six matched pairs around one verse, framed by salutation and epilogue; contrast and single theme alternating ring by ring; a repeated word stitching every seam; and a frame of two personal pleas, the second in Paul's own oversized hand. The structure does the persuading, and, on Wilson's reading, it was built to be remembered.

Half Two · The Reading

Paul meets the Torah party on Torah ground.

Genesis before Sinai, Deuteronomy's curse, Habakkuk's righteous one, Leviticus's "live by them," the hanged man, Sarah and Hagar, and one commandment that is the whole law. Paul never sidesteps the Scriptures the agitators claimed; he reads them until they testify against the agitators' use of them.

What matters is new creation, and the letter is built so that a hearer could carry that sentence home, with the whole argument folded inside it.

🏗️ The Architecture

Victor Wilson reads Galatians through the five canons of classical rhetoric, the five stages by which a speech was made: invention, arrangement, style, memory and delivery. Arrangement is where the letter's design lives, and the rest of this section follows him there; the Movement Commentary takes up style, memory and delivery in turn.

Second canon · the order of the parts, chosen to persuade

The frame: a letter with its classical parts

Robert Hall's outline, which Wilson adopts, keeps the letter form and lays a deliberative speech inside it. The salutation stands in for the proem; the proposition states what is to be proved; the proof is everything from 1:10 to 6:10; the epilogue recapitulates in Paul's own hand. What the outline does not have is as telling as what it has. There is no narration standing on its own (the story of chapters 1–2 is inside the proof, as evidence) and no refutation, because a deliberative speech urges a decision rather than rebutting charges.

No thanksgiving

Every other Pauline letter to a congregation moves from greeting to thanksgiving. Galatians moves from greeting to θαυμάζω, "I am astonished" (1:6). The missing thanksgiving is not an oversight but the letter's first rhetorical act: before Paul has said a word of argument, the form has told the Galatians how serious this is.

Two personal pleas as bookends

Wilson's description of the frame is exact: the proof falls between two end pieces of intensely personal address, first the plea to reconsider Paul's gospel (A, 1:1–9), last the epilogue "notarized" after the secretary's script by Paul's distinctively large signature (A′, 6:11–18). Argument lives inside; Paul's own voice holds the outside.

The ring: six pairs around one verse, inside two end pieces

Inside the proof (1:10–6:10), Wilson finds the arguments arranged concentrically: six pairs of units (B to G and their partners) mirror one another around 3:24, while the salutation and the epilogue (A and A′) stand outside the proof as its two end pieces, a seventh pair that frames the whole. Each unit on the way up has a partner on the way down, and the proof rises to its theological apex and falls back through balanced pairings. He calls the form chiastic for simplicity, while noting that classical rhetoricians prefer ring composition for frames this large, and he points to concentric proposals for Philippians and Colossians as evidence that the form is not foreign to Paul. John Bligh had seen a concentric Galatians a generation earlier with different results.

The alternation

Wilson's sharpest structural observation is that the rings take turns. Pairs built on an internal, oppositional contrast (faith against works, blessing against curse, Spirit against flesh) alternate with pairs that play on a single theme (circumcision, eyes, the rationale of law or of faith). The alternation holds from the outermost ring to the center, and it is the kind of pattern an audience hears without being told.

Three word-distributions that hold the ring together

A proposed ring is only as good as the evidence that the pairs really pair. Three of Wilson's observations can be checked with a concordance, and they survive the check.

Circumcision · C and C′ only

Within the proof (1:10–6:10), the rite and the party that pushed it appear only in 2:3–14 and 5:1–12, six times and five by Wilson's count, and nowhere between. The epilogue takes the word up again (6:12–15), which is what an epilogue restating the contrast should do.

Eyes · E and E′ only

ὀφθαλμοί occurs twice in the letter, at 3:1 ("before whose eyes Jesus was placarded as crucified") and 4:15 ("you would have torn out your eyes"). Both units step aside from the Scripture argument to summon the Galatians' memory of Paul among them.

Abraham · seven times in F

The patriarch is named seven times in 3:6–18 and, in the rest of the proof, only twice more: "Abraham's seed" at 3:29, the last word of G′, and the allegory's "Abraham had two sons" (4:22). F is where Paul enlists, in Wilson's phrase, "the founder and father of the people of God"; F′ revisits the theme as adoption into God's family.

The seams

Arrangement is the large picture; style, the third canon, works at the level of diction. Wilson's example from Galatians is Paul's habit of stitching one unit to the next with a repeated word: "accursed" (1:8–9) to "violently persecuting" (1:13), "privately" (2:2) to "slipped in to spy" (2:4), "works of the law" (2:16) to "works of the law" (3:2), "the free woman" (4:31) to "freedom" (5:1), "persecuted" (5:11) to "bite and devour" (5:15). The hooks make the proof flow instead of lurch, and they are how a hearer kept their place. The full list, with the Greek, is on the Movement Commentary.

Why this matters for how the letter is read. Galatians does not divide cleanly into pericopes. A reading that stops at 2:2, 2:14 or 3:5 cuts a hook in half, and a study that treats 4:21–31 without 2:15–21 misses that the two are mirror images: the same two groups, with the labels exchanged. The ring is not decoration. It is the letter's way of making each part answer another.

⚖️ What Kind of Speech Is This?

Classical rhetoric knew three species of speech, each with its own setting and aim. Which one Galatians belongs to has been argued for fifty years, and the answer changes how the narrative of chapters 1–2 is read.

Rhetorical species: disputed

Judicial · Betz
  • The courtroom genre: a defendant persuades a court of innocence after the fact.
  • Galatians as an "apologetic letter": Paul defending his apostleship and his gospel.
  • The narrative of 1:12–2:14 as a narratio, the statement of facts a defense requires.
  • Strength: judicial speeches narrate, and here is a narrative.

Betz 1975, 1979 · reported via Wilson

Deliberative · Kennedy, Hall, Witherington
  • The assembly genre: a speaker urges a decision about the future.
  • The letter's aim is not to defend Paul but to move the Galatians to a choice about the gospel.
  • Paul never recounts charges in order to rebut them; the story looks forward to the Galatians' renewed commitment.
  • The exhortation of 5:1–6:10 is a call to future action, which deliberative speech expects.

Kennedy 1984 and Hall 1987 via Wilson · Witherington 1998

Letter first · Longenecker
  • Galatians is a letter before it is a speech: a "rebuke–request" letter in the Hellenistic pattern, with rebuke (1:6–4:11) giving way to request (4:12–6:10).
  • Rhetorical analysis helps inside the sections, but the epistolary frame governs.
  • Strength: it explains the shift of tone at 4:12, "become as I am," which the speech outlines treat as incidental.

Longenecker 1990

Wilson follows Hall: the letter is deliberative, with the salutation standing in for the proem. Aristotle's observation governs the narrative question. In deliberative oratory there is little room for narration, since nobody can narrate what has not yet happened; if a story is told, it is a story of the past meant to help the hearers plan the future (Rhetoric 3.16). On that account the narrative of chapters 1–2 is not Paul's defense but his evidence, every item chosen to prove that his gospel came from God and not from Jerusalem. Wilson adds a count: by his reckoning "gospel" occurs thirteen times in the letter, five in the proposition, six in the narrative, and two in the remaining four chapters. The story is where the proposition gets its proof.

What the species question settles, and what it does not

It settles how to ask questions of chapters 1–2. A judicial reading asks whether Paul's account matches the record; a deliberative reading asks what each item is there to prove. It does not settle Paul's truthfulness, which he puts under oath (1:20), and it does not make the letter any less of a letter: the greeting, the "I am astonished," the large letters and the grace are epistolary, and Longenecker is right that the form matters. These pages hold the three readings together: a letter in form, deliberative in aim, concentric in arrangement. The narrative-as-evidence point is developed, with Hall's second article, in the Delivery section of the Movement Commentary.

📜 The Torah Chain of Chapter 3

The agitators had the Torah on their side, or thought they did: circumcision is in Genesis 17, and the law is the law. Paul's answer is not to set the gospel against the Scriptures but to read the Scriptures in order. Chapter 3 is a chain of quotations, each one chosen for its position in the story, and much of the argument is the order.

Gal 3:6 Genesis 15:6
"Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness."

Righteousness by faith, stated of the patriarch himself, before circumcision (Genesis 17) and centuries before Sinai. The first link fixes the order of the story.

Gal 3:8 Genesis 12:3; 18:18
"In you all the nations will be blessed."

Scripture "preached the gospel beforehand" to Abraham: the nations are in the promise from the start. Paul calls this gospel, προευηγγελίσατο.

Gal 3:10 Deuteronomy 27:26
"Cursed is everyone who does not abide by all things written in the book of the law, to do them."

The law's own verdict on those under it. On Wright's reading in The Climax of the Covenant, the curse in view is Deuteronomy's curse of exile on a covenant people who have not kept the covenant, a curse Israel's history had confirmed.

Gal 3:11 Habakkuk 2:4
"The righteous one will live by faith."

The prophet's word for how the righteous live, set directly against the next link.

Gal 3:12 Leviticus 18:5
"The one who does them will live by them."

The law's own principle, "doing," set beside Habakkuk's "faith." Paul does not say the law is bad; he says the law does not rest on faith, so it cannot be the road to the righteousness of 3:6.

Gal 3:13 Deuteronomy 21:23
"Cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree."

The Messiah bore the law's curse, becoming a curse "for us," so that Abraham's blessing could reach the nations and the promise of the Spirit come through faith (3:14). The curse-link and the blessing-link meet at the cross.

Gal 3:16 Genesis 13:15; 17:8
"…and to your offspring" — singular.

The promise was spoken to one seed, who is the Messiah. Wright reads the singular as the one family gathered in him rather than a grammatical trick: the point of 3:16 is made good at 3:28–29, "you are all one … and if you are the Messiah's, then you are Abraham's seed."

Gal 3:17 Exodus 12:40
Four hundred and thirty years.

The interval between promise and law, taken from the Torah's own chronology as the Greek Bible gives it (Exodus 12:40 LXX counts the years in Canaan and Egypt together). A ratified covenant is not annulled by a law that came that much later: 3:15 argues it from a human will, 3:17 applies it to the covenant "ratified beforehand by God." That covenant is the promise to Abraham, and the scene most readers have in view is Genesis 15, where God alone passed between the pieces while Abraham slept, binding himself and asking nothing (the Abraham profile works through the ceremony). The argument is from sequence, and the sequence is Scripture's.

promise · faithlaw · cursechronology

Promise before law; the law's own curse; faith's own prophet; the curse borne; the seed; the dates. The order is the argument.

What Paul is and is not doing with these texts

He is not proof-texting against the Torah. Every link is a Torah or prophetic text, and the argument runs on their sequence: God dealt with Abraham by promise and faith before there was a law to keep, so the law, when it came, could not have changed the basis of the relationship (3:17–18). Richard Hays's work on Galatians 3:1–4:11 shows that the chain presupposes a story, the story of God's promise to Abraham fulfilled in the faithful Messiah, and reads each text as a moment in it; Wright's chapters on 3:10–14 and 3:15–20 in The Climax of the Covenant show how the curse and the singular seed belong to that same story. On either account, the agitators are not out-argued with a different Bible. They are out-read with their own.

2:16 · the quiet psalm underneath the thesis

"By works of the law no flesh will be justified" is Psalm 143:2 with "by works of the law" written in: the psalmist's "no one living is righteous before you" becomes Paul's verdict on the law as a means of justification. The thesis of the letter is itself a reading of Scripture.

5:14 · the whole law in one word

Leviticus 19:18, "love your neighbor as yourself," is for Paul the one word in which "the whole law is fulfilled." The same text does the same work in Jesus' teaching, and Paul's use of it in the ethical section means his argument never ends by discarding the Torah. It ends by saying what the Torah was always for.

👩‍👦 Hagar and Sarah: The Allegory That Turns Sinai Upside Down

4:21–31 is the boldest reading of Scripture in the letter and, on Wilson's ring, the mirror image of 2:15–21: the same two groups, the labels exchanged. Paul calls it allegory, ἀλληγορούμενα (4:24), and he expects his readers to know the story and the traditions that had grown around it. This section gives the shape; the passage has a study of its own, verse by verse.

Two mothers, two sons, two covenants, two Jerusalems. Everything the agitators would have put on the Sarah side, Paul puts on Hagar's.

The reversal

In Jewish reading of Genesis, the line of Sarah and Isaac is Israel, marked by circumcision; the line of Hagar and Ishmael is the nations. Paul keeps the genealogy and inverts the mapping. The covenant of Sinai, with its demand for circumcision, is placed with Hagar, so that those who press the law on gentile believers are, in the terms of the story, Ishmael's heirs, and the uncircumcised gentile believers are Sarah's children, born of promise. Wilson calls the move "a stunning reversal of expectations," and it is also, as Hays shows in Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul, a reading anchored in a prophet. Isaiah 54:1, the barren woman who will have more children than the married one, was addressed to Zion after exile; Paul hears in it the Jerusalem above whose children are the gentiles the agitators would exclude. The bridge is Isaiah 51:2, where the exiles are told to "look to Sarah who bore you": in Isaiah, Sarah is already the barren mother of Zion, so "the barren one" of 54:1 is Sarah's own figure and the Jerusalem above is her household. The site's Sarah profile follows that line from Genesis 21 through Isaiah 51 and 54 to this verse.

The traditions Paul is reading against

The allegory lands harder when the interpretive background is in view. Paul was not the first to read these two women as more than two women, and the reversal he performs on their readings is one instance of a method the site traces across his letters on the Second Temple mechanics page.

Philo · the two women as stages of education

In On Mating with the Preliminary Studies, Philo reads Hagar as the encyclical studies, the preparatory learning one must "mate with" first, and Sarah as virtue and wisdom, the goal. One passes through Hagar to reach Sarah. Paul's two women are not stages on one road but two roads.

Jubilees · circumcision as the covenant's eternal sign

Jubilees 15 retells Genesis 17 and makes circumcision an eternal ordinance; those who are uncircumcised do not belong to the children of the covenant. This is the kind of conviction the agitators carried into Galatia, and it is exactly what the allegory refuses: in Paul's reading, the uncircumcised children of promise are Sarah's.

Ishmael "playing" · from Genesis 21:9 to Galatians 4:29

Genesis says only that Sarah saw Ishmael מְצַחֵק, "playing" or "laughing," and the Greek Bible adds "with Isaac her son." The participle is built on the root of Isaac's own name: Ishmael was, so to speak, Isaac-ing, playing the heir. Jubilees 17:4 still has him "playing and dancing" while Sarah grows jealous; the harsher reading, mockery, idolatry or an arrow aimed in play, belongs to the rabbinic traditions Longenecker gathers (Genesis Rabbah 53:11 among them). Paul's "persecuted … then as now" stands in that line and assumes his readers knew it. The Sarah profile sets out the whole laughter wordplay.

"Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia" · 4:25

The sentence is the allegory's hinge and its least stable line: some manuscripts read "now Sinai is a mountain in Arabia," without Hagar's name. If the name stands, Paul may be hearing an Arabic word for rock in it, or simply following the geography: Sinai lies outside the land, in the wilderness to which Hagar was sent and where Ishmael settled (Genesis 21:21), so the covenant given there belongs, on the map of the story, to the slave woman's side. Variant readings Why Hagar "is" Sinai: disputed

Sarah's words as Scripture · 4:30

"But what does Scripture say? Cast out the slave woman and her son." The line is Sarah's demand (Genesis 21:10), spoken to keep the inheritance for Isaac (Genesis gives no motive; Jubilees 17:4 supplies jealousy); Paul can call it Scripture because God ratified it in the next breath, telling Abraham to "listen to her voice" (21:12). The Sarah profile and the Hagar profile hold the two sides of the scene together: the demand Paul quotes, and the God who saw and heard the woman it expelled.

Allegory, not erasure

Paul's ἀλληγορούμενα does not deny that Hagar and Sarah existed or that Genesis means what it says. It reads the story as a pattern with a present referent, the way the prophets read the exodus, and the sharpness is in the mapping, Sinai on the slave side. That mapping is what the argument needs: it answers the circumcision party on the ground of Genesis, which they would have claimed as their own, and it is why the ring places this unit opposite 2:15–21. There the two groups are "Jews by nature" and "gentile sinners," and neither is justified by works of law; here the two groups are children of the slave and children of the free, and the gentile believers are on the free side. The categories are kept; the occupants are exchanged. Susan Eastman's study of the surrounding chapter adds the frame: the allegory sits between Paul's labor pains (4:19) and "for freedom" (5:1), a mother's argument about which mother the Galatians belong to.

🌳 Two Humanities: Works of the Flesh, Fruit of the Spirit

The ethical section (5:13–6:10) is built on one contrast stated three ways, and the grammar of the contrast carries the theology. The flesh has works, plural and produced; the Spirit has fruit, singular and grown.

The works of the flesh · 5:19–21
τὰ ἔργα τῆς σαρκός"are obvious"
sexual immorality, impurity, debauchery
idolatry, sorcery
enmities, strife, jealousy, outbursts of anger, rivalries, dissensions, factions, envy
drunkenness, carousing, "and things like these"

The fruit of the Spirit · 5:22–23
ὁ καρπὸς τοῦ πνεύματοςsingular
love, joy, peace

faithfulness, gentleness, self-control

Fifteen works, open-ended ("and things like these"); nine fruit, one harvest. Eight of the fifteen works are sins against community, which is where a church biting and devouring itself (5:15) would recognize itself.

Why the list form matters. The agitators had a reasonable question, which John Barclay's Obeying the Truth reconstructs from the letter: without the law, how will these gentile converts know how to live? Paul's answer is not a shorter law. It is a different kind of thing, fruit, which the law can describe but cannot grow, produced by the Spirit in people who "keep in step" with him (5:25). The vice list shows what the flesh produces on its own; the fruit list shows what the Spirit produces in them; and the sentence between them, "against such things there is no law," is the letter's wry last word on the Torah question. The nine fruit are read one by one on the Theology page.

🔤 Wordplay and the Words That Carry the Letter

Paul's Galatian Greek is sharp, and several of its sharpest moments turn on a single word.

5:12 · the unkindest cut
ἀποκόψονται

"I wish those who unsettle you would cut themselves off." The verb means to amputate, and in a region that knew the self-castrated devotees of the mother goddess the pun was hard to miss, and many commentators hear it: let the circumcisers go the whole way.

4:3, 9 · 5:25 · 6:16 · one root, two directions
στοιχεῖα · στοιχῶμεν · στοιχήσουσιν

The "elements" the Galatians were enslaved to (στοιχεῖα) share a root with the verb Paul uses for walking in line: "let us keep in step with the Spirit" (5:25) and "all who walk by this rule" (6:16). The root means a row or rank. Paul's question is which line the Galatians are marching in.

5:15 · a church eating itself
δάκνετε καὶ κατεσθίετε

"If you bite and devour one another, watch out that you are not consumed by one another." Three verbs of eating, for a community whose dispute began over who could eat with whom (2:12). The hook into the ethical section from "persecuted" (5:11) turns pressure from outside into consumption inside.

6:11 · the autograph
πηλίκοις γράμμασιν

"See with what large letters I write to you with my own hand." Letters were dictated to a secretary and authenticated by a closing in the sender's hand; E. Randolph Richards's study of Paul's secretaries sets the practice out. Paul takes the pen for the whole epilogue, and the oversized script is signature, emphasis and sarcasm in one gesture: the only marks that matter are the marks of Jesus he bears (6:17).

3:1 · the public notice
προεγράφη ἐσταυρωμένος

Jesus was "placarded" as crucified before their eyes. The verb is used for posting a public notice. Paul's preaching, and on Wilson's reading his conduct, had put the crucified Messiah up in Galatia like a proclamation on a wall.

3:24 · the slave at the school door
παιδαγωγός

Not the teacher but the household slave who walked the child to school and kept him in line until he came of age: custody with a built-in end date. "Schoolmaster" misleads; "guardian" is closer. The law's term of service, in one word.

"For freedom the Messiah set us free. Stand firm, then, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery." Galatians 5:1

ἐλευθερία and its family occur eleven times in Galatians, more than in any other letter of Paul's; the word enters at 2:4, the false brothers spying out "our freedom," and climaxes here, at the hinge from argument to life.

🧊 The Whole Letter in One Picture

Put the craft and the reading together and the letter resolves into a single move. The surface problem was a demand for circumcision. The deeper problem was a definition of belonging, and Paul reaches the depths to fix the surface.

Surface problem — what the Galatians were facing
A demand for circumcision Paul's authority questioned Calendar observance Tables dividing A church biting and devouring
the waterline
The underwater problem Belonging measured by Torah markers, circumcision, food and calendar, instead of by union with the Messiah: the gospel plus something, which is a different gospel.
Paul's solution Read the Torah in order: promise before law, the law's own curse borne at the cross, one seed and one family by faith. Then hand the family a Spirit, not a rulebook.
The result Crucified with the MessiahOne familyFreedomFruitNew creation

This is why the ring climbs to a verse about faith and the letter ends with a sentence about creation. Paul is not handing the Galatians a stricter identity; he is reminding them which story they already belong to, and it is the direction the Resurrection as Revelation project keeps pressing: what is true of the risen Messiah becomes true of the people who are his.

Belonging defined by the Torah's markers produces two tables and a church consuming itself; belonging defined by the crucified Messiah produces one family, free, bearing fruit.
