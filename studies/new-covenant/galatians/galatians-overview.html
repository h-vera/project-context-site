Paul's Letter to the Galatians

A short, hot letter to churches that had been told trusting Jesus was not enough. Paul's answer is a gospel that sets people right with God through the crucified Messiah, makes one family out of every nation, and hands that family a Spirit instead of a rulebook.

✦ How to Read Galatians

Paul writes from a place of deep frustration and deeper love, and the two never separate. He moves from the greeting straight to astonishment that the Galatians are turning to a different gospel (1:6). He calls them foolish (3:1) and his little children, for whom he is in labor again (4:19). Hear both voices as you read.

The Big Idea

Because people are set right with God through the faith of Jesus and not by works of the Torah, the family of God is one and multi-ethnic, and it is the Spirit, not the law, who makes it new.

Or, in the letter's own last word on the matter: neither circumcision nor uncircumcision is anything, but new creation (6:15).

The Central Question

Not "circumcision, yes or no?" but something underneath it.

❓
Surface question

Must non-Jewish believers be circumcised and keep the Torah to belong to God's people?

🔑
Real question

What makes a person right with God and a member of his family, and what makes that family holy?

What matters is new creation.
Author Paul, with "all the brothers with me" (1:2); the closing is in his own hand (6:11)
Audience "The churches of Galatia" (1:2), several congregations with non-Jewish believers at the center of the dispute
Date Disputed: c. AD 48–49 or the early to mid 50s; see the historical setting
Occasion Teachers demanding circumcision and Torah observance of non-Jewish believers; the churches being drawn in (4:10; 5:2–4)
Length 6 chapters, 149 verses; the shortest of Paul's major doctrinal letters
A translation to keep open: “faith in” or “faithfulness of” Jesus?

In 2:16, πίστις Χριστοῦ can be read as our faith in Jesus or Jesus' own faithfulness. The same question arises in 2:20 and 3:22. These pages keep both readings in view: neither makes Torah observance the basis of God's justifying verdict. Follow the wording in Movement 4 and the fuller account of justification.

🏛️ What Was Happening in Galatia

Three pieces of the backstory explain the urgency. The Context page examines the evidence and the questions that remain open.

The demand

Teachers were pressing circumcision on non-Jewish believers. Paul treats this as a change to the gospel itself (1:6–9; 5:2–4), and says the teachers want to avoid persecution for the cross and boast in the Galatians' flesh (6:12–13).

Who were the agitators? →

Paul's commission

Paul says his gospel came through revelation of Jesus Christ, and his call was to proclaim the Son among the nations (1:11–17). His later meeting with the Jerusalem leaders confirmed fellowship without requiring Titus to be circumcised (2:1–10).

Paul's account and Acts →

The table at Antioch

Peter withdrew from eating with non-Jewish believers, and others followed. Paul saw a public contradiction of the gospel (2:11–14). The dispute concerned who could share a table as full members of one family.

Why the meal mattered →

Which Galatia, and when was the letter written?

Destination and date: disputed

South Galatia: churches in the Roman province, including the cities of Acts 13–14. This allows an early date, c. AD 48–49, though it does not require it. North Galatia: churches in the ethnic region farther north, usually associated with a date in the early to mid 50s.

The identification of the Jerusalem visit in Galatians 2 also matters. Read the destination evidence and the visit comparison before settling the chronology.

🏗️ Three Parts, One Argument

Read the letter once from beginning to end, then follow its argument through these three parts. Each answers a question raised by the one before it.

① Chapters 1–2

The Gospel of the Crucified Messiah

Paul recounts his call, Jerusalem's recognition, and the confrontation at Antioch to establish the gospel he preached. The broken table becomes the question of right standing before God: people are justified through faith, not works of the law (2:16). The answer is also personal. Paul has been crucified with the Messiah, who now lives in him (2:19–20).

Key text · 2:19–20The Messiah lives in me.
② Chapters 3–4

Creates a New, Multi-Ethnic Family

The Galatians' reception of the Spirit leads Paul back to Abraham's promise. The law came later: it guarded until the Messiah, but could not give life (3:21–25). In Christ, believers are Abraham's offspring, adopted children, and heirs (3:29–4:7). Paul longs for Christ to be formed in them (4:19), then reads Hagar and Sarah as an argument about slavery, promise, and freedom.

Key text · 3:28You are all one in the Messiah Jesus.
③ Chapters 5–6

That Is Transformed by the Spirit

Freedom takes the form of serving one another in love (5:13–14). The Spirit's fruit becomes visible in restored relationships, burdens carried together, and good done without giving up (5:22–6:10). Life by the Spirit also looks ahead to the harvest of eternal life (6:8–9). Paul closes by putting the cross and new creation at the center of the family's identity (6:14–15).

Key text · 6:15What counts is new creation.

The three-part reading follows BibleProject. For the fruit's nine qualities and their biblical connections, see The Fruit of the Spirit.

A second map: Wilson's proposed seven-ring reading

Proposed structural reading

Victor Wilson pairs passages across the letter around 3:24: seven rings when the opening and closing frame are counted. His proposal draws attention to repeated words, corresponding appeals, and the placement of the argument about law and faith. It is an interpretation of the letter's design, rather than a structure Paul explicitly announces.

The Literary Design page compares the proposed structures; the Movement Commentary reads the pairs in detail. Use the chapter-order map above to keep the forward argument in view.

💡 The Letter's Governing Themes

Six convictions run under all three parts; they are why a letter about a first-century dispute over circumcision is still the New Covenant's clearest account of what it means to belong. Each is developed on the Theology page; here is the orientation.

Gal 2:16

Declared Righteous by Faith

"To be justified," δικαιοῦσθαι, is a term from Israel's Scriptures for God's verdict that someone stands in right relationship with him. Paul's conviction is that no one receives that verdict by observing the Torah's commands, only through the faith of Jesus. Read three ways →

Gal 2:16, 20; 3:22

The Faith of Jesus

πίστις Χριστοῦ: Jesus' own faithfulness in living and dying for us, or our trust and devotion to him. Either way, the ground of a person's standing is what God did through Jesus, which they could never have done for themselves.

Gal 2:19–20

Crucified with the Messiah

When people trust the Messiah, what is true of him becomes true of them: his death and resurrection become theirs, and the life they now live is lived by faith in the Son of God who loved them and gave himself for them. Union →

Gal 3:6–9, 26–29

One Family of Abraham

Abraham was declared righteous by trusting God's promise that all nations would be blessed through him and his offspring. God's purpose was always one multi-ethnic family relating to him by faith; those who are the Messiah's are Abraham's offspring and heirs. One Family →

Gal 3:19–25

The Law: Good, and Temporary

Given at Sinai long after the promise, the law exposed Israel's share in the human condition and imprisoned everyone under sin, and it kept Israel in line, a guardian until the offspring came. Once the Messiah came, the guardian's term was over. The law →

Gal 5:16–25; 6:2

The Spirit Makes New Humans

The law could summarize God's will as love of neighbor but could not supply the power to do it. Jesus fulfilled it and now lives in his people by the Spirit, producing the fruit that fulfills "the law of the Messiah." The fruit →

🪶 How the Letter Is Built

Four features of the letter's craft carry most of its force. Each is developed on the Literary Design page, and the ring is walked pair by pair on the Movement Commentary; here is the orientation.

① A curse where the thanksgiving should be

Every other letter Paul wrote to a church opens with thanks or with a blessing of God. Galatians opens with θαυμάζω, "I am astonished" (1:6), and then, twice, ἀνάθεμα ἔστω, "let him be accursed" (1:8–9), applied even to an angel from heaven and to Paul himself. The missing thanksgiving is the letter's first argument.

② A story told as evidence, not as defense

Chapters 1–2 are the longest stretch of autobiography in Paul's letters. In the classical categories Galatians is a deliberative letter, urging a decision, and its narrative works the way narrative does in deliberative speech: every item (no consultation with flesh and blood, only fifteen days with Cephas, Titus not compelled, "privately") is chosen to prove that Paul's gospel came from God.

Betz read the letter as judicial, a defense; Kennedy and Hall as deliberative; Wilson follows Hall. See the Movement Commentary's Delivery section.

③ Scripture argued on the Torah's own ground

Paul answers the Torah party with the Torah: Genesis 15:6 and 12:3 on Abraham, Deuteronomy 27:26 on the curse, Habakkuk 2:4 and Leviticus 18:5 set against each other, Deuteronomy 21:23 on the hanged man, Genesis 16–21 read as allegory, Leviticus 19:18 as the whole law in one word. He does not sidestep the law; he reads it until it points past itself. The Torah chain →

④ Rings, and the words that stitch them

On Wilson's reading the proof mirrors itself around 3:24, and Paul hooks each unit to the next with a repeated word: "accursed" to "persecuting," "privately" to "slipped in to spy," "works of the law" to "works of the law," "the free woman" to "freedom." The hooks are how a hearer kept their place; they are listed in full on the Movement Commentary.

The large letters

"See with what large letters I am writing to you with my own hand" (6:11). Letters were dictated to a secretary and authenticated by a closing in the sender's own hand (compare 1 Corinthians 16:21; 2 Thessalonians 3:17). Paul takes the pen for the whole epilogue, and the oversized script is his signature, his emphasis, and his last word on the agitators in one gesture: the marks that matter are not the one they want on the Galatians' bodies but the marks of Jesus he bears on his own (6:17).

📅 A Four-Sitting Reading Plan

Begin with the whole letter, then return to its three parts with the Scriptures and companion accounts beside you.

Sitting 1 · The whole letter, aloud

Read all six chapters in one pass. Mark every "gospel" and every "faith." Notice the changes in tone and the questions Paul keeps pressing.

Sitting 2 · Chapters 1–2 with Acts 9, 11, 15

Read Paul's story next to Luke's. Notice what each selects, and ask of each item in Galatians 1–2 not "does it match Acts?" but "what is it here to prove?"

Sitting 3 · Chapters 3–4 with Genesis 12, 15, 17 and 21

Read the Abraham chapters Paul reads, then Deuteronomy 27:26 and 21:23 and Habakkuk 2:4. The argument of Galatians 3 is a reading of these texts, and it is more careful than it first appears.

Sitting 4 · Chapters 5–6 with Leviticus 19:18, Psalm 1 and John 15

Begin with the neighbor-love command Paul quotes. Then read Psalm 1 and John 15 alongside his fruit imagery as a comparison across Scripture, without assuming he is directly quoting either passage.

💬 Questions for Study and Discussion

Open a question for a pointer to where on the Galatians pages the material for it sits.

On the gospel and belonging

The agitators did not deny Jesus; they added a requirement. Why does Paul treat an addition as a different gospel rather than a stricter version of the same one?
Where to look: the opening passage above, and the Theology page's Seven Drumbeats of Grace, which follows the logic of 2:21 through the letter.
What are the modern equivalents of circumcision, the markers a community quietly requires before it counts someone as fully in?
Where to look: on the Context page, the covenant markers and what conversion cost a God-fearer, for the first-century weight of the marker before you translate it.
Paul confronts Peter, the senior apostle, in public. What made that necessary rather than merely brave, and what would "walking straight toward the truth of the gospel" look like in a church today?
Where to look: the chapters 1–2 map above, and The Table at Antioch on the Context page.
Before Paul rebukes the Galatians, he names the Father who raised Jesus (1:1) and the Messiah who gave himself for our sins to rescue us from the present evil age (1:4). How do these opening claims shape the argument that follows?
Where to look: the Theology page's What Matters Is New Creation and Resurrection below the waterline.
Which details of Paul's call does Galatians 1:11–17 itself give, and which details do Acts 9, 22 and 26 supply? How do 1 Corinthians 9:1 and 15:8 add Paul's own witness to seeing the risen Jesus?
Where to look: The call, in two tellings on the Context page, and What was unveiled on the road on the Theology page.

On the law and the Spirit

Paul says the law is good and also that it imprisoned everyone under sin. How can both be true, and why does the timing (promise first, law 430 years later) matter to his argument?
Where to look: The Law: Holy, Temporary, and Not Against the Promises on the Theology page, and the Literary page's Torah chain, which lays the citations of chapter 3 in order.
The opponents' question was fair: without the law, how will people learn God's will? How does Paul's answer, the Spirit, avoid becoming "do whatever you feel"?
Where to look: the chapters 5–6 map above, and Freedom: From What, For What on the Theology page.
Fruit "requires cultivation." What does keeping in step with the Spirit look like as a practice rather than a feeling?
Where to look: The Fruit of the Spirit on the Theology page, especially its examples of each quality in the letter.
"Neither circumcision nor uncircumcision, but new creation." What would it mean for a church to be known for what it is rather than for what it is not?
Where to look: the closing passage above, and the Theology page's closing synthesis.
What do believers already have in 4:4–7, and what do they still await in 5:5, 5:21 and 6:8–9? How do present belonging and future hope shape a life led by the Spirit?
Where to look: on the Theology page, The Spirit's tense is future as well as present and the age track under the thesis.
