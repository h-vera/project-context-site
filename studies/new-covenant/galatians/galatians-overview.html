✦ How to Read Galatians

Galatians is the only letter Paul wrote to a church that opens with neither thanksgiving nor blessing. Every other letter to a congregation opens with gratitude or with a benediction of God (2 Corinthians and Ephesians bless rather than thank); this one opens with astonishment, θαυμάζω, that they are so quickly deserting the one who called them for a different gospel (1:6). Paul wrote it from a place of deep frustration and deeper love, and the two never separate. He calls them foolish (3:1) and he calls them his little children, for whom he is in labor again (4:19), and both are true at once.

The occasion is clear from the letter itself. Teachers had arrived in the Galatian churches after Paul left, undermining him and insisting that the non-Jewish believers be circumcised and take up the practices of the Torah in order to belong to the people of God. The Galatians were being drawn in: they had begun to keep the calendar (4:10), and circumcision was next (5:2–4). Paul's response moves in three large steps: he restates and defends the gospel of the crucified Messiah (chapters 1–2); he argues that this gospel is what creates the new, multi-ethnic family promised to Abraham (3–4); and he shows that this family is made holy not by the law but by the Spirit (5–6).

The Big Idea

Because people are set right with God through the faith of Jesus and not by works of the Torah, the family of God is one and multi-ethnic, and it is the Spirit, not the law, who makes it new.

Or, in the letter's own last word on the matter: neither circumcision nor uncircumcision is anything, but new creation (6:15).

The Central Question

Not "circumcision, yes or no?" but something underneath it.

❓
Surface question

Must non-Jewish believers be circumcised and keep the Torah to belong to God's people?

🔑
Real question

What makes a person right with God and a member of his family, and what makes that family holy?

What matters is new creation.

The one phrase that governs the whole letter

In 2:16 Paul says a person is not justified by works of the law but διὰ πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, "through faith of Jesus Christ." The genitive can be read two ways, and the letter has been read both ways for a very long time. It may mean our faith in Jesus, our trust in and allegiance to him. It may mean the faithfulness of Jesus, his own faithful living and dying on our behalf. The same ambiguity returns at 2:20, "I live by faith of the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me," and at 3:22.

These pages do not force the choice, because the letter's point survives it either way: people are set right with God on the basis of what God did through Jesus, not on the basis of what they do for themselves. The BibleProject commentary puts it plainly, and the Movement Commentary notes where each reading changes the shading of a sentence.

🏛️ What Was Happening in Galatia

The letter assumes a backstory its first readers knew and we have to reconstruct. The outline is this; the Commentary & Context page builds it in full, from the Acts 13–14 mission to the table at Antioch. The Jesus movement began in Jerusalem as a Jewish messianic movement, but its message was for all humanity, and it crossed the ethnic line fast. By the time Paul was writing, non-Jews were joining in numbers, and that raised a question the movement had not had to answer before.

① Who belongs, and how?

For centuries the covenant people of God had been one people, Israel, joined by birth or by proselyte conversion and set apart by practices the Torah commanded: circumcision of males, the food laws, the Sabbath and the calendar. Many Jewish believers held, in good faith, that non-Jews who wanted to be fully part of God's family needed to take these on. The dispute surfaces in Acts 15, where Luke tells how the church settled it; the Context page reads Luke's account on its own terms and then sets it beside Paul's.

② The visitors

Some of these believers came to the Galatian churches after Paul had moved on. They questioned his standing as an apostle, implied his gospel was secondhand and incomplete, and pressed the non-Jewish men to be circumcised (1:7; 5:10, 12; 6:12–13). Paul never names them. The Galatians were being drawn in: calendar observance had begun (4:10), and Paul writes as though circumcision was imminent but not yet accepted (5:2–4).

③ Paul's commission

Paul's defense of his authority is a retelling of his call: commissioned by the risen Jesus himself for the non-Jewish world (Acts 9; Gal 1:11–17), consulting Jerusalem only years later, and finding the acknowledged leaders in full support of a gospel that did not require circumcision (2:1–10).

④ The scene at Antioch

The tension ran deeper than Galatia. Peter had been eating freely with non-Jewish believers in Antioch until a group from Jerusalem arrived; then he drew back, and others followed him. Paul confronted him publicly for not walking straight toward the truth of the gospel (2:11–14). The Galatians were being asked to make Peter's mistake.

⑤ The pressure behind the pressure

Paul's own diagnosis of the agitators is that they want the Galatians circumcised so as to escape persecution for the cross (6:12) and to boast in the Galatians' flesh (6:13). Circumcision made a non-Jewish believer legible to the synagogue and the city; it bought safety.

⑥ Which Galatia?

"Galatia" named both an ethnic region in the north of Asia Minor and a Roman province that reached south to the cities of Acts 13–14. Where the churches were bears on when the letter was written: a northern destination implies a later date, a southern one allows either. The question is open (below).

Why the stakes were so high. The agitators were not denying Jesus. They were adding to him. That is exactly why Paul treats their teaching as a different gospel rather than a lesser one: if righteousness could come through the law, "then Christ died for nothing" (2:21). An addition to the gospel that makes the cross unnecessary is not an addition.

🏗️ Three Parts, One Argument

Read straight through, Galatians falls into three parts, and each part has a key sentence that the next part picks up. This is the map the BibleProject poster draws, and it is the right first map, because it follows the order in which the Galatians heard the letter.

① Chapters 1–2 The Gospel of the Crucified Messiah

Move: Paul defends his message and his authority, retells his call and his dealings with Jerusalem and Antioch, and states the gospel: a person is set right with God by the faith of Jesus, not by works of the law.

Key text · 2:19–20 "I have been crucified with the Messiah; it is no longer I who live, but the Messiah lives in me."
② Chapters 3–4 Creates a New, Multi-Ethnic Family

Move: Abraham was declared righteous by faith, and God's purpose was always one family from all nations. The law, given later, was good and temporary; the Messiah, Abraham's offspring, fulfilled it and took its curse, so the blessing reaches everyone.

Key text · 3:28 "There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male and female; you are all one in the Messiah Jesus."
③ Chapters 5–6 That Is Transformed by the Spirit

Move: If not the law, then what shapes this family's life? The Spirit of Jesus, who produces in them the fruit the law could describe but not grow, so that they fulfill the Torah of the Messiah by bearing one another's burdens.

Key text · 6:15 "Neither circumcision nor uncircumcision is anything, but new creation."

Three parts · each with a key sentence the next part takes up

There is a second map, and it does not compete with the first. Victor Wilson, reading the letter through classical rhetoric, found that the letter is arranged concentrically: the salutation and the epilogue stand as two end pieces, and inside them the proof (1:10–6:10) runs in six pairs of units that mirror one another around a single verse, 3:24, so that the letter climbs from Paul's own story to justification by faith and comes back down, step for step, to Galatia. Seven rings in all, counting the frame. The three-part map is how Galatians reads; the ring is, on Wilson's reading, how it was built to be remembered.

💡 The Letter's Governing Themes

Six convictions run under all three parts. They are why a letter about a first-century dispute over circumcision is still the New Covenant's clearest account of what it means to belong. The Theology page gathers them into a synthesis.

Gal 2:16

Declared Righteous by Faith

"To be justified," δικαιοῦσθαι, is a term from Israel's Scriptures for God's verdict that someone stands in right relationship with him: forgiven, given a place in the family, and being transformed by grace. Paul's conviction is that no one receives that verdict by observing the Torah's commands, only through the faith of Jesus.

Gal 2:16, 20; 3:22

The Faith of Jesus

πίστις Χριστοῦ: Jesus' own faithfulness in living and dying for us, or our trust and devotion to him. Either way, the ground of a person's standing is what God did through Jesus, which they could never have done for themselves.

Gal 2:19–20

Crucified with the Messiah

The heart of Paul's gospel: when people trust the Messiah, what is true of him becomes true of them. His death and resurrection become theirs, and the life they now live is lived by faith in the Son of God who loved them and gave himself for them.

Gal 3:6–9, 26–29

One Family of Abraham

Abraham was declared righteous by trusting God's promise that all nations would be blessed through him and his offspring. God's purpose was always one large, multi-ethnic family relating to him by faith; those who are the Messiah's are Abraham's offspring and heirs.

Gal 3:19–25

The Law: Good, and Temporary

Given at Sinai long after the promise, the law had a negative role, exposing Israel's share in the human condition and imprisoning everyone under sin, and a positive one, a guardian keeping Israel in line until the offspring came. Once the Messiah came, the guardian's term was over.

Gal 5:16–25; 6:2

The Spirit Makes New Humans

The law could summarize God's will as love of neighbor but could not supply the power to do it. Jesus fulfilled it and now lives in his people by the Spirit, producing the fruit that fulfills "the Torah of the Messiah."

An addition to the gospel that makes the cross unnecessary is not an addition.

🪶 How the Letter Is Built

Four features of the letter's craft carry most of its force. Each is developed on the Literary Design page, and the ring itself is walked pair by pair on the Movement Commentary page; here is the orientation.

① A curse where the thanksgiving should be

Every other letter Paul wrote to a church opens with thanks or with a blessing of God. Galatians opens with θαυμάζω, "I am astonished" (1:6), and then, twice, ἀνάθεμα ἔστω, "let him be accursed" (1:8–9), applied even to an angel from heaven and to Paul himself. The missing thanksgiving is the letter's first argument: there is nothing here Paul can be polite about.

② A story told as evidence, not as defense

Chapters 1–2 are the longest stretch of autobiography in Paul's letters. In the classical categories, Galatians is a deliberative letter, urging a decision, and its narrative works the way narrative does in deliberative speech: every item (no consultation with flesh and blood, only fifteen days with Cephas, Titus not compelled, "privately") is chosen to prove that Paul's gospel came from God.

Betz read the letter as judicial, a defense; Kennedy and Hall as deliberative; Wilson follows Hall. See the Movement Commentary's Delivery section.

③ Scripture argued on the Torah's own ground

Paul answers the Torah party with the Torah: Genesis 15:6 and 12:3 on Abraham, Deuteronomy 27:26 on the curse, Habakkuk 2:4 and Leviticus 18:5 set against each other, Deuteronomy 21:23 on the hanged man, Genesis 16–21 read as allegory, Leviticus 19:18 as the whole law in one word. He does not sidestep the law; he reads it until it points past itself.

④ Rings, and the words that stitch them

The proof mirrors itself around 3:24, and Paul hooks each unit to the next with a repeated word: "accursed" to "persecuting," "privately" to "slipped in to spy," "works of the law" to "works of the law," "the free woman" to "freedom." The hooks are how a hearer kept their place, and they are listed in full on the Movement Commentary.

The large letters

"See with what large letters I am writing to you with my own hand" (6:11). Letters were dictated to a secretary and authenticated by a closing in the sender's own hand (compare 1 Cor 16:21; 2 Thess 3:17). Paul takes the pen for the whole epilogue, and the oversized script is his signature, his emphasis, and his last word on the agitators in one gesture: the marks that matter are not the one they want on the Galatians' bodies but the marks of Jesus he bears on his own (6:17).

🚶 The Letter in Three Passes

A compressed walk through each part. The full treatment, pair by pair, is on the Movement Commentary page.

① Chapters 1–2 · The gospel of the crucified Messiah

Paul opens by expressing bewilderment that the Galatians have embraced a different gospel (1:6–9), the one promoted by people who badmouth him and demand circumcision. So he first defends the authenticity of his message and his authority as an apostle. He was commissioned by the risen Jesus himself for the non-Jewish world (1:11–17; the story is in Acts 9); only later did he go to Jerusalem to consult Peter and James, and when he told the leaders there that he was not requiring non-Jewish believers to be circumcised or to keep the food laws, they gave him the right hand of fellowship (1:18–2:10).

Then the harder story. Peter had come to Antioch and was eating with the non-Jewish believers, until people from the Jerusalem opposition arrived; then he withdrew, and Paul confronted him to his face for hypocrisy, for not staying true to the gospel (2:11–14). The confrontation becomes the argument. People are not justified by works of the Torah but by the faith of Jesus the Messiah (2:16), and the reason anyone can say they are right with God is not what they do for themselves but what Jesus did for them, which they could never have done.

✦ Key text 2:19–20 — "I have been crucified with the Messiah; it is no longer I who live, but the Messiah lives in me. And the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith of the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me."

② Chapters 3–4 · Creates a new, multi-ethnic family

This understanding of what Jesus accomplished has huge implications for who belongs. Paul turns to Abraham, who was declared righteous simply by trusting God's promise that all nations would find blessing through him and his offspring (3:6–9; Genesis 15:6; 12:3). God's purpose was always one large family relating to him by faith. But then why the Torah? Paul's answer is brief and dense, and he will fill it out later in Romans. The law was given at Sinai long after the promise (3:17), and the Torah itself shows it was meant to be temporary. Negatively, it worked like a magnifying glass on Israel's sin and ended up pronouncing everyone guilty: "the Scripture imprisoned everything under sin" (3:22). Positively, it was a guardian, a παιδαγωγός, keeping Israel in line until the promised offspring came (3:24).

And he came. Jesus, the faithful Israelite who truly loved God and neighbor, took the curse and consequence of Israel's failure into himself as Israel's king (3:13), so that through him, Abraham's offspring, the blessing reaches all people regardless of ethnicity, status or gender (3:14, 28). Chapter 4 says the same with two more pictures: the heir who comes of age and receives adoption and the Spirit who cries "Abba" (4:1–7), and the two mothers, Hagar and Sarah, read as two covenants (4:21–31). Requiring Torah observance of non-Jewish believers, Paul concludes, acts as if Jesus had not fulfilled the promise or dealt with sin, neglects the freedom he won, and limits God's blessing to one ethnic family.

✦ Key text 3:28 — "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female; for you are all one in the Messiah Jesus."

③ Chapters 5–6 · Transformed by the Spirit

The opponents had a reasonable objection: the Torah is a proven guide to God's will, so how will non-Jewish believers learn it without the laws? Paul's answer is the Spirit. The laws are good and wise, and they can all be summarized, as Jesus summarized them, in the command to love your neighbor as yourself (5:14; Leviticus 19:18). But good as they are, they never gave Israel the power to obey them. The good news is that Jesus fulfilled them on our behalf and now lives in his people through the Spirit, making them into new humans who fulfill the law by loving others.

So Paul sets the two humanities side by side. The works of the flesh are obvious, behaviors that dehumanize people and wreck relationships and communities (5:19–21); the Torah prohibited them, and Jesus put them to death on the cross. The Spirit produces something else, ὁ καρπὸς τοῦ πνεύματος, the fruit of the Spirit, which is Jesus' own way of life reproduced in his family (5:22–23).

Nine fruit, one harvest: the character of God formed in people who walk by the Spirit (after the BibleProject Scholarship Team)

The fruit is not automatic. It requires cultivation, like real fruit: "if we live by the Spirit, let us also keep in step with the Spirit" (5:25), pruning old habits and growing new ones, and finding ourselves carried along as Jesus reshapes heart and mind. The practice is concrete: restore the one who stumbles, gently (6:1); carry one another's burdens, and so fulfill τὸν νόμον τοῦ Χριστοῦ, the Torah of the Messiah (6:2); sow to the Spirit and do not tire of doing good (6:7–10). The demand for circumcision, Paul concludes in his own hand, is, in the BibleProject's phrase, "an adventure in missing the point."

✦ Key text 6:15 — "For neither circumcision is anything, nor uncircumcision, but new creation."

🔑 Key Passages

Eight texts carry the letter. Read these slowly and the rest opens up.

1:6–9
No other gospel

The proposition the whole letter proves. A "different gospel" is not a variant; it is "no gospel at all," and the anathema falls on any preacher of it, angel or apostle.

2:16
Not by works of the law

Three times in one verse: justified not by works of law but by the faith of Jesus the Messiah. The thesis sentence of Paul's gospel, with Psalm 143:2 underneath it.

2:19–20
Crucified with the Messiah

What is true of him becomes true of them. The most personal sentence in the letter and the heart of its theology.

3:8
The gospel preached beforehand to Abraham

"In you all the nations will be blessed." Paul finds the multi-ethnic family in Genesis, before Sinai, and calls it gospel.

3:24
The guardian until the Messiah

The law as παιδαγωγός, custody with an end date, "so that we might be justified by faith." On Wilson's reading, the apex of the whole ring.

3:28
All one in the Messiah

Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female: the boundaries the agitators' gospel would keep in force, dissolved for belonging in one sentence.

5:1, 6
For freedom, and faith working through love

Christ set us free for freedom, and what counts is neither circumcision nor its absence but faith working through love: the hinge from argument to life.

5:22–23; 6:2
The fruit of the Spirit and the Torah of the Messiah

What the Spirit grows, and what the family does with it: bear one another's burdens.

📅 A Four-Sitting Reading Plan

Galatians can be read aloud in about twenty minutes, and it should be, at least once, before it is dissected; it was written to be heard in one sitting. Then work the parts.

Sitting 1 · The whole letter, aloud

Read all six chapters in one pass. Mark every "gospel" and every "faith." You will feel the letter climb and come back down without any outline at all.

Sitting 2 · Chapters 1–2 with Acts 9, 11, 15

Read Paul's story next to Luke's. Notice what each selects, and ask of each item in Galatians 1–2 not "does it match Acts?" but "what is it here to prove?"

Sitting 3 · Chapters 3–4 with Genesis 12, 15, 17 and 21

Read the Abraham chapters Paul reads, then Deuteronomy 27:26 and 21:23 and Habakkuk 2:4. The argument of Galatians 3 is a reading of these texts, and it is more careful than it first appears.

Sitting 4 · Chapters 5–6 with Leviticus 19:18, Psalm 1 and John 15

The command Paul says sums up the law, the tree planted by streams of water, and the vine and the branches. The fruit image in 5:22 has roots in all three.

💬 Questions for Study and Discussion

Open a question for a pointer to where on the Galatians pages the material for it sits.

On the gospel and belonging

The agitators did not deny Jesus; they added a requirement. Why does Paul treat an addition as a different gospel rather than a stricter version of the same one?
Where to look: the No Other Gospel theme above, and the Theology page's Seven Drumbeats of Grace, which follows the logic of 2:21 through the letter.
What are the modern equivalents of circumcision, the markers a community quietly requires before it counts someone as fully in?
Where to look: on the Context page, the covenant markers and what conversion cost a God-fearer, for the first-century weight of the marker before you translate it.
Paul confronts Peter, the senior apostle, in public. What made that necessary rather than merely brave, and what would "walking straight toward the truth of the gospel" look like in a church today?
Where to look: the walkthrough of chapters 1–2 above, and The Table at Antioch on the Context page.
Before Paul rebukes the Galatians, he names the Father who raised Jesus (1:1) and the Messiah who gave himself for our sins to rescue us from the present evil age (1:4). How do these opening claims shape the argument that follows?
Where to look: the Theology page's What Matters Is New Creation and Resurrection below the waterline.
Which details of Paul's call does Galatians 1:11–17 itself give, and which details do Acts 9, 22 and 26 supply? How do 1 Corinthians 9:1 and 15:8 add Paul's own witness to seeing the risen Jesus?
Where to look: The call, in two tellings on the Context page, and What was unveiled on the road on the Theology page.

On the law and the Spirit

Paul says the law is good and also that it imprisoned everyone under sin. How can both be true, and why does the timing (promise first, law 430 years later) matter to his argument?
Where to look: The Law: Holy, Temporary, and Not Against the Promises on the Theology page, and the Literary page's Torah chain, which lays the citations of chapter 3 in order.
The opponents' question was fair: without the law, how will people learn God's will? How does Paul's answer, the Spirit, avoid becoming "do whatever you feel"?
Where to look: the chapters 5–6 walkthrough above, and Freedom: From What, For What on the Theology page.
Fruit "requires cultivation." What does keeping in step with the Spirit look like as a practice rather than a feeling?
Where to look: the fruit grid in the walkthrough above, and The Fruit of the Spirit on the Theology page.
"Neither circumcision nor uncircumcision, but new creation." What would it mean for a church to be known for what it is rather than for what it is not?
Where to look: the New Creation theme above, and the Theology page's closing synthesis.
What do believers already have in 4:4–7, and what do they still await in 5:5, 5:21 and 6:8–9? How do present belonging and future hope shape a life led by the Spirit?
Where to look: on the Theology page, The Spirit's tense is future as well as present and the age track under the thesis.
