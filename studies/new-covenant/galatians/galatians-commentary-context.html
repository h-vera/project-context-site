The World Behind the Letter

A historical and literary commentary designed to help readers hear Galatians inside the cities it was written to: Roman colonies on an Anatolian plateau, synagogues with gentile admirers at the door, a mother goddess with self-wounding devotees, an emperor's deeds carved on temple walls, and a handful of new believers being told that one more step would make them real.

✦ Purpose of This Commentary Page

This is a world-building commentary.

Galatians is often read as a timeless treatise on faith against works. It is better read as an urgent letter to specific churches, written into a dispute whose terms were set by first-century Jewish identity, Roman civic life, and the economics of gift and honor. Paul is not writing a doctrine of justification in the abstract. He is answering the question of what a gentile in Pisidian Antioch or Iconium had to do to belong to the people of the God of Israel, and his answer reorganized the ancient world's categories of belonging.

Core reading claim

The letter's central question is not, "How is a person saved?" in the abstract. It is, "Who belongs to Abraham's family, on what basis, and what makes that family holy?" Every historical detail on this page is here because it sharpens one of those three questions.

Surface Problems

Visitors demanding circumcision of gentile believers; Paul's authority and gospel called secondhand; calendar observance taken up; tables dividing at Antioch; a church quarrelling.

Deeper Problem

Belonging to God's people measured by the Torah's identity markers, circumcision, food and calendar, rather than by union with the crucified Messiah: the gospel plus something.

Paul's Solution

Read the Torah in order (promise before law), see the curse borne at the cross, count one family by faith, and give that family a Spirit rather than a rulebook.

🗺️ Acts 13–14: The Birth of the Galatian Churches

On the reading these pages follow, the best starting point for Galatians is not Galatians 1:1 but Acts 13:13. Luke narrates the founding of four congregations in the southern part of the Roman province of Galatia, on Paul's first missionary journey with Barnabas, and the story he tells supplies the people, the opposition and the sermon that the letter assumes. (If the letter was written to ethnic Galatia in the north, Luke gives almost nothing, Acts 16:6 and 18:23, and this section becomes an analogy rather than a backstory. The question is taken up below.)

The sermon at Pisidian Antioch is a Galatians sermon

Luke gives Paul's synagogue address at Pisidian Antioch at length (13:16–41), and it is the one extended sample we have of what Paul first preached to the people who would receive this letter. It retells Israel's story from the exodus to David, announces Jesus as the promised son of David, raised from the dead, and ends with the sentence that the letter will spend six chapters defending:

"Let it be known to you therefore, brothers, that through this man forgiveness of sins is proclaimed to you, and from everything from which you could not be justified by the law of Moses, in him everyone who believes is justified." Acts 13:38–39

Whatever one concludes about Luke's method, the sermon as he reports it already sets justification by faith against the law of Moses, in a Galatian synagogue, before a mixed audience of Jews and God-fearers (13:16, 26). The Galatians did not first hear the thesis of 2:16 in the letter. They heard it the day their church was born.

Luke gives us the cast

Jews and devout converts

"Many of the Jews and devout converts to Judaism followed Paul and Barnabas" (13:43). Some of the first members were proselytes, gentiles who had already taken the step the agitators would later demand, and others were Jews who had never doubted that circumcision was the covenant's sign.

God-fearing gentiles

Paul addresses "Israelites, and you who fear God" (13:16, 26): gentiles attached to the synagogue, drawn to Israel's God and Scriptures, who had not become proselytes. The letter's question of whether such people must be circumcised to belong is exactly their question.

"Almost the whole city"

The next Sabbath the city turned out (13:44), the synagogue leaders were "filled with jealousy" (13:45), and Paul and Barnabas announced that they were turning to the gentiles (13:46–47, quoting Isaiah 49:6). The gentile majority of the Galatian churches begins here.

Why opposition formed, and from whom

In each city the pattern repeats. The synagogue is the door; many Jews and God-fearers believe; the synagogue's leadership, watching its gentile adherents and its standing in the city shift toward the new movement, organizes opposition through the people who could make it count. At Pisidian Antioch "the devout women of high standing and the leading men of the city" drive the missionaries out (13:50). At Iconium a plot forms "to mistreat and stone them" (14:5). At Lystra, Jews who had followed them from Antioch and Iconium win over the crowd, stone Paul and drag him out of the city as dead (14:19).

Lystra: gods, a priest of Zeus, and a stoning

Lystra is the one Galatian scene Luke tells at length (14:8–20), and it shows the pagan side of the world the letter addresses. A man lame from birth is healed; the crowd cries out in Lycaonian that the gods have come down in human form; the priest of Zeus-before-the-city brings oxen and garlands to sacrifice; Paul and Barnabas tear their clothes and preach the one living God who made heaven and earth and "did not leave himself without witness." Then Jews from Antioch and Iconium arrive, the crowd turns, and Paul is stoned. Timothy, whom Paul would later circumcise for the sake of mission among Jews (16:1–3), came from Lystra. The letter's "you received me as an angel of God" (4:14) and "I bear on my body the marks of Jesus" (6:17) have been read against this scene; the connection is attractive and cannot be proved.

Why Acts 13–14 belongs at the front of the commentary

Luke gives the sermon, the mixed audience, the synagogue's jealousy and the city's elite; the letter grows out of all four. The churches of Galatia were born from a proclamation that already set faith against the law of Moses as the ground of justification, among people for whom circumcision was the known line between admiring Israel's God and belonging to his people. The agitators did not bring a new question. They brought the old answer. And in Luke's design, as BibleProject's overview reads it, the first journey's consequences come to a head at the Jerusalem council, where the question these churches raised by existing is answered (below).

🧭 Which Galatia?

"Galatia" meant two things in Paul's day, and the letter does not say which it means. The ethnic region took its name from the Celtic Galatai who crossed into Anatolia in the third century BC and settled the north-central plateau around Ancyra, Pessinus and Tavium. The Roman province, organized in 25 BC from the kingdom of Amyntas, kept the name and reached far south, taking in parts of Pisidia, Phrygia and Lycaonia, including the cities of Acts 13–14. "The churches of Galatia" (1:2) could be either.

Destination and date: disputed Working position: south, early (held loosely)

Two Galatias. The teal ellipse is the ethnic region; the dashed amber line is the Roman province that included it and reached south to the cities Luke narrates.

The case for the south
  • We know these churches: Luke narrates their founding, their mixed membership and their persecution, and the letter fits all three.
  • Barnabas is mentioned three times as someone the readers know (2:1, 9, 13); he was on the first journey and never, so far as we know, in the north.
  • A province name is the only common term for a population of Pisidians, Phrygians and Lycaonians; Paul elsewhere names churches by province (Asia, Macedonia, Achaia), and "the churches of Galatia" took part in his collection (1 Cor 16:1).
  • If written before the council of Acts 15, the letter's silence about the apostolic decree is explained, and the visit of 2:1–10 is the famine-relief visit of Acts 11:27–30.
The case for the north
  • "Galatians" as a form of address (3:1) most naturally names people who were Galatians by ethnicity.
  • Acts 16:6 and 18:23 send Paul through "the Phrygian and Galatian region," which looks like territory distinct from the southern cities already visited.
  • Paul's "fourteen years" and two Jerusalem visits map more easily onto Acts 15 as the second visit, placing the letter in the mid-50s.
  • The theological maturity of the argument, especially its kinship with Romans, suits a later date.

Working position, and what hangs on it

The commentaries this suite draws on, Longenecker, Witherington and Fung, read the letter to the churches of Acts 13–14 and date it around AD 48–49, before the Jerusalem council; Wright's biography of Paul does the same, and in The Resurrection of the Son of God he says he is "inclined" to place the letter early, adding that its developed already-and-not-yet eschatology is the more remarkable if so. These pages follow them as a working position, held loosely. What hangs on it is less than it seems. The letter's argument, its ring structure and its theology read identically under either view. What changes is the backstory: south, and Acts 13–14 is the founding narrative; north, and the founding is unrecorded. And the chronology of the Jerusalem visits, which is the next section.

🏛️ Jerusalem: The Visits, the Council, and the Silence

Galatians 1–2 counts Paul's trips to Jerusalem (three years after his call, 1:18; fourteen years later, 2:1) and Acts records more of them (9:26; 11:27–30; 15:2; 18:22; 21:17). Lining them up has occupied a century of scholarship, and Wilson, with Hall, declines to settle it: the narrative of Galatians 1–2 was written to persuade, not to supply a diary. Two representative reconstructions are set out below. They tend to go with the two Galatias, though a southern destination with a later date is a third combination some hold.

Reconstruction A · south, early

  1. c. AD 33 · The call. On the road to Damascus (Acts 9; Gal 1:15–16). Then Arabia and Damascus (1:17).
  2. c. 36 · Jerusalem, visit one. Fifteen days with Cephas, a glimpse of James (1:18–19; Acts 9:26–30). Then Syria and Cilicia (1:21).
  3. c. 46–47 · Jerusalem, visit two. With Barnabas and Titus, "privately" with the pillars; the famine-relief visit of Acts 11:27–30 (2:1–10).
  4. c. 47–48 · The first journey. Cyprus and southern Galatia (Acts 13–14).
  5. c. 48 · Antioch. The confrontation with Cephas (2:11–14). The agitators reach Galatia.
  6. c. 48–49 · Galatians written, possibly the earliest letter of Paul's we have.
  7. c. 49 · The council (Acts 15). The decree is issued after the letter, which is why the letter never cites it.

Reconstruction B · north, later

  1. c. AD 33 · The call, Arabia, Damascus (1:15–17).
  2. c. 36 · Jerusalem, visit one (1:18–19; Acts 9:26–30).
  3. c. 47–48 · The first journey (Acts 13–14), with the famine visit of Acts 11 left unmentioned in the letter.
  4. c. 49 · Jerusalem, visit two = the council. Gal 2:1–10 and Acts 15 are the same meeting, told from two angles.
  5. c. 49–50 · Antioch (2:11–14), after the council; the "people from James" press the decree's limits or more.
  6. c. 50 and 52 · The second and third journeys pass through ethnic Galatia (Acts 16:6; 18:23); churches are founded there.
  7. c. 53–57 · Galatians written from Ephesus or Macedonia, near in time and thought to Romans.

The call, in two tellings

Read Paul's testimony and Luke's narratives together, keeping each source's contribution visible.

Paul's own account

Galatians 1:11–17. Paul explains the source of his gospel: God's revelation of his Son and commission to proclaim him among the gentiles. He gives little detail about the encounter itself.

Scriptural call language

Isaiah 49:1, 5; Jeremiah 1:5. The language of being set apart from the womb and called for a mission echoes Israel's prophetic callings. These passages illuminate how Paul describes his vocation.

Paul's other testimony

1 Corinthians 9:1; 15:8. Paul says he has seen Jesus the Lord and that the risen Christ appeared to him. These first-person claims contribute evidence beyond Galatians' brief account.

Luke's narrated accounts

Acts 9, 22, 26. Luke supplies narrative settings and details for comparison with Paul's letters. Acts 9, for example, describes the road to Damascus, the light and the blindness.

Wright reads the revealed Son first as Israel's Messiah, which is what Paul preaches in the Damascus synagogues "at once" (Acts 9:20), and he notes that Luke, like Paul, tells the event in the terms of a prophet's call. The word "revelation" alone does not settle the precise character of Paul's experience, and Galatians shows how Paul interpreted what happened to him rather than "what precisely it was that happened." He suggests that "in me" (en emoi) may emphasize revelation through Paul's proclamation, while requiring revelation to Paul first.

N. T. Wright, The Resurrection of the Son of God, pp. 379–381.

The silence about the decree

Acts 15 ends with a letter from Jerusalem that settles the circumcision question in the gentiles' favor and asks only for abstention from idol-food, blood, strangled meat and sexual immorality (15:28–29). If Galatians was written after that, Paul had a Jerusalem ruling in hand that answered the agitators directly, and did not use it. Reconstruction A explains the silence by dating; Reconstruction B needs another explanation, most often Paul's reluctance to rest his gospel on Jerusalem's authority, which is itself a theme of chapter 1.

"Privately" and the council

Galatians 2:2 describes a private meeting with the acknowledged leaders. Acts 15 describes a public assembly with speeches and a decree. Those who identify the two read the private meeting as one stage of the larger event; those who separate them understand the private meeting as a separate, earlier occasion. Keener's commentary on Acts 15 lays out the options; Longenecker's introduction argues the identification with Acts 11.

What both reconstructions agree on. Paul's gospel to the gentiles was recognized by Cephas, James and John, who added nothing to it and asked only that the poor be remembered (2:6–10). The Antioch confrontation came after that recognition, not before it, which is what gives Paul's charge of hypocrisy its force: Cephas withdrew from a table that, as Paul read the Jerusalem agreement, he had already conceded belonged to everyone. Whatever the dates, the sequence inside the letter stands.

What Luke is doing in Acts 15

Acts 15 is Luke's chapter, and it deserves to be read for Luke's point before it is read for Paul's. The pages above use it for chronology; this part reads it as the thing Luke wrote, and then sets it beside the letter without collapsing the two.

In Luke's design, as BibleProject's overview reads it, the first journey's consequences come to a head in Jerusalem. The churches founded in 13–14 raised, by existing, the question the council answers, and only then does the mission turn toward Europe (15:36–16:10).

Luke's point: who decided, and how

Luke does not settle the question by apostolic fiat. He gives four voices, and the order in which he gives them is itself the point. BibleProject's overview of Acts puts the outcome plainly: Peter, Paul and James "all show from the Scriptures and from their experience that God's plan was always to include the nations within his covenant people," and the decision was "groundbreaking," because membership among Jesus's people would no longer rest on ethnic identity or the laws of the Torah but on trusting Jesus and following him.

Peter · from experience to grace

God chose that the gentiles should hear through him (15:7); "bore witness to them by giving them the Holy Spirit just as he did to us" (15:8); "made no distinction between us and them, having cleansed their hearts by faith" (15:9). To impose the law now would be "a yoke on the neck of the disciples that neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear" (15:10). "We believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will" (15:11).

Barnabas and Paul · from the field

They relate "what signs and wonders God had done through them among the gentiles" (15:12). Luke lets the missionaries testify but gives them no speech; the argument that carries the day is Peter's experience and James's Scripture, which is Luke's way of saying the gentile mission was not Paul's private project.

James · from Scripture

"Simeon has related how God first visited the gentiles, to take from them a people (λαόν) for his name" (15:14): gentiles as people, Israel's own word. The prophets agree: Amos 9:11–12 in the Greek Bible, the tent of David rebuilt "that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord, and all the gentiles who are called by my name" (15:16–17). Judgment: "we should not trouble those of the gentiles who turn to God" (15:19).

The letter · for the Spirit

"Some persons have gone out from us and troubled you with words, unsettling your minds, although we gave them no instructions" (15:24). "It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to lay on you no greater burden than these requirements" (15:28). Reached "with one accord" (15:25); received in Antioch with rejoicing (15:31).

Experience, Scripture, Spirit. That is the order of Luke's council: what God did among the gentiles, what the prophets said he would do, and the Spirit's verdict on both. It is close to the order of Galatians 3: the Spirit the Galatians received by hearing with faith (3:1–5), then Abraham and the prophets (3:6–29), with the Spirit as the promise's content (3:14). The comparison does not require that either writer copied the other; both are describing how the first believers reasoned.

The four requirements

What the council asked of gentile believers instead of circumcision was four abstentions: from food polluted by idols, from sexual immorality, from what has been strangled, and from blood (15:20, 29). Luke gives the reason in the next sentence: "Moses has had in every city those who proclaim him, for he is read every Sabbath in the synagogues" (15:21). Jewish believers with Torah scruples sit at the same tables. Two readings of the four are current. Both see a shared life behind them; they differ on the rationale and on the scope.

The temple reading · BibleProject, Witherington
  • The four belong together in a pagan temple: sacrificial meals, meat with the blood in it, animals killed without draining, and the sexual immorality Jewish writers associated with idolatry.
  • BibleProject's overview reads the letter as requiring gentile believers "to stop participating in pagan temple sacrifices," while not requiring "an ethnically Jewish identity" or the Torah's laws.
  • Witherington's Acts commentary argues the case in detail: the one thing a gentile believer had to leave was the gods.
The Leviticus reading · Bruce, Keener
  • Leviticus 17–18 lays duties on "the stranger who sojourns among you": no sacrifice away from the sanctuary (17:8–9), no eating of blood (17:10–12), no eating of hunted animals whose blood has not been drained (17:13–14, with a rule for anyone who eats carrion, 17:15), no forbidden unions (18:6–26).
  • Bruce notes that the decree's four items match those rules; Keener's commentary sets out the evidence and the alternatives.
  • On this reading the council asked of gentiles what the Torah itself asked of gentiles living within Israel, so that Jews and gentiles could share a table without the Jews breaking faith.

Either way the decree is not a smaller law for salvation. It is the terms on which a mixed church can eat together, which is to say, the kind of table problem Paul describes at Antioch, addressed in writing. Then Luke does something a reader of Galatians should notice. On the second journey Paul carries the decisions to Derbe and Lystra "for observance" (16:4), in the same paragraph in which he circumcises Timothy "because of the Jews who were in those places" (16:3). For Luke, circumcision is a matter of mission, not of salvation; that is Paul's "neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything" (5:6; 6:15) in narrative form.

Reading Acts 15 without wresting it

Luke's purpose and Paul's are not the same, and the honest reading keeps them apart. Luke is showing how Jesus, by the Spirit, led a divided church to one mind so that the mission could go on; his council ends in a letter, an agreement about tables, and rejoicing in Antioch. He does not hide the strain: "no small dissension and debate" (15:2), and a few verses later Paul and Barnabas part company over Mark (15:39). Paul is arguing a gospel against people who are winning; his letter describes no public council and quotes no decree, invokes no Jerusalem ruling even where one would have helped, and never mentions the four abstentions. On the early dating the silence is a matter of chronology; on the later it needs another explanation, most often Paul's reluctance to rest his gospel on Jerusalem's authority, which is the burden of chapter 1. What the two share is not a document but a conviction and a method: that God had given gentiles the Spirit as gentiles, that Scripture had said he would, and that to demand circumcision afterwards was to lay a yoke on people God had already received. Acts 15 is Luke's testimony that Paul's gospel was the church's. Galatians is Paul's testimony that it did not come from the church. Both can be true, and the pages read them that way. 🍞 The Table at Antioch

Syrian Antioch was the third city of the empire and the place where the Jesus movement first became visibly mixed. Jewish believers scattered from Jerusalem had preached there to Greeks as well as Jews; Barnabas was sent to see; he fetched Paul from Tarsus; and it was in Antioch that the disciples were first called Christians (Acts 11:19–26). Martin Hengel's work on the "Hellenists" traces how this Greek-speaking, gentile-welcoming community became the base of Paul's mission. Antioch was also where the question of the table came to a head.

"For before certain people came from James, he was eating with the gentiles; but when they came he drew back and separated himself, fearing those of the circumcision. And the rest of the Jews joined him in the play-acting, so that even Barnabas was carried away by their hypocrisy." Galatians 2:12–13

Why a meal was the whole question

In the ancient Mediterranean, to eat with someone was to belong with them. Meals marked the boundaries of households, associations and peoples, and the food laws of the Torah made the Jewish table a boundary with God's name on it. Peter had crossed it. Acts 10–11 tells how: a vision, a gentile household, and the conclusion that "God has shown me that I should not call any person common or unclean" (10:28). At Antioch he was living that conclusion, eating with uncircumcised believers as one family, until the arrival of people from James made him afraid.

What Peter's withdrawal said
  • There are two tables in this church, and the Jewish one is the real one.
  • Gentile believers belong provisionally; full belonging is still marked by Torah.
  • The pressure from "those of the circumcision" is worth more than the gentiles' standing.
  • Barnabas's agreement made it the policy of the church's leadership.
What Paul's gospel said
  • One table, because one justification: Jew and gentile alike "through faith of the Messiah" (2:15–16).
  • To withdraw is to "compel the gentiles to live like Jews" (2:14), which is the agitators' demand in another form.
  • The issue is "the truth of the gospel" (2:5, 14), the same phrase as in Jerusalem.
  • Fear of the circumcision party is the thread that runs from Antioch to Galatia (2:12; 6:12).

Who were "those of the circumcision," and what did James send?

Paul does not say that James sent the demand, only that people came "from James" and that Cephas feared "those of the circumcision." The phrase may mean Jewish believers who insisted on the Torah's boundaries, or the wider Jewish community whose displeasure could cost the Jerusalem church dearly in a tense decade. Either way, Paul's complaint is not that Peter believed something false but that he acted against what he believed, and that his action, because of who he was, redrew the church's boundary in a single meal. The identity of the group: plausible readings, not settled

Antioch and Galatia are one event seen from both ends

At Antioch a Jew withdraws from the gentile table under pressure from the circumcision party. In Galatia gentiles are being pressed to join the Jewish table by being circumcised. Paul puts the two stories back to back (2:11–14 into 2:15–21) because they are the same error: belonging measured by the Torah's marks. On Wilson's ring the two circumcision units, 2:3–14 and 5:1–12, frame the whole proof, and the Antioch scene is where the first of them ends.

👥 The Agitators: Who, What, and Why

We know the people who troubled Galatia only through Paul's reflection of them, and John Barclay's warning about "mirror-reading" a polemical letter applies with full force: not every denial in the letter answers an accusation, and not every emphasis is a correction. What can be said with confidence is less than the commentaries sometimes claim, and it is still a great deal.

Who they were

Jewish believers in Jesus, not outsiders: they preached "a gospel" (1:6–7), wanted the Galatians circumcised (5:2; 6:12–13), and were persuading rather than coercing (3:1; 5:7–8). Paul never names them, calls them "the ones troubling you" (1:7; 5:12; cf. 5:10), and treats them as a movement rather than an individual.

What they taught

Circumcision as the completion of gentile conversion (5:2–3), observance of the Jewish calendar (4:10), and, presumably, that the family of Abraham was entered through the covenant's sign (which is why Paul argues Abraham at such length). They may have claimed Jerusalem's backing and questioned Paul's standing (chapters 1–2 answer both).

Why, by Paul's account

"They want to make a good showing in the flesh … so that they may not be persecuted for the cross of the Messiah" (6:12), and "so that they may boast in your flesh" (6:13). Also: "they court you, so as to shut you out, so that you will court them" (4:17).

Their sincerity

Nothing in the letter suggests the agitators thought of themselves as opponents of the gospel. On their understanding, Paul had done half a job: he had brought gentiles to Israel's Messiah and left them outside Israel. Circumcision would finish it. Josephus tells a story that shows how reasonable this looked. When Izates, king of Adiabene, was drawn to the God of Israel, the Jewish merchant Ananias told him he could worship God without circumcision; then Eleazar, a Jew from Galilee strict about the law, found the king reading Moses and asked how long he intended to remain uncircumcised while reading what the law required (Antiquities 20.34–48). The king was circumcised. Eleazar's question is the agitators' question, and Paul's letter is Ananias's answer argued from the Torah itself.

Why persecution avoidance was a real motive

A gentile who worshipped Israel's God without joining Israel was legible to no one. Circumcised, he was a proselyte, inside a people whose practices Rome had long tolerated. Uncircumcised and refusing the city's gods, he was something new, and Larry Hurtado's Destroyer of the gods shows how disturbing that novelty was to neighbors. Circumcising the gentile believers would have made the Galatian churches a recognizable extension of the synagogue, and would have spared their Jewish members the hostility Paul himself had met in these cities (Acts 13:50; 14:19).

Why Paul would not grant it

Because "if righteousness were through the law, then the Messiah died for nothing" (2:21). An addition that makes the cross unnecessary is a subtraction. Paul's counter-claim is that the agitators' sincerity was exactly the problem: the better their case looked, the more completely it replaced the Messiah with a mark. Barclay's Obeying the Truth reconstructs the one objection of theirs that Paul takes most seriously, that without the law gentile converts would have no moral guide, and shows that chapters 5–6 are his answer.

🏛️ A Day in a Galatian City

Pisidian Antioch, where the first of these churches was planted, was a Roman colony, settled with veterans under Augustus, Latin-speaking at the top, with a sanctuary of the emperor on the hill and a copy of Augustus's own account of his deeds, the Res Gestae, carved in stone. The best-preserved copy of that text stands in the north, on the temple of Augustus and Rome at Ancyra. Whichever Galatia the letter went to, its readers lived under the gaze of Caesar's temple.

The synagogue and its door

Jews had lived in Phrygia and Lydia since Antiochus III settled two thousand Jewish families there around 200 BC (Josephus, Antiquities 12.147–153), and John Barclay's survey of the diaspora shows communities in Asia Minor that were established, visible and, within limits, protected. Their synagogues drew gentile admirers, the "God-fearers" of Acts, who kept the Sabbath loosely, learned the Scriptures and stopped at the door marked circumcision.

The emperor's temple

The imperial cult in Galatia was not a Roman imposition on a reluctant province; the cities competed to honor Augustus, and the temple at Ancyra with its carved Res Gestae was a civic boast. Loyalty to the city and honor to Caesar were one gesture, and a convert who withdrew from both was conspicuous.

The Mother of the gods

Pessinus, in ethnic Galatia, was the ancient seat of Cybele, the Great Mother, whose devotees, the galli, castrated themselves in her service and were known throughout Asia Minor. Paul's wish that the circumcisers would "cut themselves off" (5:12) was written for an audience that knew exactly what that looked like.

What conversion cost a God-fearer

A gentile who moved from the synagogue's doorstep into the Jesus movement had given up the city's gods without gaining the synagogue's protection. He belonged to no recognized category, and the agitators' offer, circumcision, was among other things an offer of a category.

Why circumcision tempted. It was painful, irreversible, and for an adult male in a city with public baths and a gymnasium, visible. It also resolved every ambiguity at once: a circumcised believer was a proselyte, a member of a protected people, legible to his neighbors and safe from the charge of having abandoned the gods for nothing. ✡️ Circumcision as Covenant Sign

To hear the agitators fairly, circumcision has to be heard as they heard it: not a ritual preference but the sign God himself attached to the covenant with Abraham, "an everlasting covenant" in Abraham's flesh (Genesis 17:13), reaffirmed under persecution and sealed in Jewish memory with blood.

Genesis 17:10–14

"Every male among you shall be circumcised … and the uncircumcised male … shall be cut off from his people; he has broken my covenant." The sign and the sanction, from the same chapter.

Jubilees 15

The second-century BC retelling makes circumcision an ordinance for all generations and declares that the uncircumcised belong not to the children of the covenant but to the children of destruction. This is the kind of conviction the agitators held.

1 Maccabees 1:60–61

Under Antiochus IV, mothers who had their sons circumcised were put to death with their infants hung from their necks. Circumcision became the mark of fidelity under persecution, and the Maccabean memory was alive in the first century (deSilva's introduction to the Apocrypha sets out the story and its afterlife).

Galatians 1:13–14

Paul's own "zeal for the traditions of my fathers," and his persecution of the church, stand in that Maccabean line. When he writes that he once advanced in Judaism beyond his contemporaries, he is describing a former self who would have agreed with the agitators.

Acts 15:1

"Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved." Luke's one-sentence summary of the position, put in the mouths of believers from Judea.

Galatians 5:2–3

Paul's reply: accept circumcision and the Messiah will be of no benefit to you; accept it and you owe the whole law. He does not deny Genesis 17. He goes behind it, to Genesis 15 and 12, where Abraham was counted righteous and the nations were blessed, before the sign was given.

Genesis 17 and the shape of the story

The site's Abraham profile reads the Abraham cycle (Genesis 11:27–25:11) as a ring whose center is Genesis 17: the covenant of circumcision and the new names. Whatever one makes of that proposal, Genesis 17 is where a circumcision argument naturally stands, and it was not a weak position. Paul's reply moves to the story's beginning, the call and the counted righteousness of Genesis 12 and 15, and to its far side, the expulsion of the slave woman's son in Genesis 21 (4:30; the allegory has a study of its own). He does not dispute Genesis 17; he argues that the story around it decides what it means. That the agitators themselves read the cycle as a ring is not something the letter tells us.

Not legalism, but covenant loyalty

E. P. Sanders's account of Second Temple Judaism as "covenantal nomism," in which obedience to the law is the response of a people already in covenant and not a ladder into it, changed how this dispute is read. The agitators were not teaching that one earns God's favor by effort. They were teaching that God's covenant has a sign and that the sign is required of those who join it, which is what Genesis 17 says. Paul's argument has to be, and is, that the covenant's basis was faith before it had a sign, that the sign belonged to Abraham's household in the age before the Messiah (Genesis 17 gives it to his descendants and to the foreign-born of his house alike, 17:12–13), and that the Messiah has now gathered Abraham's family on the basis on which Abraham himself was reckoned righteous. Matthew Thiessen's Jewish Paul presses the point from the other side: Paul did not want gentiles to stop being gentiles, because the promise was that the nations, as nations, would be blessed.

🎁 Grace, Patronage, and the Gift

"Grace," χάρις, was not a religious word before it was a social one. In the Greco-Roman world charis was the favor a patron showed a client and the gratitude the client owed back; as David deSilva shows, a gift created a relationship, and the relationship ran on obligation. John Barclay's Paul and the Gift asks what kind of gift Paul thought the Christ-gift was, and his answer explains the fury of Galatians.

Gift in the Greco-Roman world
  • Given to the worthy: a benefactor chose recipients whose standing fit the gift.
  • Returned in honor: a client repaid with public praise, loyalty and service.
  • Discriminating by nature: to give indiscriminately was foolishness, not virtue.
  • A gift that ignored worth was an insult to the system and a puzzle to its beneficiaries.
The Christ-gift in Galatians
  • Given without regard to worth: to "gentile sinners" and to the persecutor of the church (1:13–16; 2:15).
  • Incongruous by design: the gift creates the worth it does not find, "I live by faith of the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me" (2:20).
  • Circumcision would make the gift congruous, fitted to a prior standing, and so "nullify the grace of God" (2:21).
  • To seek standing by law is to "fall from grace" (5:4): not to lose a feeling but to leave a gift-relationship for a wage-relationship.

"Called in grace" · 1:6

The Galatians were called ἐν χάριτι, in grace, and Paul's astonishment is that they are deserting the one who called them that way. The letter's first use of the word in argument (the greeting of 1:3 has it first) already carries Barclay's point: the calling had no prior condition, and the agitators were adding one.

"Remember the poor" · 2:10

The one request the pillars made of Paul was that he remember the poor, which he says he was eager to do. Bruce Longenecker's Remember the Poor argues that this was not only the Jerusalem collection but a standing commitment of Paul's gentile communities to the poor among them: the Christ-gift, received, became a gift given on.

Why this makes 2:21 the letter's hinge. A gift that has to be completed by the recipient is not the gift that was given. Paul's "I do not nullify the grace of God" is a claim about the kind of thing the cross was: a gift to the unworthy that made them Abraham's children. Add circumcision as a condition of belonging, and the gift becomes a reward, the cross becomes unnecessary, and the Messiah "died for nothing."

🏠 Slaves, Heirs, and Guardians: The Household Behind 3:24–4:7

Paul's picture of the law as a παιδαγωγός and of believers as heirs who have come of age and received adoption assumes a household his readers knew from the inside. Three of its features matter.

The pedagogue

Not the schoolteacher but the slave who walked a boy to school, carried his things and kept him in line, with a rod when necessary, until he came of age. The figure was familiar enough to be a stock character in comedy. Paul's point (3:24–25) is custody with an end date: the guardian's authority ended on the day the boy became a man.

The minor heir

"As long as the heir is a child, he is no different from a slave, though he is owner of everything, but he is under guardians and managers until the date set by his father" (4:1–2). Greek and Roman law both knew the guardianship of minors, though a date "set by the father" fits some Hellenistic and provincial practice better than Roman law, where the terms were fixed by statute; Richard Longenecker's commentary works through the options. The point survives all of them: heirship and freedom arrive together, on a date the father chose.

Adoption

υἱοθεσία (4:5) is a Roman legal word. Romans adopted adults to secure an heir, and the adoptee gained the full status of a son; Julius Caesar's adoption of Octavian and Augustus's of Tiberius were the empire's most famous examples. Michael Peppard's study of divine sonship in the Roman world shows how public and how political the institution was. Paul's gentile readers heard "adoption as sons" as a change of legal standing, not a metaphor for feeling loved.

The exodus underneath the household

Sylvia Keesmaat reads 4:1–7 as an exodus story told in household terms: slavery, a God who sends a deliverer at the appointed time, redemption, sonship ("Israel is my firstborn son," Exodus 4:22), and the inheritance. The Roman legal picture and the Israelite story coincide, which is what Paul's gentile readers needed: their adoption into Abraham's family was the exodus happening to them.

✉️ The Letter as a Physical Object

Galatians was dictated, carried, and read aloud, and each of those facts leaves marks on the text.

Dictation

Paul composed through a secretary, as Romans 16:22 shows and as E. Randolph Richards documents for the whole corpus. Dictation explains the letter's oral texture: the sudden "O foolish Galatians" (3:1), the broken sentences, the repeated words that stitch the seams.

The autograph

"See with what large letters I write to you with my own hand" (6:11). The sender's closing in his own script authenticated a dictated letter; Paul takes the pen for the entire epilogue and draws attention to the size of his writing, as emphasis and as proof.

Carriage

No carrier is named. "The churches of Galatia" (1:2) means the one letter was carried from city to city and read in each, which is why the argument has to stand on its own without the carrier's explanation that Paul's other letters could rely on.

Hearing

Brian Wright's study of communal reading shows how normal it was for texts to be heard in groups. The Galatians heard this letter in one sitting, perhaps twenty minutes, and the ring structure Wilson describes is what made a twenty-minute argument memorable: a hearer could hold the pairs the way a reader holds an outline.

A letter built for the ear. Wilson's case, that Galatians was patterned for oral performance and memory, is the case behind this site's Structured Edition. Reading the letter on a page as a row of paragraphs hides the design that its first hearers caught by instinct. The Movement Commentary walks that design; the Literary Design page diagrams it.

📜 The Tanakh Story Behind the Letter

Galatians is one of the most Scripture-dense of Paul's letters, with ten explicit quotations in six short chapters, and it does not quote at random. Paul is telling Israel's story in a particular order, and the order is the argument.

Genesis

Abraham believed and was counted righteous (15:6); the nations were blessed in him (12:3); the promise was to his seed (13:15; 17:8); two sons by two mothers (16; 21). The story before Sinai, which Paul makes the ground of everything; the Abraham profile walks it episode by episode.

Exodus and Deuteronomy

The law given 430 years after the promise (Exodus 12:40); the curse on those who do not keep all of it (Deuteronomy 27:26); the curse on the hanged man (21:23). The law's own account of its sanctions, which Wright reads as the curse of exile, borne by the Messiah.

Habakkuk, Leviticus, Isaiah

"The righteous one will live by faith" (Habakkuk 2:4) against "the one who does them will live by them" (Leviticus 18:5); "love your neighbor" as the whole law (19:18); the barren woman who sings (Isaiah 54:1) as the Jerusalem above.

A note on method: how much can be reconstructed?

Much of this page reconstructs a situation from a one-sided letter, and the limits are real. The agitators' theology is inferred; the visits are counted two ways; the destination is argued. The honest position is to treat the south-and-early reading as a well-supported working hypothesis, to say where the alternative would change a judgment, and to let the letter's own argument, which is the same under both, carry the weight.

The Galatians' danger was not sincerity. It was a definition of belonging.

Final synthesis

The commentary lens for Galatians is not "faith versus works" in the abstract. It is "learn to hear the letter inside cities where a gentile believer belonged to no recognized people, where the synagogue's sign of belonging was a sincere and ancient offer, and where a crucified Messiah had made one family out of two." Paul teaches these churches that the family was founded on faith before it had a sign, that the gift that made them members cannot be completed by a mark, and that the Spirit, not the law, makes the family new.
