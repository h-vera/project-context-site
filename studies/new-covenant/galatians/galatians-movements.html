Πρὸς Γαλάτας

Seven Rings Around a Single Verse

Galatians does not march from chapter to chapter; on Wilson's reading it climbs. Seven matched pairs, salutation to epilogue, rise from Paul's greeting and his own story to the verse the ring is built around, then come back down, step for step, to the churches that needed to hear it. This page walks the pairs.

Where 1 Thessalonians moves in five waves that each land on the coming of Jesus, and 2 Thessalonians in three blocks that each land on a prayer, Galatians moves in rings. On the reading followed here, Victor Wilson's in Divine Symmetries, building on Robert Hall, the letter is concentric: the salutation and the epilogue stand as two end pieces, and inside them the proof (1:10–6:10) runs in six pairs of units that mirror one another around a center, and the center is a single verse, 3:24. Seven rings in all. So this page does not walk the chapters in order. It walks the pairs. Each movement reads a unit on the way up beside its partner on the way down, and ends at the seam, with the repeated word Paul uses to stitch one unit to the next. Three short sections at the end step back to the other canons of classical rhetoric, style, memory and delivery, because Wilson's point is that the ring was built to be heard and remembered, not diagrammed. For the letter read straight through, in its three parts, start with the Book Overview; the Literary Design page diagrams the craft, the Commentary & Context page builds the world behind the letter, and the Theology page gathers the synthesis.

① Movement 1 · A ⇄ A′ · 1:1–9 ⇄ 6:11–18 · The Frame

The five canons of classical rhetoric · Wilson reads Galatians through them in turn

Outer ring Salutation + proposition ⇄ epilogue Species: judicial or deliberative Ring: proposed (Wilson 1997)

Before the ring there is the frame, and before the frame there is a question that has occupied Galatians scholarship for fifty years: what kind of speech is this letter? Hans Dieter Betz noticed that almost every commentary supplies an outline of Galatians and almost none explains how the outline was derived, and he answered his own complaint by reading the letter through classical rhetoric. His conclusion was that Galatians is a judicial speech of the apologetic kind, Paul defending his apostleship the way a defendant persuades a court. George Kennedy accepted the method and rejected the verdict. Galatians, he argued, is deliberative: its purpose is not to defend Paul against charges but to move the Galatians toward a decision about the gospel they had received. Kennedy's student Robert Hall pressed the case in two articles, and it is Hall's outline that Wilson adopts.

Judicial · Betz
  • A courtroom genre: the speaker's task is to persuade a court of innocence after the fact.
  • Reads the story of 1:12–2:14 as a narratio, the statement of facts a defense requires; Betz's commentary outlines the letter as prescript, exordium, narratio, propositio, probatio, exhortatio, postscript.
  • Its strength is the narrative itself: judicial speeches characteristically narrate, and here is a narrative.
Deliberative · Kennedy, Hall
  • A genre for decisions about the future: the speaker urges a course of action.
  • A simpler outline, because deliberative speech normally omits narration and refutation: salutation in place of the proem, proposition, proof, epilogue.
  • Hall's observations: Paul never recounts charges in order to rebut them; the narrative looks forward to the Galatians' renewed commitment rather than backward to a defense; and the exhortation of 5:1–6:10 is a call to future action.

Narration is scarce in deliberative oratory for a reason Aristotle states plainly: nobody can narrate what has not yet happened, so when a deliberative speaker does tell a story, it is a story of the past told to help the hearers plan the future better (Rhetoric 3.16). On Kennedy and Hall's account, the narrative section is exactly what misdirected Betz. Wilson adds a count. By his reckoning the word "gospel" occurs thirteen times in the letter: five in the proposition, six in the narrative, and only two across the remaining four chapters. The narrative is where the proposition gets its evidence, not where Paul answers for himself.

Proof1:10–6:10Reasons to accept the proposition: a narrative (1:10–2:21) and a series of arguments (3:1–6:10), arranged concentrically
Epilogue6:11–18Recapitulation, summary and blessing, in Paul's own hand

Wilson's contribution is to look inside the proof. The arguments between 1:10 and 6:10 are not a list; they are arranged concentrically, and the classical outline does not compete with that arrangement any more than a staircase competes with the building it stands in. For simplicity he calls the form chiastic, while noting that scholars of classical rhetoric usually call a frame this large ring composition. John Bligh had seen a concentric Galatians decades earlier, with results Wilson says differ from his own, and Wilson points to comparable proposals for Philippians (Luter and Lee) and Colossians (Drake) as evidence that ring composition is not foreign to Paul's less-disputed letters.

One more feature of the ring before the frame itself: the pairs alternate. Rings built on an internal opposition (faith against works, blessing against curse, Spirit against flesh) take turns with rings that play on a single theme (circumcision, eyes, the rationale of law or of faith). The alternation is Wilson's observation, and it holds all the way to the center.

↑ On the way up · A · 1:1–9

Salutation and proposition

The salutation breaks rank in its first line. Paul cannot wait for the body of the letter to broach his subject, so he loads it into the address: an apostle οὐκ ἀπ' ἀνθρώπων οὐδὲ δι' ἀνθρώπου, not from human beings nor through a human being, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father who raised him from the dead (1:1). There is neither thanksgiving nor blessing, which makes this opening unique among Paul's letters to churches. In its place stands the proposition (1:6–9): astonishment that they are so quickly deserting to ἕτερον εὐαγγέλιον, a different gospel, which is no gospel at all, and the thesis the whole proof will support, that there is no gospel beyond the one Paul preached to them.

He says it twice, with the same curse both times, ἀνάθεμα ἔστω, let that one be accursed, even if the preacher is an angel from heaven, even if it is Paul himself (1:8–9).

↓ On the way down · A′ · 6:11–18

Signature, summary and blessing

Paul takes the pen from the secretary: see πηλίκοις γράμμασιν, with what large letters, I write to you in my own hand (6:11). The epilogue restates the contrast between Paul and his opponents in compressed form. They want a good showing in the flesh and press circumcision to escape persecution for the cross of Christ (6:12), though they do not keep the law themselves (6:13). Paul boasts in nothing but the cross, through which the world has been crucified to him and he to the world (6:14); neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything, but καινὴ κτίσις, new creation (6:15); peace and mercy on all who walk by this rule, and on the Israel of God (6:16); and the marks of Jesus on his body (6:17), set against the mark the agitators want on the Galatians' bodies. Grace closes it (6:18).

Project Context observation: the two end pieces hold the letter's only explicit mention of the resurrection, "who raised him from the dead" (1:1), and its "new creation" (6:15). N. T. Wright hears the second as the first under another name, "resurrection seen precisely as new creation," and reads the resurrection as the premise the whole letter assumes and never states; the Theology page follows him. On that reading the frame opens and closes on the one thing the proof takes for granted.

Two personal pleas hold the ring. Wilson's image is of a proof that falls between two end pieces of intensely personal address: the first asking the Galatians to reconsider his gospel, the last notarized, after the secretary's script, by Paul's conspicuously large signature. The outer ring is Paul's own voice. Everything inside it is argument, and the argument rises and falls between those two moments of direct address.

How confident is this structure?

Project Context marks proposed structures with a confidence level, and this one has several. Textual: the word distributions Wilson leans on (circumcision confined to C and C′ within the proof, eyes to E and E′, Abraham seven times in F) can be checked by anyone with a concordance. Strong: the seam hooks are verbal and sit where he says they sit. Proposed: the seven-pair architecture and the single-verse center are Wilson's reading; Bligh's concentric Galatians divides the letter differently, and most commentaries outline it linearly. Disputed: the rhetorical species, on which Betz and Kennedy still have followers.

This page follows Wilson because the pairs explain more of the letter's cross-references than a linear outline does. It does not claim Paul drew the diagram.

⟡ Seam ↑ A → B "accursed" (ἀνάθεμα, 1:8–9) → "violently persecuting" (καθ' ὑπερβολὴν ἐδίωκον, 1:13). The anathema on anyone who preaches another gospel hands off to Paul's confession that he once tried to destroy the church; the curse he pronounces had described him.
⟡ Seam ↓ B′ → A′ "sows to the flesh" (εἰς τὴν σάρκα, 6:8) → "a good showing in the flesh" (ἐν σαρκί, 6:12–13). Project Context addition: Wilson's list leaves this seam unmarked.

② Movement 2 · B ⇄ B′ · 1:10–2:2 ⇄ 5:13–6:10 · Christ's Servant, the Spirit's Harvest

Oppositional contrast Servant of Christ vs servant of others ⇄ Spirit vs flesh Verbal link: δοῦλος · δουλεύετε

The first contrast pair. On the way up, Paul insists that the gospel he preached has its own complete integrity: it came from God, not from or even through a human being (1:11–12). On the way down he draws the consequence for the Galatians: sow to the Spirit, not to the flesh (6:8). Wilson reads both units as setting divine inspiration against human appeasement, and both as setting a life before Christ against a life after him, the persecutor against the man called by grace in B, the flesh against the Spirit in B′. B′ confirms what B began: the one is life in the flesh, the other life in the Spirit.

↑ On the way up · B · 1:10–2:2

Not of human origin

Verse 10 is the hinge from proposition to proof and gives the unit its name: am I now persuading people, or God? If I were still pleasing people, Χριστοῦ δοῦλος οὐκ ἂν ἤμην, I would not be Christ's slave. Then the claim (1:11–12): the gospel οὐκ ἔστιν κατὰ ἄνθρωπον, is not according to a human standard, neither received from a human being nor taught, but given through an apocalypse of Jesus Christ.

The narrative that follows is evidence for exactly that claim, item by item. His former life in Judaism, persecuting the church beyond measure (1:13–14): no one would have taught him this. The call, God's pleasure to reveal his Son in him (1:15–16): he did not consult flesh and blood, did not go up to Jerusalem to those who were apostles before him, but went to Arabia and back to Damascus. Three years later, fifteen days with Cephas and a glimpse of James (1:18–19), under oath (1:20). Then Syria and Cilicia, unknown by face to the churches of Judea, who only heard that the persecutor now preaches the faith he once tried to destroy (1:21–24). Fourteen years later, Jerusalem again, by revelation, laying the gospel before the acknowledged leaders κατ' ἰδίαν, privately (2:1–2): the word that will hand the argument to C.

↓ On the way down · B′ · 5:13–6:10

Sow to the Spirit

B′ begins where C′ left off, with freedom, and immediately guards it: you were called to freedom, only not freedom as an opening for the flesh; through love δουλεύετε ἀλλήλοις, be slaves to one another (5:13). The whole law is fulfilled in one word, love of neighbor (5:14). Then the sustained contrast: walk by the Spirit and you will not carry out the desire of the flesh (5:16); the two are set against each other (5:17); the works of the flesh are listed (5:19–21) against the fruit of the Spirit (5:22–23); those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh (5:24).

The practice follows. Restore the one caught in a trespass gently; bear one another's burdens and so fulfill τὸν νόμον τοῦ Χριστοῦ, the law of Christ (6:1–2); test your own work; share with the one who teaches (6:3–6). The harvest law closes the unit: what a person sows he will reap; sowing to the flesh reaps corruption, sowing to the Spirit reaps eternal life; do not tire of doing good; work the good toward all, especially the household of faith (6:7–10).

The slave word crosses the ring. In B Paul is Christ's slave and therefore no people-pleaser (1:10). In B′ the Galatians, freed, are to be slaves to one another through love (5:13). δοῦλος in 1:10 and δουλεύετε in 5:13 are the pair's own echo, one Wilson does not list, and together they reframe the letter's keyword: freedom in Galatians is freedom for a different service. Project Context observation.

1:12, 1:16 · ἀποκάλυψις — the source of the gospel

The gospel came δι' ἀποκαλύψεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ (1:12), and God's pleasure was ἀποκαλύψαι his Son in Paul (1:16). The same verb returns at the innermost ring, where faith is what was waiting ἀποκαλυφθῆναι, to be revealed (3:23). What was unveiled to Paul on the road is, in G, what was unveiled to everyone when faith came.

N. T. Wright draws out what the word does and does not tell us. It is the vocabulary of unveiling, something hidden in God's sphere made visible in the human one, and in Paul the word-group usually has eschatological force: what one would expect to see on the last day has been disclosed ahead of time (Romans 1:18 with 2:5; 1 Corinthians 1:7; 2 Thessalonians 1:7). Paul chose it to put his gospel beyond the reach of any chain of tradition the Galatians could appeal up, but it says nothing, Wright cautions, about the kind of experience it was. The genitive of 1:12 is a revelation whose content was Jesus, as 1:16 shows; and ἐν ἐμοί there can mean to Paul or through Paul. The purpose clause that follows, that he might proclaim him among the nations, makes "through" the main sense, which requires "to," and together they rule out a merely inward experience set against an outward seeing.

Wilson's list also pairs 1:11 ("not of human origin") with 1:16 ("not with flesh and blood") as a B→C hook; on his own outline both verses fall inside B, so this one stitches within the unit rather than across the seam.
⟡ Seam ↓ C′ → B′ "persecuted" (διώκομαι, 5:11) → "bite and devour" (δάκνετε καὶ κατεσθίετε, 5:15). Persecution from outside hands off to a church consuming itself from inside.

③ Movement 3 · C ⇄ C′ · 2:3–14 ⇄ 5:1–12 · The Circumcision Faction

Single theme Circumcision and the party that promoted it Word distribution: within the proof, only here

The second ring is Wilson's clearest case, and it is a matter of counting. Within the proof (1:10–6:10), the rite of circumcision and the party pushing it appear only in C and C′, six times and five by his count, and nowhere between them. The epilogue takes the word up again at 6:12–15, which is what an epilogue restating the contrast should do. This is the pair that addresses the "different gospel" of 1:6 head on: the legalistic faction whose demand inflames Paul and precipitates the letter. It is also a pair on a single theme rather than a contrast, the alternation at work.

↑ On the way up · C · 2:3–14

Titus, Jerusalem, Antioch

The Jerusalem meeting turns on one man: Titus, a Greek, was not compelled to be circumcised (2:3). The pressure came from false brothers smuggled in to spy out τὴν ἐλευθερίαν ἡμῶν, the freedom we have in Christ Jesus, so as to enslave us (2:4), and Paul did not yield for an hour, so that the truth of the gospel might remain for you (2:5). The acknowledged leaders added nothing (2:6); they recognized the gospel for the uncircumcised entrusted to Paul as the gospel for the circumcised had been to Peter (2:7–8), gave the right hand of fellowship, and asked only that the poor be remembered (2:9–10).

Then Antioch. Cephas ate with gentiles until people from James arrived, then drew back out of fear of the circumcision party; the other Jews joined the play-acting, and even Barnabas was carried off (2:11–13). Paul opposed him to his face, because they were not ὀρθοποδοῦσιν, walking straight toward the truth of the gospel (2:14): if you, a Jew, live like a gentile, how can you compel the gentiles to live like Jews? The question is left hanging over the gentile reader. On Wilson's outline it is the seam into D.

↓ On the way down · C′ · 5:1–12

For freedom set free

Τῇ ἐλευθερίᾳ ἡμᾶς Χριστὸς ἠλευθέρωσεν: for freedom Christ set us free; stand, then, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery (5:1; the same word, ζυγός, that Peter uses at the Jerusalem council for the law his fathers could not bear, Acts 15:10). The theme is explicit now and the stakes are stated flatly: if you accept circumcision Christ will be of no benefit to you (5:2); whoever is circumcised is obligated to the whole law (5:3); you are severed from Christ, fallen from grace (5:4). What counts instead is πίστις δι' ἀγάπης ἐνεργουμένη, faith working through love (5:6).

Then the agitators, never named. Who cut in on you to keep you from obeying the truth (5:7)? A little leaven leavens the whole lump (5:9). The one troubling you will bear the judgment (5:10). If I still preach circumcision, why am I still persecuted? Then the scandal of the cross would be abolished (5:11). And the harshest sentence Paul ever wrote: I wish those unsettling you would ἀποκόψονται, cut themselves off (5:12), a savage pun for a region that knew the self-mutilating devotees of the mother goddess.

Antioch and Galatia are one confrontation, seen from both ends. In C, a Jew withdraws from table fellowship under pressure from the circumcision party, and Paul calls it a failure to walk straight toward the truth of the gospel (2:14). In C′, gentiles are being pressed to add circumcision, and Paul calls it being cut in on, kept from obeying the truth (5:7). The phrase ἡ ἀλήθεια τοῦ εὐαγγελίου, the truth of the gospel, frames C at 2:5 and 2:14, and C′ makes that truth a thing to be obeyed or hindered. The pair makes Peter's lapse and the Galatians' temptation the same event: the gospel compromised by fear of the same faction.
⟡ Seam ↑ C → D Jew … gentiles (Ἰουδαῖος … ἐθνικῶς … τὰ ἔθνη, 2:14) → Jews … gentiles (Ἰουδαῖοι … ἐξ ἐθνῶν, 2:15). The question Paul puts to Cephas becomes the premise of the argument he puts to the Galatians.
⟡ Seam ↓ D′ → C′ "the free woman" (τῆς ἐλευθέρας, 4:31) → "freedom" (τῇ ἐλευθερίᾳ, 5:1). The allegory's last word is the exhortation's first.

④ Movement 4 · D ⇄ D′ · 2:15–21 ⇄ 4:21–31 · Two Groups, Twice

Oppositional contrast Justified by faith vs by works ⇄ born of flesh vs born of promise The reversal: Wilson's reading

The circumcision dispute leads, as Wilson puts it, to its larger context: the problem of the law. D turns the argument toward the letter's central theological point, the deficiency of justification by law over against justification by faith, and does so by naming two groups. D′ names two groups again, through the allegory of the two sons, and springs what Wilson calls a stunning reversal of expectations. The children of promise are not the Jews by right, because they are enslaved to the law; the gentiles are the true children of Sarah and of the Jerusalem above (4:26).

↑ On the way up · D · 2:15–21

Jews by nature, gentile sinners

Ἡμεῖς φύσει Ἰουδαῖοι καὶ οὐκ ἐξ ἐθνῶν ἁμαρτωλοί, we, Jews by nature and not gentile sinners (2:15): the two groups as the circumcision party sees them. Then the sentence both groups share: a person is not justified ἐξ ἔργων νόμου, by works of the law, but through faith of Jesus Christ; and we believed in Christ Jesus in order to be justified by faith and not by works of law, because by works of law no flesh will be justified (2:16, with Psalm 143:2 underneath). The objection (2:17) is answered by Paul's own case: through the law I died to the law, so that I might live to God; Χριστῷ συνεσταύρωμαι, I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me (2:19–20). The last line is the unit's verdict: if righteousness came through the law, then Christ died for nothing (2:21).

πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ (2:16) can be read as faith in Christ or as the faithfulness of Christ. The debate does not change the pair's shape, since either way the term it contrasts with is works of law.

↓ On the way down · D′ · 4:21–31

Children of the slave, children of the free

Tell me, you who want to be under law, do you not hear the law (4:21)? Abraham had two sons, one from the slave woman, one from the free; one born κατὰ σάρκα, according to the flesh, the other δι' ἐπαγγελίας, through promise (4:22–23). Paul reads the story as allegory, ἀλληγορούμενα (4:24): two covenants, Hagar as Sinai, bearing children for slavery, corresponding to the present Jerusalem; but the Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother (4:26). Isaiah's barren woman sings (4:27, Isaiah 54:1). You, brothers, are children of promise like Isaac (4:28); then as now the one born by the flesh persecuted the one born by the Spirit (4:29); and Scripture's verdict is Sarah's: cast out the slave woman and her son, for the slave's son will not inherit with the free woman's (4:30, Genesis 21:10). We are not children of a slave but of the free woman (4:31).

The Genesis side of the allegory, with the traditions that heard hostility in Ishmael's "playing," is on the site's Hagar and Sarah profiles; the mapping itself is diagrammed on the Literary Design page, and the passage has a verse-by-verse study of its own.

The labels have changed sides. D sorts the world as the circumcision party does, Jews by nature and gentile sinners, and then denies that the sorting justifies anyone. D′ sorts it again, children of the slave and children of the free, and the gentile believers are on the free side while the law-bound are on the slave side. Reading the two units together is what makes the reversal land: the categories are kept and the occupants are exchanged.

Allegory, not erasure

Paul's ἀλληγορούμενα does not deny that Hagar and Sarah existed. It reads their story as a pattern with a present-tense referent, the way the prophets read the exodus. The sharpness of D′ is in the mapping, Sinai on the slave side, not in any doubt about the history, and the mapping is what the argument needs: it answers the circumcision party on the ground of Genesis, which they would have claimed as their own.

⟡ Seam ↑ D → E "works of the law" (ἐξ ἔργων νόμου, 2:16) → "works of the law" (ἐξ ἔργων νόμου, 3:2). The verdict of the narrative becomes the first question of the argument.
⟡ Seam ↓ E′ → D′ "my little children" (τέκνα μου, 4:19) → "two sons" (δύο υἱούς, 4:22). Paul's children in labor hand off to Abraham's children by two mothers.

⑤ Movement 5 · E ⇄ E′ · 3:1–5 ⇄ 4:12–20 · Before Your Eyes

Single theme Eyes · Paul's presence among them ὀφθαλμοί only at 3:1 and 4:15

Eyes are mentioned twice in Galatians, at 3:1 and 4:15, and both are here. Wilson's observation is that these two units step aside from the theological and scriptural argument on either side of them (D–E–F on the way up, F′–E′–D′ on the way down) to summon the Galatians' own memory of Paul's presence among them. E begins with a strident condemnation and E′ ends with an outpouring of compassion. Between them runs the same appeal: remember what you saw.

↑ On the way up · E · 3:1–5

Foolish Galatians

Ὦ ἀνόητοι Γαλάται, O foolish Galatians, who put the evil eye on you, you before whose eyes Jesus Christ προεγράφη ἐσταυρωμένος, was placarded as crucified (3:1)? The verb is the word for a public notice posted for all to read; the crucified Christ had been posted in Galatia. Wilson takes this as Paul's proclamation of Christ crucified both in the gospel he delivered and in his personal conduct, a reading E′ will confirm.

Five questions follow with one answer (3:2–5). Did you receive the Spirit by works of law or by ἀκοῆς πίστεως, the hearing of faith? Having begun with the Spirit, are you now finishing with the flesh? Did you suffer so much for nothing? Does the one who supplies the Spirit and works miracles among you do so by works of law or by the hearing of faith? The unit argues from the Galatians' own experience and from nothing else.

↓ On the way down · E′ · 4:12–20

My little children

Γίνεσθε ὡς ἐγώ, ὅτι κἀγὼ ὡς ὑμεῖς: become as I am, because I also have become as you are (4:12), the opening Wilson reads as confirming that Paul's life, not only his preaching, was the placard. Then memory. He first preached to them because of a weakness of the flesh, and though his condition was a trial to them they did not scorn or spit but received him as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus (4:13–14). Where is that blessedness now? You would have torn out your eyes and given them to me (4:15). Have I become your enemy by telling you the truth (4:16)? The agitators court you to shut you out, so that you will court them (4:17).

Then the tone changes in a single verse: τέκνα μου, my little children, for whom I am again in labor until Christ is formed in you; I wish I could be present with you now and change my tone, because I am at a loss about you (4:19–20).

The pastor's heart, in Wilson's phrase. E opens with ἀνόητοι Γαλάται and E′ closes with τέκνα μου; the ring turns rebuke into labor pains. Both are the same appeal to the same memory, but the second has stopped arguing and started pleading, which is also what happens, on a larger scale, as the letter descends from the apex. They received him ὡς Χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν (4:14); what they had seen in 3:1 was Christ crucified. The two units agree about what the Galatians were looking at.
⟡ Seam ↑ E → F "the hearing of faith" (ἐξ ἀκοῆς πίστεως, 3:5) → "Abraham believed" (ἐπίστευσεν, 3:6). The Galatians' believing is matched at once by the patriarch's.
⟡ Seam ↓ F′ → E′ "turning back … enslaved again" (ἐπιστρέφετε πάλιν … δουλεύειν, 4:9) → "become as I am" (γίνεσθε ὡς ἐγώ, 4:12). Wilson's hook of imitated character: the becoming they are drifting toward, set against the becoming he asks for.

⑥ Movement 6 · F ⇄ F′ · 3:6–18 ⇄ 4:1–11 · Blessing and Curse, Slavery and Adoption

Oppositional contrast Blessing through Abraham vs curse of the law ⇄ enslaved to the elements vs redeemed by God Abraham ×7 in F ἐξαγοράζω at 3:13 and 4:5

The ring returns to argument by contrast: blessing against curse in F, enslaved against redeemed in F′. Wilson's point about F is that Paul argues for the preeminence of faith even inside the domain of the law by enlisting, in Wilson's words, "the founder and father of the people of God," the figure the law's partisans claimed as their own. Abraham is named seven times in the unit, as the one who led the response to God in faith (3:6) and as the father of many nations, gentiles among them (3:7–8), which Genesis 17:5 ties to his name (Wilson: "hence his name, Abraham"). Legitimate descent from Abraham becomes, for Paul, a matter of faith rather than biology, and F′ revisits the theme as adoption into God's family.

↑ On the way up · F · 3:6–18

Blessing through Abraham, curse of the law

Just as Abraham believed God and it was counted to him as righteousness (3:6, Genesis 15:6), so those of faith are Abraham's sons (3:7). Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the gentiles by faith, προευηγγελίσατο, preached the gospel beforehand, to Abraham: in you all the nations will be blessed (3:8). The contrast then runs on the law's own words. Those of the works of the law are under a curse, Deuteronomy 27:26 (3:10); the righteous one will live by faith, Habakkuk 2:4 (3:11); Christ ἐξηγόρασεν, redeemed us, from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us, Deuteronomy 21:23 (3:13), so that Abraham's blessing might come to the gentiles in Christ Jesus and we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith (3:14).

The last paragraph argues from timing and grammar. A ratified covenant is not annulled by a law that came 430 years later (3:15–17); the promise was to Abraham's seed, singular, ὅς ἐστιν Χριστός, who is Christ (3:16); and if the inheritance is from law it is no longer from promise (3:18).

The covenant "ratified beforehand by God" (3:17; the "ratified covenant" of 3:15 is the human analogy) is the promise to Abraham, whose scene most readers take to be Genesis 15, which God alone walked through; the site's Abraham profile works through the ceremony and Paul's use of it.

↓ On the way down · F′ · 4:1–11

Enslaved to the elements, adopted by God

The heir, while a minor, is no different from a slave though he owns everything (4:1–2). So we, as minors, were enslaved under τὰ στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου, the elements of the world (4:3). But when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under law, ἵνα τοὺς ὑπὸ νόμον ἐξαγοράσῃ, to redeem those under law, so that we might receive υἱοθεσία, adoption as sons (4:4–5); and because you are sons God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts crying Abba, Father (4:6). No longer a slave but a son, and if a son, an heir through God (4:7).

Then the turn. Formerly, not knowing God, you were enslaved to things that are not gods; now that you know God, or rather are known by God, how can you turn back to the weak and beggarly elements and want to be enslaved to them again (4:8–9)? You observe days and months and seasons and years (4:10). I am afraid I have labored over you in vain (4:11).

The same verb carries both halves. Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law in 3:13 (ἐξηγόρασεν) and was sent to redeem those under law in 4:5 (ἐξαγοράσῃ). ἐξαγοράζω occurs in Galatians only in these two units. The curse of F and the slavery of F′ are two names for the condition the one purchase ends, and the promise of the Spirit (3:14) is the Spirit of the Son (4:6). Project Context observation; the hook is not in Wilson's list.

4:3, 4:9 · στοιχεῖα — the elements

The word means the basic things: the letters of the alphabet, the physical elements, or, in a reading many commentators prefer here, the elemental powers. Whatever its exact reference, the unit's sharpest move is to put the Galatians' calendar-keeping under the law (4:10) on the same side of the ledger as their former paganism (4:8). To go under the law now, Paul says, is to go back, not forward.

⟡ Seam ↑ F → G "offspring" (σπέρμα, 3:16) → "the offspring" (τὸ σπέρμα, 3:19). The seed to whom the promise was made is the seed until whom the law was added.
⟡ Seam ↓ G′ → F′ "offspring … heirs" (σπέρμα … κληρονόμοι, 3:29) → "the heir" (ὁ κληρονόμος, 4:1). Heirs according to promise become the heir who is still a minor.

⑦ Movement 7 · G ⇄ G′ · 3:19–23 ⇄ 3:25–29 · Why the Law, and What Faith Does

Single theme The rationale of the law ⇄ the result of faith Innermost ring (Wilson)

Wilson sets up the innermost ring with a warning he gives on Paul's behalf: unless one critical theme is addressed, Paul is left with a withered argument. He cannot bypass the place of the Torah in the tradition of the elders without subverting his own religious inheritance, so he must answer two questions. What was the law for, and how does faith displace it? G answers the first; G′ answers the second; the apex between them states the purpose that joins them. The ring is time-stamped on both sides: G speaks of "until" and "before faith came," G′ of "now that faith has come."

↑ On the way up · G · 3:19–23

The justification of the law

Τί οὖν ὁ νόμος; Why then the law? It was added because of transgressions, until the seed should come to whom the promise was made, ordered through angels by the hand of a mediator (3:19); and a mediator implies more than one party, but God is one (3:20). Is the law then against the promises? Never (3:21): if a law had been given that could make alive, righteousness would indeed have been by law. Instead Scripture shut everything up under sin, so that the promise, by faith of Jesus Christ, might be given to those who believe (3:22). Before faith came we were held in custody under law, confined until the coming faith should be revealed (3:23).

Wilson's label for the unit is exact: the justification of the law. Not justification by it, but the case for why it existed at all, as an interim guardian.

↓ On the way down · G′ · 3:25–29

The justification of faith

Ἐλθούσης δὲ τῆς πίστεως, but now that faith has come, we are no longer under a guardian (3:25); for you are all sons of God through faith in Christ Jesus (3:26). As many as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ (3:27). There is neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor free, no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus (3:28). And if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's seed, heirs according to promise (3:29).

The unit is the justification of faith in the same sense: what faith, having arrived, accomplishes. It unites in Christ, and the three pairs it dissolves are the very boundaries the circumcision faction's gospel would have kept in force.

The guardian word. παιδαγωγός (3:24–25) is not the teacher but the household slave who walked the child to school and kept him in line until he came of age: custody with a built-in end date. G describes the custody; G′ describes the coming of age; and the "neither slave nor free" of 3:28 lands in a unit that has just declared the custodian off duty.

Wilson's own discussion of this ring falls on a page of the chapter not consulted for this study (p. 263). The reading above follows his outline labels and the text; it will be checked against his discussion when that page is in hand.

⟡ Seam ↑ G → H "the coming faith" (τὴν μέλλουσαν πίστιν, 3:23) → "by faith" (ἐκ πίστεως, 3:24).
⟡ Seam ↓ H → G′ "by faith" (ἐκ πίστεως, 3:24) → "faith having come" (ἐλθούσης τῆς πίστεως, 3:25). Project Context addition: the hook into the apex is in Wilson's list; the hook out of it is not.

⑧ The Apex · H · 3:24 · Justification by Faith

Center Single-verse center (Wilson) Densest faith-language in the letter

Wilson's ring rises to one verse and turns on it. The concentric arrangement, he says, leads incrementally to the theological and conventional heart of the letter, justification by faith, the heart of Paul's gospel; and the heart is where the two sides of the letter's tension meet. On one side are those who would bind the emerging faith to Judaic law, above all through circumcision. On the other is Paul, who insists that Christ has freed people of faith from the law's constraints.

The ring's center · H
ὥστε ὁ νόμος παιδαγωγὸς ἡμῶν γέγονεν εἰς Χριστόν, ἵνα ἐκ πίστεως δικαιωθῶμεν.So the law became our guardian until Christ, in order that we might be justified by faith.
Galatians 3:24
What the law was · G's answer, restated παιδαγωγὸς εἰς Χριστόν a guardian until Christ
What it was for · G′'s ground ἵνα ἐκ πίστεως δικαιωθῶμεν so that we might be justified by faith

The verse is a hinge the text itself marks. G ends with faith as something still to be revealed (3:23); G′ opens with faith having come (3:25); 3:24 stands between the future and the past of the same word and supplies the purpose clause that governs both. It is also the densest run of faith-language in the letter, seven occurrences of πίστις or πιστεύω in the five verses 3:22–26, which is the kind of concentration an oral audience hears before a reader sees it.

A center, proposed

Wilson boxes 3:24 as the apex and this page follows him, but a center is a reading, not a datum. Bligh's concentric Galatians, which Wilson acknowledges as a forerunner, arrives at different divisions, and most commentaries treat 2:15–21 as the letter's thesis statement rather than 3:24 as its summit. The two are not rivals. D states the thesis on the ascent, and H is where the proof reaches it from the law's own side, having answered the law's own question. Readers who prefer 2:16 as the letter's center should notice where Wilson's ring places it: exactly opposite the allegory of the two covenants, so that the thesis and its most provocative application mirror each other.

🧵 Style · The Seams Are Stitched

Third canon · diction and figures of speech

Arrangement is the large picture, how the parts of the argument are ordered to persuade with most effect; style, in the classical scheme, works at the level of diction and figures of speech. Wilson's example from Galatians is Paul's use of repeated words and phrases. Some repetition clusters naturally inside a paragraph. But Paul also stitches the separate units of the proof together, Wilson compares it to the way John hooks his doublets, with key words that recur across a boundary: the last thing said in one unit becomes the first thing said in the next. The hooks create continuity across the seams and make the proof flow instead of lurching. They are also, as the next section argues, how a hearer kept their place.

🧠 Memory · Built to Be Remembered

InventionArrangementStyleMemoryDelivery

Fourth canon · commitment of the work to memory

For a modern writer, committing a work to memory is an afterthought with no bearing on how it is composed. For Paul, Wilson argues, the constraints of oral performance shaped the final pattern of the work even where he was not conscious of them; and the patterning conventions were so innate to ancient authors and so naturally expected by their audiences that the rhetoricians did not bother to describe them. Concentric forms aid memory. They pair statements across a center like partners in a dialogue, "a sort of delayed antiphon around a central point," and when the pairing is joined to a progressive development, the climb that the arrangement traces, the form is both compelling and rewarding to hold.

The two halves of Galatians move differently, and the difference is part of the mnemonic design. The ascent builds inductively, from Paul's personal recollections of his call and ministry outward toward the universal offer of faith in Christ; the descent moves deductively, from that universal offer back down to the particular case. Wilson draws the ascent as a set of widening circles of social reach: Paul's personal call; the specific problem of the Galatian churches; the circumcision faction, whose influence presumably reached emerging Christians well beyond Galatia; the justification in the law that encompassed all of Judaism; and finally the universal faith offered in Christ, which excludes no Jew and inhibits no gentile.

Wilson's closing caution belongs on a site like this one. Modern readers, attuned to outlines and the demarcations of the printed page, perceive the flow of an ancient work only with a disciplined attention the first hearers had by instinct; but the mnemonic clues of an indigenous orality remain in the text, waiting to be noticed. The Structured Edition is Project Context's attempt to put them back on the page.

A letter built to be remembered rewards being read the way it was built.

🎙️ Delivery · The Narrative as Performance

InventionArrangementStyleMemoryDelivery

Fifth canon · oral presentation

The epilogue, Wilson notes, restates the contrast between Paul and his opponents; the letter ends as it was delivered, aloud, with the two sides set against each other one last time. Delivery as a canon concerns the oral presentation of the speech, and it is under this heading that Wilson takes up the question that has dogged Galatians 1–2 for a century: how many times, and when, did Paul go to Jerusalem, given that the data in Galatians 1–2 and in Acts 9 and 15 appear to conflict? What, in short, is the historical value of the narrative? Wilson declines to resolve the chronology. What he offers instead is a different way of viewing the evidence, drawn from Hall's second article (1991).

The classical rhetoricians, Hall showed, are explicit about what a narration is for. Philosophy sets out to demonstrate what is true and religion to reveal, but rhetoric has a single goal, to persuade; and when narrative is placed in the service of rhetoric, its first intention is not to instruct, to communicate facts objectively, but to persuade. The handbooks even prescribe the order of composition: write the proofs first, then compose the narration so that it will best serve them. Quintilian asks of a narration that it be concise and plausible; he does not ask that it follow the historical order of events. In Hall's summary, as Wilson reports it, the ancient narrator ruled over the facts at his disposal, selecting, compressing, reordering, assigning motives and building suspense, and the handbooks allowed still more than that when the cause was judged noble.

Reading 1–2 as a diary
  • Expects completeness: every visit, in order.
  • Counts the trips to Jerusalem and lines them up against Acts 9, 11 and 15.
  • Treats "three years" and "fourteen years" as the point of the passage.
  • Ends either in a harmony or in a declared contradiction, and stops at the data.
Reading 1–2 as a narration
  • Asks of each item what it proves.
  • Notices that every detail is evidence for the proposition: no consultation with flesh and blood, no trip to Jerusalem at first, only fifteen days, "privately," "not even Titus," the oath of 1:20.
  • Reads omissions as selection, and selection as argument.
  • Expects a narrative shaped after the case it serves, because that is how narrations were written.

What this does and does not claim

It does not claim that Paul invented anything. Paul swears before God that he is not lying (1:20), and nothing in the conventions of narration required him to. It does not settle the visits question; Wilson explicitly leaves it open. What it changes is the question to ask first of Galatians 1–2: not "does this match Acts item for item?" but "what is this narrative built to prove?" Luke selects too; Acts gives Paul's visits as Luke's story needs them. Selection is not deception on either side.

On this site's reading, that leaves Paul's truthfulness untouched and his craft in view. The narrative of 1:10–2:21 was heard in one sitting by a congregation that had just heard the proposition of 1:6–9, and every sentence of it was chosen to make that proposition stand. That is what a narration in the service of a deliberative speech is for.
