Where the passage sits

"Do You Not Hear the Law?"

Galatians 4:21–31 is eleven verses long and has drawn more commentary per verse than anything else in the letter. Paul takes the story the circumcision party would have claimed as their own, Abraham's two sons, and reads it so that the people pressing the law on gentiles turn out to be on the wrong side of it. He calls the reading allegory. It is also a very careful piece of exegesis, and this page takes it one layer at a time.

The hooks

Paul "in labor" (4:19) before the woman "not in labor" (4:27); "free woman" five times, "slave woman" five times; then "for freedom" (5:1).

Three things frame the passage. It follows Paul's most personal appeal (4:12–20), in which he calls the Galatians his little children and says he is in labor with them again; it is answered, on Wilson's ring, by the thesis of 2:15–21, where two groups were named and neither was justified by works of law; and it opens straight into "For freedom the Messiah has set us free" (5:1), which is not a new section but the allegory's conclusion. The Movement Commentary reads the pair; the Literary Design page diagrams it in brief; this page slows down.

The passage, annotated

Galatians 4:21–31

A literal-literary rendering from the Greek. Verses on Hagar's side of the argument are marked in rose, on Sarah's in teal; the notes point to the words the rest of the page turns on.

Galatians 4:21–31Project Context rendering
21
Tell me, you who want to be under law: do you not hear the law?
"Law" twice, in two senses: the regime they want to be under, and the Torah as Scripture read aloud (ἀκούετε). Genesis is "the law."
22
For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one from the slave woman and one from the free woman.
παιδίσκη and ἐλευθέρα: the women are never named until v. 24, and then only Hagar is. Sarah's name does not appear in the passage.
23
But the one from the slave woman was born according to the flesh, and the one from the free woman through promise.
κατὰ σάρκα / δι' ἐπαγγελίας. "Flesh" here is ordinary human capacity; "promise" is what only God's word could do with a ninety-year-old.
24
These things are spoken allegorically: for these women are two covenants, one from Mount Sinai, bearing children into slavery; she is Hagar.
ἀλληγορούμενα, the only time the word appears in the New Covenant. "Are" (εἰσιν) is the allegory's copula: the women stand for covenants.
25
Now Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia; and she stands in the same column as the present Jerusalem, for she is in slavery with her children.
συστοιχεῖ, "stands in the same file or column." The manuscripts disagree about this sentence; see below.
26
But the Jerusalem above is free; she is our mother.
The second column is never given a mountain. Its city is "above" (ἄνω), and "our" includes the gentile readers.
27
For it is written: Rejoice, barren one who does not bear; break forth and shout, you who are not in labor; for many are the children of the desolate one, more than of her who has the husband.
Isaiah 54:1, word for word from the Greek Bible. ἡ οὐκ ὠδίνουσα, "not in labor," answers Paul's ὠδίνω in 4:19.
28
And you, brothers, like Isaac, are children of promise.
"You" (the printed text) or "we" (many manuscripts): either way the gentile Galatians are placed on Isaac's side by name.
29
But just as then the one born according to the flesh persecuted the one born according to the Spirit, so also now.
"Promise" (v. 23) has become "Spirit": the promise's content is the Spirit (3:14). Genesis does not say "persecuted"; see Traditions.
30
But what does the Scripture say? Cast out the slave woman and her son; for the son of the slave woman shall by no means inherit with the son of the free woman.
Genesis 21:10, Sarah's words, with the ending changed: Genesis has "with my son Isaac"; Paul writes "with the son of the free woman."
31
Therefore, brothers, we are not children of a slave woman but of the free woman.
The conclusion, and the seam into 5:1: "For freedom the Messiah has set us free."
ἀλληγορούμενα
allēgoroumena · v. 24

"Spoken so as to mean something else." Philo used the family of words constantly; Paul uses it once. What he means by it is the question of the Method section.

συστοιχεῖ
systoichei · v. 25

"Stands in the same column." From στοῖχος, a row or file, the root also behind the "elements" of 4:3, 9 and "keep in step" in 5:25. Greek writers used the noun for a column of paired opposites, as Longenecker notes; Paul's allegory is literally two columns.

παιδίσκη
paidiskē · ×5

"Slave girl, female slave." The Greek Bible's word for Hagar in Genesis 16 and 21. Five times here, matched by five of ἐλευθέρα, "free woman": the passage is balanced to the word.

ἐλευθέρα
eleuthera · ×5

"Free woman." Paul lets the adjective do Sarah's work; her name never appears. In 5:1 the adjective becomes the noun ἐλευθερία, and the allegory becomes an exhortation.

ἐδίωκεν
ediōken · v. 29

"Was persecuting." The same verb Paul uses of his own former life (1:13, 23) and of the pressure the agitators are avoiding (5:11; 6:12). "Then as now" ties Ishmael to the present dispute.

ἔκβαλε
ekbale · v. 30

"Cast out." Sarah's imperative to Abraham, now Scripture's imperative to the Galatians. Whether it tells them to expel the agitators, or only states the verdict, is weighed below.

The story Paul reads

Two Sons in Genesis

Everything in the allegory rests on Genesis 16 and 21, with Genesis 17 and 18 between them. Paul assumes his hearers know the story well enough to catch what he leaves out. Here it is, with the facts the allegory will use in bold.

Genesis 15–25 · the two sons

  1. Genesis 15:6. Abram believes God's promise of offspring and is counted righteous: before any son, before circumcision, before Sinai. The chronology the whole letter argues from.
  2. Genesis 16:1–4. Sarai, barren, gives her Egyptian slave Hagar to Abram "that I may be built up through her." Hagar conceives. The son is Abraham's by ordinary means, and by Sarah's plan.
  3. Genesis 16:6–14. Afflicted by Sarai, Hagar flees toward Shur, on the road to Egypt. The angel of the LORD finds her at a spring, sends her back, and promises her a son, Ishmael, "God hears." She names God El Roi, the God who sees me. Scripture's first annunciation by the angel of the LORD is made to a slave woman in the wilderness.
  4. Genesis 17:9–27. The covenant of circumcision. Abraham, now ninety-nine, is circumcised, and so is Ishmael, aged thirteen, and every male of the household, the same day. Ishmael is the first circumcised son. God promises that the covenant will be established with Isaac, yet to be born (17:19–21), and blesses Ishmael (17:20).
  5. Genesis 18:9–15. Sarah, listening at the tent door, laughs at the promise of a son. "Is anything too hard for the LORD?"
  6. Genesis 21:1–7. Isaac is born "at the set time of which God had spoken"; Sarah: "God has made laughter for me." Isaac is circumcised on the eighth day. Both sons are circumcised; only one is the son of the promise (17:19, 21).
  7. Genesis 21:8–13. At the feast for Isaac's weaning Sarah sees Ishmael מְצַחֵק, "playing" or "laughing," a word that puns on Isaac's name (the Greek Bible adds "with Isaac her son"), and demands: "Cast out this slave woman with her son, for the son of this slave woman shall not inherit with my son Isaac." Abraham is grieved; God tells him to listen to her voice, "for through Isaac shall your seed be named," and promises to make a nation of Ishmael too, "because he is your seed."
  8. Genesis 21:14–21. Hagar and the boy wander in the wilderness of Beersheba; the water runs out; "God heard the voice of the boy"; God opens her eyes to a well; Ishmael grows up in the wilderness of Paran, and his mother takes him a wife from Egypt.
  9. Genesis 25:9, 12–18. Isaac and Ishmael bury their father together. Ishmael's twelve sons become princes of the tribes that range "from Havilah to Shur, opposite Egypt."
What Genesis says
  • Hagar is a slave, an Egyptian, and the mother of Abraham's firstborn.
  • Ishmael was born by ordinary means; Isaac by a promise that outran nature.
  • Ishmael was circumcised before Isaac was born.
  • Sarah demanded the expulsion; God endorsed it, and named the line through Isaac.
  • God saw and heard Hagar and Ishmael, twice, and blessed the boy.
What Genesis does not say
  • That Ishmael persecuted Isaac. It says he was "playing."
  • That Hagar has anything to do with Mount Sinai, which is four centuries away.
  • That the two mothers are covenants, or that Sarah is a city.
  • That Sarah's words settle who inherits in any later generation.
  • Anything about Jerusalem at all.

The fact beneath the allegory

Paul never says that Ishmael was circumcised, and he does not need to; the Galatians could read Genesis 17. But it is the fact that gives his reading its force. The agitators' whole case was that circumcision makes a gentile a son of Abraham. Genesis answers that Abraham's first circumcised son was precisely the one who did not inherit, and that the son who did inherit was marked out by a promise given before his circumcision and before his birth. Even Jubilees, the book that made circumcision an eternal ordinance, records that Ishmael was circumcised with the household (15:23–24) and then says that God did not choose him or his sons (15:30). Paul's allegory is the Genesis fact turned into a map.

The site's character profiles give the Genesis side in full: Hagar, with the two wilderness scenes and El Roi, and Sarah, with the laughter wordplay and "listen to her voice."

Paul's device

The Two Columns

Paul's own word for what he is doing is συστοιχεῖ: Hagar "stands in the same column" as the present Jerusalem. The verb pictures a table with two columns, the kind in which Greek thinkers lined up paired opposites, and it is the clearest guide to how the passage works. Everything in the story is sorted into one column or the other, and the argument is about which column two particular things belong in: the covenant of Sinai, and the city of Jerusalem.

Four of the seven rows keep the story's own contrast: mother, son, birth, standing. Paul moves two, the covenant and the city, and the "today" row follows them.

What stays and what moves

It matters that Paul leaves most of the table alone. Hagar is still the slave; Ishmael is still the son of the flesh; Isaac is still the child of promise; the slave's son still does not inherit. Paul is not denying Israel's story or inventing a new one. The whole weight of the passage falls on two moves. The covenant of Sinai, which the ordinary telling placed on Sarah's side as the covenant of her descendants, is placed on Hagar's, because it "bears children into slavery": it governs minors, as 3:23–4:3 argued, and the agitators are trying to put free adults back under it. And the city that stands for that covenant, "the present Jerusalem," the Jerusalem whose authority the agitators appear to have invoked, goes with it. Once those two rows move, the last row moves by itself: whoever stands under the Sinai covenant as the ground of belonging is, in the terms of the story, Hagar's child, whatever his ancestry.

The second column has no mountain

Hagar's column has a mountain, a city and a covenant named. Sarah's column has a covenant Paul does not name, a city "above," and no mountain. The asymmetry is deliberate. The covenant of promise was not given at a place; it was given to a man before he had a son, and it is the covenant "ratified beforehand by God" (3:17), whose scene most readers take to be Genesis 15, where God alone walked between the pieces. Its city is not yet visible. That is why the free column can hold the Galatians: there is no mountain they have to climb and no gate they have to pass.

The geography of 4:25

"Hagar Is Mount Sinai in Arabia"

Verse 25 is the hinge of the allegory and its most contested line. The manuscripts disagree about what it says, and commentators disagree about why Paul can say it.

Four readings in the manuscripts The reason for the link: disputed

What the manuscripts read
  • "Now Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia" (τὸ δὲ Ἁγὰρ Σινᾶ ὄρος): read by A, B and D among others, and printed in the Nestle-Aland text (NA28).
  • "Now Sinai is a mountain in Arabia" (τὸ δὲ Σινᾶ ὄρος): without Hagar's name, read by P46, the oldest papyrus of Paul, giving a plain geographical note.
  • "For Sinai is a mountain in Arabia" (τὸ γὰρ Σινᾶ ὄρος): also without the name, read by Sinaiticus and C among others.
  • "For Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia" (τὸ γὰρ Ἁγὰρ Σινᾶ ὄρος): the reading of the later majority, often explained as a blend of the others.
  • Either way the sentence places the Sinai covenant in Arabia, outside the land, and ties it to the present Jerusalem.
Why Hagar "is" Sinai: the proposals
  • Geography. Sinai lies in Arabia; Hagar was sent into the wilderness on the road to Egypt, and Ishmael settled in Paran, which the Torah's own poetry sets beside Sinai (Deuteronomy 33:2). Ishmael's descendants were the Arabs of the desert.
  • A pun. An Arabic word for rock sounds like Hagar's name, and may have been used of Sinai; several commentators, Longenecker among them, report the suggestion without resting on it.
  • The allegory's own logic. Paul has already said that Hagar is a covenant (v. 24); "Hagar is Sinai" only restates where that covenant came from.
  • A name in Israel's memory. The Tanakh knows an Arab people called the Hagrites (1 Chronicles 5:19–20; Psalm 83:6), so Hagar's name already belonged to the desert east and south of the land.

Hagar's two journeys run south and west into the wilderness; her son's home, Paran, lies beside Sinai; and Paul draws a line from that mountain back up to the present Jerusalem.

Why the geography works, whatever the pun

Sinai is outside the land. That is the point most of the proposals share, and it is enough for the argument. The covenant of the law was given in the wilderness, in the region to which Hagar was sent and in which her son's tribes lived, and it was given to a people who were not yet home. For Paul that is the law's character: it belongs to the time before the inheritance, the guardian's custody, the minor's household (3:23–4:3). "In Arabia" is a map of the argument. The land, and the city within it, belong to the promise, and the present Jerusalem, by standing on Sinai, has placed itself in the wilderness. The geographic reading: plausible and sufficient

The prophet Paul hears

Why Isaiah 54:1 Is About Sarah

The one quotation in the passage that is not from Genesis looks, at first, like a change of subject. Isaiah 54:1 is a song to Zion, the barren wife restored after exile. Paul quotes it to prove that the Jerusalem above is "our mother." Richard Hays showed how the two are joined, and the join runs through Sarah.

Genesis 11:30

"Sarai was barren; she had no child." Nearly the first thing Scripture says about her, a verse after her name (11:29), and the problem the whole Abraham story is built around.

Genesis 21:1–7

The barren woman bears, at ninety, "at the set time"; laughter and a feast. The impossible birth becomes Scripture's pattern for God's way of making a people.

Isaiah 51:1–3

"Look to Abraham your father and to Sarah who bore you; for he was but one when I called him." The exiles are told that their own restoration will follow Sarah's pattern: one, barren, then many. Zion's wilderness will become "like Eden."

Isaiah 54:1

"Rejoice, barren one who does not bear … for the children of the desolate one are more than the children of her who has the husband." Three chapters later the prophet addresses Zion as the barren woman, Sarah's figure: bereft now, and about to have more children than she can house (54:2–3).

Galatians 4:26–27

"The Jerusalem above is free; she is our mother; for it is written …" Paul takes the prophet at his word. The barren mother is Sarah; Sarah is Zion; Zion's promised children, more than the married woman's, are the gentile churches that the agitators would exclude.

What the citation gives Paul
  • A prophetic warrant for calling Sarah a city, which Genesis never does.
  • A mother whose children come by promise, not by the normal course of nature or of nations.
  • Numbers: "more than" the woman with the husband, which fits a mission in which the family of promise was growing among the nations.
  • Its place in Isaiah: the song of the barren woman follows the Servant's death and vindication (Isaiah 53). The children come after the cross.
The word that ties it to 4:19
  • Isaiah's woman is "not in labor," οὐκ ὠδίνουσα.
  • Eight verses earlier Paul had said of himself, "I am again in labor," ὠδίνω, "until the Messiah is formed in you" (4:19).
  • Susan Eastman reads the whole chapter through this maternal language: the apostle in labor, the barren one who has children without labor, "our mother." Paul's pain is the sign that the free mother's children are being born.

Zion as mother was already in the Psalms

Paul's move is less strange than it looks. Psalm 87 already imagines Zion as a mother on whose register foreigners are entered: Egypt, Babylon, Philistia, Tyre and Cush, and of each one "this one was born there" (Psalm 87:4–6). The Jerusalem above with gentile children is the psalm's picture, read with Isaiah's barren woman, and it is why Paul can say "our mother" to Galatians who had never seen Jerusalem. The heavenly Jerusalem of Jewish apocalyptic writing (Longenecker gathers the texts) and of Hebrews 12:22 and Revelation 21:2 is the same hope seen from above.

What Paul may assume his readers know

The Readings Behind the Reading

Paul was not the first to read Hagar and Sarah as more than two women, and the allegory lands harder when the traditions he is turning are in view. The site's Second Temple series treats a detail like the "persecuted" of 4:29 as a recognition marker: Scripture retold with something added is the surest sign that a tradition is at work beneath the text. Four of those traditions can be read on this site's own shelf.

Philo · two stages of one education

In On Mating with the Preliminary Studies Philo reads Hagar as the encyclical studies, the preparatory learning one must pass through, and Sarah as virtue and wisdom, the goal. His Hagar is a stage on the road to Sarah, and one leaves her behind without shame. Paul's two women are not stages but columns: nobody passes from Hagar's to Sarah's by progress. Philo also notes that Hagar's name means "sojourning."

Jubilees · the sign that does not choose

Jubilees 15 makes circumcision an eternal ordinance and declares the uncircumcised "children of destruction." It is the conviction the agitators carried. But the same chapter records that Ishmael was circumcised with the household (15:23–24) and then says that God did not choose him or his sons, "because he knew them" (15:30). Paul agrees with Jubilees on the fact and draws the opposite moral: if the sign did not choose Ishmael, it does not choose anyone.

Jubilees 17 and the rabbis · "playing"

Jubilees 17:4 still has Ishmael "playing and dancing" while Sarah grows jealous; the participle מְצַחֵק is Isaac's own name as a verb, Ishmael playing the heir. The harsher readings, mockery, idolatry, an arrow loosed in play, belong to the rabbinic traditions Longenecker gathers (Genesis Rabbah 53:11 among them). Paul's "persecuted" stands in that line and may assume the Galatians had heard it.

Josephus · the threat and the age

Josephus says Sarah would not have Ishmael raised with Isaac, "as being too old for him, and able to do him injuries when their father should be dead" (Antiquities 1.215). He also records that the Arabs circumcise at thirteen "because Ishmael, the founder of their nation," was circumcised at that age (1.214). A first-century Jew, then, knew Ishmael as a circumcised threat to the heir, and as the father of Arabia: both halves of Paul's Hagar column.

Did the agitators use the story first?

A long-standing proposal, C. K. Barrett's as Longenecker reports it, is that the Abraham material in Galatians 3–4 was the agitators' before it was Paul's: they taught the Galatians that Isaac's line is the circumcised line, and that an uncircumcised gentile is Ishmael. On that reading Paul's "Tell me, you who want to be under law, do you not hear the law?" is sarcasm aimed at people who had been quoting Genesis at the Galatians, and the allegory takes their text and inverts it. The proposal fits the passage's tone, but John Barclay's caution about reading opponents out of a polemical letter applies: we cannot be sure. What seems likely is that few in Galatia would have been surprised to hear Hagar and Sarah discussed. They would have been surprised by where Paul put Sinai. Agitators' prior use: plausible, unproved

What the passage does

The Reversal, and What It Answers

Wilson calls the allegory "a stunning reversal of expectations," and his ring explains why it comes where it does. D (2:15–21) named two groups, "we who are Jews by nature" and "gentile sinners," and said that neither is justified by works of law. D′ names two groups again, children of the slave and children of the free, and places the gentile believers among the free. The categories are kept; the occupants are exchanged.

What the agitators were saying
  • Isaac's line is Israel, marked by the sign God gave Abraham.
  • A gentile who believes in the Messiah is welcome, as Ishmael was blessed, but he is not an heir until he takes the sign.
  • The law given at Sinai is the covenant of the free people, and Jerusalem is its home.
  • Paul left the job half done.
What the allegory answers
  • Circumcision alone never singled out Isaac's line; Ishmael was circumcised too. What marked Isaac out was the promise.
  • Gentiles who believe are "children of promise, like Isaac," already; the sign would move them to the other column.
  • The Sinai covenant, read as the ground of belonging, bears slaves, because it was the guardian of minors; the present Jerusalem that insists on it stands with Hagar.
  • The heir's family is the free woman's, "and she is our mother."

How the passage is built

The site's study of Paul's Second Temple mechanics lays the passage out as a rhetorical sequence, and the sequence is worth seeing on its own: each step gives the hearer something he already agrees with before the next step moves it.

  1. The hook (v. 21). "You who want to be under law, do you not hear the law?" The appeal is to the agitators' own authority.
  2. Common ground (vv. 22–23). Two sons, two mothers, flesh and promise: the story as it was commonly told. Nothing yet to object to.
  3. The signal (v. 24a). "These things are spoken allegorically": the hearer is told that a second reading is coming, in a mode the synagogue knew.
  4. The flip (v. 24b). "One covenant is from Mount Sinai, bearing children into slavery; she is Hagar." The covenant moves columns.
  5. The clincher (v. 25). "Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia, and stands in the same column as the present Jerusalem." The city moves with it.
  6. The application (vv. 26–28). "The Jerusalem above is free; she is our mother … you, brothers, like Isaac, are children of promise." Isaiah is the warrant.
  7. The present (v. 29). "Then … so also now." The old story is happening in Galatia.
  8. Scripture's verdict (vv. 30–31). "Cast out the slave woman and her son." The agitators' own book delivers the judgment, and 5:1 draws the conclusion.

Three objections, and how the passage handles them

"But the law is ours"

Paul does not say it is not. He says what it is for, and when. Hagar's column is not "the Jews" but "those who want to be under law" (4:21), Jew or gentile, as the ground of standing. Jewish believers who eat with gentiles, as Peter did before the people from James came, are in Sarah's column.

"But Jerusalem is the mother city"

"The present Jerusalem" is, as most commentators read it, the city as the agitators invoked it: the source of their authority and the seat of the pressure Cephas felt at Antioch (2:12). The Jerusalem above is the same city's future, Isaiah's and the psalmist's, with the nations on her register. Paul is not setting a Greek heaven against a Jewish city; he is setting Isaiah's Zion against a claim made in her name.

"But Ishmael was blessed"

He was, and Genesis says so twice (17:20; 21:13, 18). The allegory is not about Ishmael's worth or Hagar's; it is about inheritance, and Genesis is clear that the inheritance went one way. Paul's "cast out" quotes the verdict on the inheritance, not on the people, and the next section asks what that means in practice.

How Paul reads

Allegory, Typology, and Paul's Way with Scripture

Paul says the things "are spoken allegorically," and the word has made commentators nervous for centuries, because allegory in Philo's hands dissolves the story into ideas. Paul's does not. The question is what he means by the word.

Allegory as Philo practiced it
  • The narrative is a surface; the real meaning is a timeless truth about the soul.
  • Hagar and Sarah become faculties or stages; the births, the journeys, the wells are read off as mental events.
  • History is not denied, but it is not where the meaning lives.
Allegory as Paul practices it here
  • The narrative is a pattern with a present referent: "then … so also now" (4:29).
  • The women become covenants and cities, which are historical realities, not ideas; the sons become peoples now living.
  • The reading is anchored in a prophet (Isaiah 54) who had already read Sarah as Zion.
  • Hays calls this an ecclesiocentric reading: Paul reads Israel's Scripture as a story about the community the Messiah has made.

What makes the reading honest

Three constraints keep the allegory from being arbitrary, and they are the reasons this page treats it as exegesis rather than homiletics. First, the sorting follows the text: the one thing Paul asserts about the sons that Genesis does not is the hostility of v. 29, and that he appears to have taken from a tradition his readers may have shared. Second, the two moved rows are argued elsewhere in the letter on other grounds: that the law "bears children into slavery" is the conclusion of 3:19–4:11, and the allegory restates it as a picture. Third, the prophet supplies the bridge from woman to city, so the "Jerusalem above" is Isaiah's and the Psalter's before it is Paul's. Longenecker's judgment is a fair one: the form is allegory, the substance is typology, and the control is the rest of the letter. N. T. Wright adds the frame: the single family promised to Abraham has arrived, and the question of 4:21–31 is only which mother it has.

The key alteration, in Paul's hand

Genesis 21:10 ends "with my son Isaac." Paul's quotation ends "with the son of the free woman." His wording differs from the Greek Genesis in smaller ways too (it drops "this" before "slave woman," twice, and strengthens the "not"), but this is the change that carries the argument, and it shows how he reads: Sarah's sentence about her own household becomes a sentence about two kinds of children, and the free woman's son can then be anyone born of promise. Longenecker notes the change; it is the allegory's method in miniature. The altered quotation

The wider pattern

The allegory is one instance of a method that can be watched across Paul's letters, and the site's study of his Second Temple mechanics names it: accept the opponents' framework and their traditions, keep their method, and reassign the referents through the Messiah. Galatians itself shows the move three more times. The angels who gave the law at Sinai, a mark of the law's glory in Jewish memory, become in 3:19–20 a mark of its distance from the one God who spoke the promise directly. The στοιχεῖα, the powers under which the nations lived, become the custodians whose term has expired (4:3, 9). And Abraham's "seed," the nation's collective name, becomes a single person who then includes the nation (3:16, 29). In each case the agitators could not reject the premise without rejecting their own tradition, and could not keep the premise without losing the conclusion. The series' overview page states the pattern in four steps, assumed, appropriated, transformed, applied, and the allegory runs through all four: the two-sons story is assumed known, its allegorical reading is appropriated from the synagogue, its referents are transformed through the Messiah, and the result is applied to churches deciding about circumcision. The Theology page takes the angels and the elements further.

"So also now" · 4:29–31

Persecution, Expulsion, and Hagar's Honor

The allegory ends with two hard sentences: the one born of the flesh persecutes the one born of the Spirit, then as now; and Scripture says, cast out the slave woman and her son. What are the Galatians being told to do, and what are they not?

"Persecuted … so also now"
  • The letter names the persecution it means: Paul's own, once, of the church (1:13, 23); the pressure the agitators avoid by circumcising gentiles (6:12); the "offense of the cross" that a circumcised church would escape (5:11).
  • Acts narrates it in these cities: the synagogue's jealousy, the leading citizens, a stoning (Acts 13:50; 14:19).
  • The claim is that the law-party's zeal, however sincere, was functioning as Ishmael's did: pressure on the child of promise.
"Cast out the slave woman and her son"
  • Minimally, the verdict: the slave's son does not inherit, so a law-based standing is no standing.
  • Possibly an instruction: expel the agitators from the churches, as Paul's anathema (1:8–9) and his wish in 5:12 suggest he would welcome. Longenecker and Witherington weigh the possibility; the text does not say it outright.
  • Never a license: the remedy Paul actually prescribes for the Galatians is the Spirit, gentleness toward a wrongdoer (6:1), and bearing burdens (6:2).

Hagar's honor, kept

Paul's Hagar is a column heading. Genesis's Hagar is a person: the first to have a birth announced to her by the angel of the LORD, the one who named God El Roi, the mother God heard in the wilderness and whose son he blessed. The allegory does not take any of that back; it borrows her status as a slave to say something about a covenant, and the thing it says is argued from the status, not from her. Reading the two together is the honest course, and the site's Hagar profile holds them together: her honored place in salvation history, and her symbolic function in Paul's law-and-promise distinction.

And Jews now?

The passage has been read as a verdict on Judaism, and it is worth saying what it is and is not. Paul's columns sort by the ground of standing, not by ancestry: "those who want to be under law" are the agitators and their gentile converts, and Jewish believers who ate with gentiles stood with Sarah. Matthew Thiessen's Jewish Paul keeps the point in view: Paul wanted gentiles to remain gentiles inside Abraham's family and never asked Jews to stop being Jews. The "present Jerusalem" is the city as a claim was made in her name, and the "Jerusalem above" is her own Isaiah's hope. Romans 9–11, where Paul returns to Israel's future at length, is the place to read the rest of what he thought; this passage is about one dispute, in one decade, over one demand. Columns sort by standing, not ancestry

The allegory in one paragraph

Abraham had two sons, one by ordinary means and one by a promise no one could have kept but God. Both were circumcised; one inherited, and it was the one the promise had named. Paul keeps every line of that story and asks only which column the covenant of Sinai stands in. Read as the ground of belonging, it stands with the slave woman, because it governs minors and the agitators are putting adults back under it; and the Jerusalem that insists on it stands there too. The free woman's city is Isaiah's Zion, barren and then crowded, whose children are the gentiles the agitators would exclude. The Galatians are already her children. For freedom the Messiah set them free; the next verse says so.

The Galatians are already the free woman's children; the allegory is not finished until "for freedom" (5:1).

For teaching and study

Reading the Passage with a Group

A sequence that lets the passage do its own work, followed by questions for discussion.

  1. Read Genesis 16 and 21:1–21 aloud first. Paul's "do you not hear the law?" assumes the story is in the room. Note who is circumcised and when (17:23–27; 21:4).
  2. Draw the two columns the way a Jewish reader of the first century would. Hagar, Ishmael, flesh, the nations; Sarah, Isaac, promise, the law, Jerusalem. Let the group fill it in; they will put Sinai on Sarah's side.
  3. Read Galatians 4:21–31 and move only what Paul moves. Two rows: the covenant and the city. Ask why those two, and look back at 3:19–4:7 for the reason.
  4. Read Isaiah 51:1–3 and 54:1–3. Ask who "the barren one" is, and how Paul can call a city "our mother."
  5. Find the word Paul changed in Genesis 21:10, and ask what the change does.
  6. End at 5:1. The allegory is not finished until "for freedom."
