Where does Scripture record women singing, prophesying, praying, serving, teaching, and bearing witness? Follow five registers through the Tanakh, Gospels, Acts, Letters, and Revelation. The index accounts for all 66 books in the Protestant collection while distinguishing narrated participation, instructions, interpretation, and literary or symbolic voices. It is a selective passage guide, not a census of every woman or every utterance.

The Frame: Genesis 1–2

Genesis supplies the survey’s opening frame. The text’s statements about humanity and the woman are distinguished here from a theological reading of Eden as sacred space.

Recorded in the text

  • Image, blessing, commission (Gen 1:26–28). God makes humanity "male and female" in his image, and the blessing and commission that follow are addressed in the plural: "God blessed them, and God said to them, 'Be fruitful… fill the earth and subdue it, and have dominion.'" The royal vocation is given to both.
  • Work and keep (Gen 2:15). The human is placed in the garden "to work it and keep it" (לְעָבְדָהּ וּלְשָׁמְרָהּ). This command precedes the woman’s formation. Reading her as sharing the garden vocation draws on the narrative as a whole; the command is not repeated to her in this verse.
  • A help corresponding to him (Gen 2:18, 20). The woman is עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ, ʿēzer kenegdô: ʿēzer is the word Scripture uses most often of God himself as Israel's help (Exod 18:4; Deut 33:29; Ps 33:20; 70:5; 121:1–2), and kenegdô means "corresponding to him," "like-opposite him."
  • Speech (Gen 2:23; 4:1, 25). The first recorded human words are the man's poem about the woman. After Eden, the first recorded human speech that names the LORD is Eve's: "I have gotten a man with the LORD" (4:1), and again at Seth's birth, "God has appointed for me another offspring" (4:25).

Widely observed in the text

  • The priestly echo. The verb pair of Gen 2:15, ʿābad and šāmar, recurs together in Numbers for the Levites' duty in the sanctuary (Num 3:7–8; 8:26; 18:5–6). This overlap supports a sanctuary reading of Eden, alongside other garden–sanctuary correspondences. Genesis 2 does not give either human a priestly title. The literary connection is an interpretation, not a narrated appointment to the later Aaronic office.
  • Built, and building a household. Genesis 2:22 uses bānâ, “build,” for God’s formation of the woman. The same root describes being built up through offspring in women’s speech (Gen 16:2; 30:3), and the people and elders of Bethlehem bless Ruth by recalling Rachel and Leah building Israel’s house (Ruth 4:11). These usages support a literary connection between woman, family, and building. They do not by themselves call Eve a temple. Explore the architectural and sanctuary reading.
  • A kingdom of priests. At Sinai a corporate priestly vocation is given to the nation: "you shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" is said to "the house of Jacob" as a whole (Exod 19:3–6). The New Testament repeats it for the church (1 Pet 2:5, 9; Rev 1:6; 5:10).
  • A canonical pattern. Garden, Sinai, sanctuary, and church can be read together as a theological pattern. Corporate priesthood, household membership, and particular sanctuary duties must still be distinguished.

What counts as "participation" here

The five registers organize song and poetic praise; prophecy and the reception or relay of revelation; prayer, vows, fasting, and lament; sanctuary and assembly; and witness, counsel, teaching, material support, and narrated action that preserves life or sustains the community. A named prophet, a recipient of an oracle, and a personified woman are different kinds of evidence. Laws, commands, metaphors, and contextual examples are therefore labeled rather than treated as reports of an event. Regulating texts receive their own section and remain visible in the index. Scripture quotations follow the ESV unless otherwise noted; explanatory summaries are this study’s paraphrases.

Read the kind of evidence

A role answers what happens? An evidence label answers how does this passage present it? These labels help keep the strength and limits of each claim visible.

Recorded / described
The text quotes speech or reports an action, title, or group. A report of prophecy may preserve no transcript.
Law / instruction
A command, qualification, or permission describes expected participation. It is not automatically an account of a completed event.
Inference
A connection is plausible from context but goes beyond the passage’s direct wording, such as Phoebe carrying Romans.
Literary / symbolic
A poem, parable, personification, or vision gives a woman a literary voice. The figure may represent a whole community.
Text / reading debated
A manuscript variant, translation, speaker assignment, or interpretive question materially affects the claim.

These are descriptive categories, not scores. A poetic voice can be explicitly quoted; a named person can be part of a symbolic action. The explanation beside each passage takes priority over its tags.

Read the speaker, the action, the setting, and the genre together. A careful survey lets each passage say as much as it says, and keeps interpretation visible.

Song and Music Register 1

Israel's story records its victories, births, and griefs in song, and a striking share of the singers the narrators name are women. The pattern begins at the Sea.

Miriam at the Sea: a call and its answer

Exodus 15 opens with Moses and the Israelites singing the long Song of the Sea (15:1–18) and closes with Miriam, "the prophetess, the sister of Aaron," taking up a tambourine while "all the women went out after her with tambourines and dancing" (15:20). The verb the narrator chooses for her is וַתַּעַן (wattaʿan), "and she answered," a verb used elsewhere for responsive or antiphonal singing (compare 1 Sam 18:7; 21:11; 29:5, where the women "answered one another" as they sang). The object is לָהֶם (lāhem), a masculine plural "them": she answers the men, or the whole company. What she sings is the song's own first line (15:1b), turned from the cohortative "I will sing" into the imperative "Sing!" (15:21). The repeated wording and response verb support a responsive-song reading, led by a woman the narrator calls a prophet. The exact performance arrangement and the refrain’s composition history are not specified.

The other named singers

Deborah

JUDGES 5:1–31

"Then sang Deborah and Barak" opens the longest poem in Judges, and the verb is feminine singular (וַתָּשַׁר): a verb before a compound subject can agree with its first member. The verse names both Deborah and Barak as singers; the feminine singular does not exclude Barak from singing. Inside the song she speaks in the first person, "until I arose, Deborah, arose as a mother in Israel" (5:7), and is summoned to sing: "Awake, awake, Deborah! Awake, awake, break out in a song!" (5:12).

Hannah

1 SAMUEL 2:1–10

Having brought Samuel to Eli at Shiloh, "Hannah prayed and said" a ten-verse psalm at the sanctuary. It ends on "his anointed," the first use of māšîaḥ in Samuel, and the Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55) shares its themes of reversal, humility, and divine strength, among other scriptural echoes. Chapter 1 describes moving lips without an audible voice (1:13). Chapter 2 presents a poetic prayer in the Shiloh narrative but does not specify its audience or whether it was sung.

The women of the cities

1 SAMUEL 18:6–7; 21:11; 29:5

"The women came out of all the cities of Israel, singing and dancing… with tambourines," and their couplet, "Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands," becomes a proverb that the Philistines themselves quote twice. The narrative makes their refrain a repeated public assessment of David’s military success.

Daughters who kept a calendar

JUDGES 11:34, 39–40; 21:19–21

Jephthah's daughter meets her father "with tambourines and with dances." The story says Jephthah carried out his vow and reports an annual four-day commemoration by Israel’s daughters (11:39–40). The dominant reading is her death; a minority interpretation proposes lifelong dedication. The tragedy must remain visible. At Shiloh, "the daughters of Shiloh come out to dance in the dances" at "the yearly feast of the LORD." Judges 21 then describes their abduction; this is a scene of communal violence, not an uncomplicated celebration of participation.

The women of Psalm 68

PSALM 68:11, 25; 148:12

"The Lord gives the word; the women who announce the news are a great host" (68:11; הַמְבַשְּׂרוֹת, a feminine plural participle of the root behind "good news"). In the procession "into the sanctuary" come "the singers in front, the musicians last, between them virgins playing tambourines" (68:24–25). Psalm 148:12 summons "young men and maidens together" to praise.

The Song of Songs

SONG 1:2; 2:7; 3:5; 5:8–9; 6:1; 8:4, 6–7, 14

In the usual assignment of speakers, the woman opens the poetry after the superscription and closes the book. Her voice stands alongside the male lover’s and the responses usually assigned to the daughters of Jerusalem. The daughters hear her charges about love and ask questions that draw out her delight in her beloved. The Hebrew text has no printed speaker labels, so detailed assignments remain interpretive.

Explore the daughters as companions and participants in learning about love.

Music, households, and trained lament

The evidence has different levels of specificity. Chronicles mentions Heman’s daughters near the temple-musician list; Ezra and Nehemiah explicitly count female singers but distinguish them from the temple-singer census. Other passages describe trained lament and its transmission without naming a formal guild.

Singers who came home

EZRA 2:65; NEHEMIAH 7:67; 12:43

The returnees' census counts "200 male and female singers" (Ezra), 245 in Nehemiah’s parallel list. These singers are listed separately from the temple singers descended from Asaph (Ezra 2:41; Neh 7:44), so the census alone does not establish their sanctuary office. At the dedication of the wall "the women and children also rejoiced, and the joy of Jerusalem was heard far away."

The trained lamenters

2 CHRONICLES 35:25; JEREMIAH 9:17–20

After Josiah's death "all the singing men and singing women have spoken of Josiah in their laments to this day… they are written in the Laments." Jeremiah calls for "the mourning women… the skillful women" and tells them to "teach to your daughters a lament, and each to her neighbor a dirge": a taught repertoire transmitted among women. The text does not identify the written collection with the canonical book of Lamentations or describe a formal guild.

Restoration pictured as women's dance

JEREMIAH 31:4, 13; ISAIAH 54:1

"Again you shall adorn yourself with tambourines and shall go forth in the dance of the merrymakers"; "then shall the young women rejoice in the dance." Isaiah's "Sing, O barren one, who did not bear" addresses Zion in a woman's voice. These images invite comparison with Exodus 15. Zion is personified; a command to sing is not itself a report that a particular woman performed a song. Compare Zeph 3:14–17 and Zech 2:10; 9:9.

From Luke's canticles to the congregation

Luke’s opening chapters gather poetic praise, blessing, and testimony. Elizabeth speaks a Spirit-filled blessing (1:41–45); Mary responds with the Magnificat (1:46–55); and Anna gives thanks and speaks about the child in the temple (2:38). Luke introduces Mary’s poem as something she said; its traditional use as a canticle does not establish a narrated musical performance. The prophecy section diagrams selected responses from Luke 1–2.

The Letters then describe the singing of the whole assembly as mutual instruction: "addressing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs" (Eph 5:19); "teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs" (Col 3:16); "when you come together, each one has a hymn, a lesson, a revelation" (1 Cor 14:26). These are instructions to congregations, rather than transcripts of particular women singing. Revelation also depicts communal heavenly praise (5:9–14; 7:9–12; 15:2–4; 19:1–8). Near its close the Spirit and the Bride say “Come” (22:17); the verse also invites the thirsty to receive life-giving water. The opening calls may be directed to Jesus (compare 22:20), while the latter invitation addresses the thirsty. The Bride is a collective image, and this verse describes speech rather than explicitly identifying a song.

Pattern Summary

The passages include victory songs, poetic prayer, processions, court singers, and taught lament. Some explicitly describe music; others preserve praise as speech or summon a community to sing. Luke’s canticles, congregational instructions, and Revelation’s collective imagery belong in the comparison with those distinctions intact.

Prophecy Register 2

Scripture gives the title "prophetess" (נְבִיאָה, nəḇîʾâ; Greek προφῆτις) to six female figures in these books: Miriam, Deborah, Huldah, the unnamed prophetess in Isaiah 8:3, Noadiah, and Anna. Revelation’s “Jezebel” claims the title; Philip’s daughters and the women condemned in Ezekiel 13 are described with a verb. Noadiah is included in the six, despite the narrative’s negative assessment. The table records each case at the level the text does.

Receiving and relaying a word, without the title

Hagar

GENESIS 16:7–14; 21:17–19

The angel of the LORD finds her by a spring and gives her an oracle about her son. She answers by naming God, "You are a God of seeing" (אֵל רֳאִי), a striking instance of a woman addressing God with a descriptive name. The text’s significance does not depend on an unqualified claim of uniqueness. In chapter 21 "God heard the voice of the boy," and the angel addresses her again.

Rebekah

GENESIS 25:22–23

"She went to inquire of the LORD" (לִדְרֹשׁ אֶת־יְהוָה), the first person in Genesis said to do so, and the oracle about two nations and the elder serving the younger is given to her directly.

Samson's mother

JUDGES 13:2–25

The angel appears to her first, and again "as she sat in the field, but Manoah her husband was not with her" (13:9). She relays the annunciation to Manoah and receives dietary restrictions during pregnancy (13:4, 14), and reasons correctly when he fears death: "If the LORD had meant to kill us, he would not have accepted a burnt offering" (13:23).

Abigail

1 SAMUEL 25:23–35

Her speech foretells what the narrative will confirm: "the LORD will certainly make my lord a sure house, because my lord is fighting the battles of the LORD" (25:28). David answers, "Blessed be your discretion, and blessed be you, who have kept me this day from bloodguilt" (25:33).

Elizabeth

LUKE 1:41–45

"Filled with the Holy Spirit," she "exclaimed with a loud cry" a blessing on Mary and the child, recognizes him before his birth as "my Lord," and pronounces the beatitude "blessed is she who believed." Luke uses the same Spirit-filling language for Zechariah’s prophecy later in the chapter (1:67).

The hinge text
"And in the last days it shall be, God declares, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh, and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy… even on my male servants and female servants in those days I will pour out my Spirit, and they shall prophesy."
— Joel 2:28–29, as Peter cites it at Pentecost, Acts 2:17–18

Peter reads the morning of Pentecost through Joel, and the women are inside his quotation twice: "daughters" and "female servants." Acts then records the fulfilment at the level of report rather than transcript: Philip's daughters prophesy (21:9), and 1 Corinthians 11:5 refers to women praying and prophesying while addressing the manner in which they do so. Chapter 14 describes prophecy as speech that builds up the community (14:3). Its restrictions on speech (14:26–40) must also be considered; the relationship among these passages is debated.

Pattern Summary

The title "prophetess" is given sparingly and across every era: the Exodus (Miriam), the judges (Deborah), the late monarchy (Huldah, Isaiah's wife), the restoration (Noadiah, on the wrong side), and the first century (Anna, with Philip's daughters described by the verb). The negative cases, Noadiah, the daughters of Ezekiel’s people, and the woman at Thyatira, are condemned for their content and allegiance; the texts treat prophecy by women as a real category that can be counterfeited.

Prayer, Vows, and Fasts Register 3

The narratives record women praying aloud and in silence, binding themselves with vows, calling fasts, and keeping vigil, and the New Testament names them in the church's prayer meetings.

Hagar in the wilderness (Gen 16:11; 21:16–17): The angel says the LORD has heard her affliction (16:11). In 21:16 Hagar weeps aloud; 21:17 explicitly says God hears the boy. An angel addresses her and God enables her to see water. The text does not quote a prayer from her.
Rebekah inquires of the LORD (Gen 25:22–23): Distressed by her pregnancy, "she went to inquire of the LORD," and the LORD answered her with the oracle about her sons.
The Nazirite vow, "a man or a woman" (Num 6:2): The Nazirite law explicitly applies to women as well as men and regulates abstention, hair, and contact with the dead. This is legislation, not a report of a particular woman making the vow. Samson’s mother receives pregnancy-related dietary commands; Judges 13:5 calls her son the Nazirite.
Women's vows (Num 30): The chapter assumes women make vows to the LORD and regulates when a father or husband may annul one; the vow of a widow or divorced woman "shall stand against her" (30:9), with no one to annul it.
Hannah at Shiloh (1 Sam 1:9–18; 2:1–10): She "prayed to the LORD and wept bitterly" and vowed a Nazirite-shaped vow for her son ("no razor shall touch his head," 1:11). Eli, watching from his seat by the doorpost of the sanctuary, mistakes her silent prayer for drunkenness, then blesses her. When she returns with the child, the narrator introduces the poetic prayer of chapter 2. Its performance and audience are not specified.
Rizpah's vigil (2 Sam 21:10–14): She spreads sackcloth on the rock and keeps the bodies of Saul's sons from the birds and beasts "from the beginning of harvest until rain fell." David hears of it, gathers the bones of Saul and Jonathan for burial, "and after that God responded to the plea for the land." Her action is a mourning vigil; the narrator does not report her praying or identify that plea as hers.
Esther calls the fast; Esther confirms the feast (Esth 4:16; 9:29–32): "Go, gather all the Jews… and hold a fast on my behalf… I and my young women will also fast." At the end of the book "Queen Esther, the daughter of Abihail, and Mordecai the Jew gave full written authority," and "the command of Esther confirmed these practices of Purim, and it was recorded in writing." Esther and Mordecai authorize Purim observance. The Hebrew text explicitly describes fasting here, but does not narrate Esther’s prayer; the Greek additions contain prayers and must be identified separately.
Wives and daughters bind the oath (Neh 10:28–29): At the covenant renewal "their wives, their sons, their daughters, all who have knowledge and understanding, join with their brothers… and enter into a curse and an oath to walk in God's Law."
Anna (Luke 2:37); the persistent widow (Luke 18:1–8): Anna "worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day" in the temple; and when Jesus teaches that his disciples "ought always to pray and not lose heart," the model he gives is a widow who will not stop asking.
Jerusalem’s mourning women (Luke 23:27–31): Women lament as Jesus is led to execution. He addresses them as daughters of Jerusalem and directs their tears toward themselves and their children in view of coming suffering. This is narrated lament; their reply and understanding of his death are not reported. Read the scene in its own setting.
The church at prayer (Acts 1:14; 12:12; 16:13; 21:5): Before Pentecost the apostles "with one accord were devoting themselves to prayer, together with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus." Peter, freed from prison, goes to the house of Mary the mother of John Mark, "where many were gathered together and were praying." At Philippi the "place of prayer" by the river is where Paul finds Lydia. At Tyre the whole church, "with wives and children," kneels on the beach to pray.
The widow's calling (1 Tim 5:5); the Corinthian gathering (1 Cor 11:5): "She who is truly a widow… has set her hope on God and continues in supplications and prayers night and day." In Corinth, Paul writes of "every wife [or woman] who prays or prophesies" and instructs her on how to do it.
Where the women pray: Shiloh, the Jerusalem temple, a gathered community, a house, a riverside prayer place, and a beach are explicit settings for prayer or worship in these passages. Hagar’s weeping, Rizpah’s vigil, Esther’s fast, and the covenant oath are related actions whose specific descriptions should be preserved.

Sanctuary and Assembly Register 4

This register gathers the texts that place women at Israel's holy places: bringing offerings, giving materials, serving at the entrance, sharing the priests' portions, standing in the covenant assembly, and hearing the Torah read.

Making the tabernacle

When the sanctuary is built, the narrator counts the women in twice. "They came, both men and women," bringing gold (Exod 35:22), and "all the men and women, the people of Israel, whose heart moved them to bring anything for the work… brought it as a freewill offering" (35:29). Between those two notices sits the one craft the text assigns by name to women: "every skillful woman spun with her hands, and they all brought what they had spun in blue and purple and scarlet yarns and fine twined linen. All the women whose hearts stirred them to use their skill spun the goats' hair" (35:25–26). Their named contribution is spinning the yarn and goats’ hair used in the sanctuary project; the passage does not assign all curtain-making exclusively to them.

The serving women at the entrance

צָבָא ṣāḇāʾ

The text: the bronze basin and its stand were made "from the mirrors of the ministering women who ministered at the entrance of the tent of meeting" (Exod 38:8; הַצֹּבְאֹת אֲשֶׁר צָבְאוּ). The Masoretic text of 1 Samuel 2:22 uses similar language in its account of Eli’s sons. That clause is absent from the Septuagint and apparently from 4QSama; it should not be presented as an uncontested second witness.

The lexical note: ṣāḇāʾ is the verb of military service and of the Levites' sanctuary duty. Numbers uses it for Levites "who can come to do duty, to do service in the tent of meeting" (Num 4:23; 8:24–25). Exodus describes women associated with the tent entrance; the precise sense of their service or gathering is debated. The later parallel has a textual variant; they do not describe the women's duties, and the parallel is lexical rather than explanatory (see notes).

Women bringing sacrifices

Leviticus 12:6–8: after childbirth a woman "shall bring to the priest at the entrance of the tent of meeting a lamb… and a pigeon or a turtledove," and the priest "shall make atonement for her." Luke 2:22–24 records the offering of birds permitted by the law; it does not say that Mary brought the lamb of the standard provision.

Leviticus 15:29–30; Numbers 6:13–21: these laws prescribe purification offerings and the completion offerings of a Nazirite, a vow open to women. Numbers 5:11–31 is a different situation: a husband brings his wife and the required offering to the priest for an ordeal prompted by jealousy. It is not a voluntary gift initiated by the woman. Legal participation, vulnerability within a procedure, and freewill support should not be conflated.

The priestly household

Some priestly portions may be shared with daughters: "you and your sons and your daughters with you" (Lev 10:14); "to you, and to your sons and daughters with you, as a perpetual due" (Num 18:11; compare 18:19). Leviticus 22:12–13 then works out the edge cases, a priest's daughter who marries outside the priesthood and one who returns widowed or divorced to her father's house, "she may eat of her father's food." These are specified household entitlements. Other most-holy portions are restricted to priestly males (Lev 10:12–13; Num 18:9–10); household membership does not confer the priest’s altar duties. Later narratives add Jehosheba, the king's daughter and the priest Jehoiada's wife, who hides the infant Joash "in the house of the LORD six years" (2 Kgs 11:2–3; 2 Chr 22:11), and Elizabeth, "from the daughters of Aaron," whom Luke introduces together with Zechariah as "righteous before God, walking blamelessly in all the commandments" (Luke 1:5–6).

The daughters of Zelophehad

Five sisters "stood before Moses and before Eleazar the priest and before the chiefs and all the congregation, at the entrance of the tent of meeting" to petition for their father's inheritance (Num 27:1–2). The LORD's verdict is, "The daughters of Zelophehad are right," and the case becomes "a statute and rule" for Israel (27:7–11; 36:1–12; Josh 17:3–6). Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah bring a legal petition that leads to a ruling on inheritance when a man dies without a son. Numbers 36 adds a tribal-marriage condition. This is a specific legal development, not a general equal-inheritance law.

Pattern Summary

The sanctuary texts show women at the entrance of the tent in four capacities: bringing offerings, giving the sanctuary's materials, being associated with service at its entrance, and bringing a legal petition. The assembly texts, from Moab to the Water Gate, name women explicitly among those who stand, hear, and swear. The New Testament's "royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9; Rev 1:6; 5:10) is addressed to the whole church without distinction, restating Exodus 19:6.

Witness, Teaching, and Patronage Register 5

The fifth register is the widest: confessions of faith the narrators preserve verbatim, mothers' teaching that became Scripture, the first announcements of the resurrection, and the named coworkers and patrons of the apostolic mission.

Confessions on record

Rahab

JOSHUA 2:9–11

"I know that the LORD has given you the land… the LORD your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth beneath." Naaman's servant girl

2 KINGS 5:2–3

A captive "little girl from the land of Israel" says, "Would that my lord were with the prophet who is in Samaria!" Her word moves a Syrian general, a Syrian king, and an Israelite king, and the healing follows.

The queen of Sheba

1 KINGS 10:1–13

JOHN 4:7–42

She receives the Gospel's first "I am he" (4:26), leaves her jar, and tells the town. "Many Samaritans from that town believed in him because of the woman's testimony," and then, having heard him, "because of his word" (4:39–42).

The wise women of Tekoa and Abel

2 SAMUEL 14:1–20; 20:14–22

The narrators identify these women as wise. Tekoa’s woman delivers a speech devised with Joab (14:2–3, 19); Abel’s woman negotiates with Joab from the wall: "I am one of those who are peaceable and faithful in Israel… Why will you swallow up the heritage of the LORD?" and the city is spared. These are examples of counsel and negotiation; neither woman is called a prophet or said to receive a divine oracle.

A mother's torah

Proverbs opens by binding the son to two teachers: "Hear, my son, your father's instruction, and forsake not your mother's teaching" (תּוֹרַת אִמֶּךָ, 1:8; again 6:20). It closes with a chapter whose superscription is "The words of King Lemuel. An oracle that his mother taught him" (31:1), an explicit attribution of instruction to a mother in 31:1–9. A separate acrostic poem in 31:10–31 portrays a woman who "opens her mouth with wisdom, and the teaching of kindness is on her tongue" (31:26). A related New Testament pattern appears in Timothy’s sincere faith, associated with his grandmother Lois and mother Eunice (2 Tim 1:5), and his knowledge of Scripture from infancy (3:14–15). Their part in his scriptural instruction is a natural inference from these passages, though the second passage does not name his teachers. Alongside them stand the older women of Titus 2:3, called καλοδιδασκάλους, "teachers of what is good," who are to train the younger. Proverbs' personified Wisdom, who "cries aloud in the street… at the entrance of the city gates she speaks" (1:20–21; 8:1–36; 9:1–6), is a literary figure rather than a woman of history, but the book chose to voice its instruction through her.

In Acts

Pattern Summary

In the Old Testament this register runs through confession (Rahab, Zarephath, Sheba), counsel (the wise women), prophetic interpretation (Huldah), and instruction (Lemuel's mother). In the New Testament it runs through testimony (Sychar, Bethany, the tomb), teaching (Priscilla with Aquila, and the older women of Titus 2), family transmission of faith and Scripture (Lois and Eunice, with their teaching role inferred), labor (the women of Romans 16 and Philippians 4), and patronage (Joanna, Susanna, Lydia, Phoebe, Nympha). Several shared terms describe women and men as disciples, coworkers, servants or ministers, and people who labor. Each passage still needs its own setting; a shared word alone does not establish an identical office in every case.

Bethlehem’s women: naming restoration at the horizon of David

RUTH 1:1, 19–22; 4:13–22 · COMPARE JUDG 21:25

What the women say

At Naomi’s return, the town’s women ask whether this is Naomi (1:19). Near the close, women bless the LORD, praise Ruth—the daughter-in-law who loves Naomi—as better to her than seven sons, and celebrate the child’s provision for her future (4:14–15). The neighboring women speak of a son born to Naomi and give the child the name Obed (4:17).

What the narrator adds

The story opens in the days of the judges (1:1). After the women name Obed, the narrator identifies him as Jesse’s father and David’s grandfather (4:17), then closes with a genealogy ending in David (4:18–22). Household restoration is thereby connected to Israel’s royal future.

The larger reading: against Judges’ closing picture of everyone doing what is right in their own eyes, Ruth’s loyal care and Bethlehem’s blessings can be read as a different way forward: responsible action, communal recognition, and trust in God’s provision. The women interpret the restoration before them; the narrator reveals its place in David’s ancestry. The book does not quote the women predicting David or commanding trust in a dynasty.

The blessing invoking Rachel, Leah, and Tamar belongs to the people and elders at the gate (4:11–12). The women’s speech to Naomi is a distinct scene (4:14–15). This comparison follows narrative setting and genealogy; it does not require placing Ruth immediately after Judges in every canonical order.

Song of Songs: a conversation about love

SONG 2:7; 3:5; 5:8–9; 6:1; 8:4, 6–7

The woman repeatedly addresses the daughters of Jerusalem with a charge about love’s awakening. Material support

Luke 8: women help sustain Jesus’ ministry

LUKE 8:1–3; 24:10 · COMPARE MARK 15:40–41

Luke places women alongside the Twelve in Jesus’ traveling company. He names Mary Magdalene, Joanna—whose husband Chuza serves Herod—and Susanna, then includes many others. These women provide from their own resources. Their material and financial support helps sustain the ministry as Jesus proclaims God’s kingdom from town to town.

Revelation: distinguish the women and the symbols

Revelation moves between messages to actual churches and visionary scenes. A teacher addressed in Thyatira and a woman who represents a city are not the same kind of evidence. The book’s imagery should be read as imagery before it is used to describe historical women’s roles.

The teacher called “Jezebel”

REVELATION 2:20–23

A claim under judgment

The message to Thyatira condemns a woman who claims to be a prophetess and whose teaching leads the servants of Jesus into idolatrous conduct. “Jezebel” evokes the earlier queen and may be a polemical designation. The passage reports the claim and condemns the teaching; it does not endorse her prophetic authority.

The woman clothed with the sun

REVELATION 12:1–17

Visionary sign

The woman bears the child, faces the dragon, and has other offspring described by their allegiance to God and Jesus. Interpretations connect her with Israel, Mary, and the people of God in different ways. She is presented as a sign, not as an individually named prophetess giving a recorded speech.

Babylon, the great city

REVELATION 17–18; ESPECIALLY 17:18; 18:7

City and imperial power

The vision portrays a ruling city as a richly adorned woman associated with exploitation and violence. Her boast in 18:7 belongs to that personification. The symbol critiques a system of power; it should not become a claim that women as a class embody corruption.

The Bride and New Jerusalem

REVELATION 19:7–8; 21:2, 9–10; 22:17

People and city

The promised Bride is shown as the holy city, and her clothing is connected to the righteous deeds of the saints. The Spirit and Bride’s invitation in 22:17 is a collective voice. The verse says they speak; it does not identify a woman’s solo or a song.

Compare carefully: the city images of Babylon and New Jerusalem invite comparison with prophetic portrayals of cities as women. They widen the study’s literary and theological scope without adding historical women to a headcount.

Women's Words as Scripture's Words

A last category cuts across the registers: places where a woman's speech is not merely reported but becomes the text's own structure, naming, or wording.

Mothers who name

Eve names Cain and Seth with theological statements (Gen 4:1, 25). Leah and Rachel participate in the naming speeches of Genesis 29:31–30:24, including names assigned to the sons born through Bilhah and Zilpah. Genesis 35:18 records Rachel’s Ben-oni and Jacob’s Benjamin. The women’s words interpret births without making every naming formula identical. Hannah names Samuel (1 Sam 1:20), Phinehas's wife names Ichabod, "the glory has departed" (4:21), and the women of Bethlehem name Obed (Ruth 4:17).

Hagar names God

"You are a God of seeing," El-Roi (Gen 16:13). The narrator adds that the well was named from her words. Genesis explicitly attributes this description to Hagar; the wording should be read alongside other biblical naming and invocation traditions.

A mother’s instruction preserved

Proverbs 31:1 introduces instruction that Lemuel’s mother taught him, usually identified with 31:1–9. The alphabetic poem in 31:10–31 is a distinct unit and is not explicitly attributed to her. Maśśāʾ is often rendered “oracle”; some construe Massa as a place-name.

A women's song in enemy mouths

The couplet the women sang on David's return (1 Sam 18:7) is quoted by the Philistine courtiers (21:11) and commanders (29:5). The narrator uses a women's song as the shorthand for David's reputation.

Daughter Zion speaks

Lamentations hands its first-person voice to personified Daughter Zion ("See, O LORD, my affliction," 1:9c, 11c–16, 18–22; 2:20–22), and the Song of Songs is voiced chiefly by the woman. In both books the text's "I" is written in a woman's voice.

A queen's decree, and the Bride's invitation

"The command of Esther confirmed these practices of Purim, and it was recorded in writing" (Esth 9:32). Near Revelation’s close, the Bride speaks as a collective symbol of the people of God: "The Spirit and the Bride say, 'Come'" (Rev 22:17).

The Record Includes Failures

A survey of participation would be incomplete if it kept only the faithful cases. Scripture records women as accountable agents in worship and prophecy on both sides of the line, and it describes the failures in the same registers, prophecy, cult, vow, and counsel, that it uses for the faithful acts.

Reading Notes: What This Page Does and Does Not Claim

Genesis 2:15 and the priestly verbs. Is the garden "a sanctuary" in the text?
The text says the human was placed in the garden "to work it and keep it." The observation that the same verb pair describes the Levites' service (Num 3:7–8; 8:26; 18:5–6) is a reading offered by many commentators and recorded here as such. The page does not claim that Genesis 2 calls the woman a priest. She appears alongside the man in the garden narrative; Genesis 1:26–28 explicitly addresses the shared human commission. Exodus 19:6 and 1 Peter 2:9 use corporate priesthood language, which does not by itself identify particular ritual duties or offices.
Exodus 15:21. Did Miriam compose the song, or answer it?
The received text presents 15:1–18 as sung by Moses and Israel and 15:21 as Miriam's answer with the opening line. Some scholars have argued on form-critical grounds that the whole poem was originally "the Song of Miriam" and was later attributed to Moses. This page records the narrative sequence, Miriam’s response, and the shared wording. A call-and-response performance is a plausible reading; the passage does not settle the song’s earlier composition history.
Isaiah 8:3. Is "the prophetess" a title or a description?
Isaiah's wife is called hannəḇîʾâ and nothing else. Some commentators read the title as hers by marriage to the prophet; others note that the Old Testament nowhere else uses "prophetess" for a prophet's wife and read it as her own vocation. The text does not settle it, so the table records the title and the silence.
Exodus 38:8 and 1 Samuel 2:22. What did the serving women do?
Exodus 38:8 associates women with the entrance of the tent; translations include serving, ministering, and assembling. The root also occurs in Levitical service texts, but a shared root does not establish identical duties. The Masoretic text of 1 Samuel 2:22 has a parallel clause absent from the Septuagint and apparently from 4QSama (see the NET textual note). Neither passage specifies a priestly office for these women.
Psalm 68:11. Why do translations differ?
The Hebrew participle ham-məḇaśśərôt is feminine plural: "the women who announce the news." Some translations render it generically ("those who proclaim"). The page follows the grammatical gender, which several modern translations also preserve, and distinguishes this feminine plural from Isaiah 40:9, where the feminine singular can address personified Zion as herald. Feminine grammar does not by itself establish a historical female officeholder. English Psalm 68:11 and 25 correspond to Hebrew 68:12 and 26.
Acts 21:9. Are Philip's daughters "prophetesses"?
Luke uses the participle "prophesying," not the noun. The page keeps that distinction in the table and in the count of titled prophetesses. Eusebius's later notices (Ecclesiastical History 3.31; 3.37; 3.39) preserve second-century memory of Philip's daughters as sources of tradition, with some confusion between Philip the evangelist and Philip the apostle.
Romans 16:7. Who is Junia, and what does "among the apostles" mean?
Junia is read here as a woman, consistent with the attested feminine name and many modern translations. A separate question concerns episēmoi en tois apostolois: translations include “prominent among the apostles” and “well known to the apostles.” These construe her and Andronicus’s relationship to the apostolic group differently. Neither reading identifies Junia as one of the Twelve. Compare the translation notes.
1 Timothy 3:11. Women deacons or deacons' wives?
Gynaikas can mean "women" or "wives." Read as women, the verse may describe female deacons or women serving alongside deacons; read as wives, it concerns deacons’ households. The term alone does not settle the role. The page lists the verse among the regulating texts and leaves the decision to the reader.
The Song of Songs. How much of it is the woman's voice?
Assigning lines to speakers involves judgment, since the Hebrew text has no speaker labels. Many readings give the woman a large share of the poetry, including its opening after the superscription and its closing words. The daughters of Jerusalem are explicitly addressed and are usually assigned responsive questions, especially in 5:9 and 6:1. Calling them learners about love is a literary interpretation; 8:6–7 should not automatically be presented as their speech.
Deborah, "wife of Lappidoth."
ʾēšet lappîdôt is usually taken as "wife of Lappidoth," but lappîd means "torch," and some read "woman of torches" or "fiery woman." The page uses the conventional rendering and notes the alternative.
Deuterocanonical and later texts.
This index uses the 66-book Protestant collection and arranges the Hebrew Bible in Tanakh divisions. Jewish, Catholic, and Orthodox collections count and arrange books differently. Judith’s hymn (Judith 16), Susanna’s prayer and vindication, the prayers in Greek Esther, and the mother in 2 Maccabees 7 are scriptural in Catholic and many Orthodox traditions, rather than merely later illustrations. The rabbinic seven prophetesses (b. Megillah 14a) and Eusebius’s traditions are later reception. These bodies of evidence are identified separately and excluded from this index’s 66-book count.
Phoebe, Junia, and Priscilla. Which role claims are direct?
Romans 16:1–2 calls Phoebe diakonos of the church at Cenchreae and prostatis of many: translations use servant, minister, or deacon, and patron or benefactor. Her delivery of Romans is inferred. Junia’s commendation is explicit; her relation to the apostles has differing translations. Acts 18:26 explicitly presents Priscilla and Aquila teaching Apollos together; appearing first in some name orders does not by itself establish rank.
Heman’s daughters and the returning singers. Are they all temple musicians?
Heman’s daughters are mentioned in 1 Chronicles 25:5, and their inclusion in the following musical service is possible, though not itemized. Ezra 2:65 and Nehemiah 7:67 explicitly count both male and female singers, but separately from Asaph’s temple singers. Both figures are combined totals of men and women; they are not counts of female singers alone. Nehemiah 12:43 reports the women’s rejoicing without making them members of its two named choirs.
The women who anoint Jesus. Are they the same person?
Luke 7:36–50 leaves its woman unnamed and does not call her a prostitute. Mark 14 and Matthew 26 also leave the Bethany anointer unnamed, while John 12 names Mary of Bethany. Mary Magdalene is introduced separately in Luke 8. Identifications across these accounts require an argument; this survey does not merge the women or fill the unnamed accounts with later tradition.
The Gospels’ textual variants. Why flag Mark 16 and John 7–8?
The manuscript tradition of Mark 16 includes endings beyond 16:8, while modern editions often mark 16:9–20 separately. The account of the woman in John 7:53–8:11 also has a significant manuscript and placement history. These passages can be studied, but their evidence should carry the textual qualification rather than being silently combined with the surrounding narrative.
Hebrew numbering and the canon. Why do references sometimes differ?
English Psalm 68:11 and 25 correspond to Hebrew 68:12 and 26. English Joel 2:28–29 is Hebrew Joel 3:1–2. Tanakh combines some books that English Christian Bibles divide, and orders the Writings differently across traditions. The 66-book interface is a navigation choice, not a claim that Jewish, Catholic, and Orthodox collections share that count.

Study Questions

Observation: Which examples are narrated actions, and which are commands, symbols, or inferences?
Use the evidence filter in the book index. Compare Miriam’s narrated music, the instruction to sing in Colossians 3:16, and the Bride’s speech in Revelation 22:17. These are related but different kinds of evidence; the number of examples selected is not a frequency measure.
Literary: What verbs do the narrators choose for Miriam, Deborah, and Hannah, and what do the forms of those verbs show?
"She answered them" (Exod 15:21, with a masculine plural object), “Deborah and Barak sang” (Judg 5:1, a feminine singular verb preceding a compound subject), "Hannah prayed and said" (1 Sam 2:1). Ask what each narrator chose to make explicit.
Structure: How do these six selected responses in Luke 1–2 differ in speaker, form, and content?
Compare Elizabeth’s blessing, Mary’s praise, Zechariah’s prophecy, the angels’ praise, Simeon’s blessing, and Anna’s reported thanksgiving and testimony. Notice other speech in the chapters, including Mary’s earlier response (1:38), rather than treating this selection as an exhaustive structure. Compare 1:41 and 1:67.
Patterns: Trace the tambourine from Exodus 15 through Judges 11, 1 Samuel 18, Psalm 68, and Jeremiah 31.
Note who holds it in each text and what event it marks. Ask why Jeremiah pictures the return with this image.
Theological: What do the false-prophetess texts condemn, and what do they assume?
List the charges in Neh 6:14, Ezek 13:17–23, and Rev 2:20–23. Distinguish a reported prophetic title, a self-claim, and the narrator’s judgment. Avoid making a counterfeit claim alone prove a broader rule about office.
Narrative action: How can a narrator show faith when a woman’s words are not quoted?
Compare Exodus 2:2–3 with Genesis 1:4 and 6:14, then read Hebrews 11:23. Separate the mother’s reported action, the shared vocabulary, and Hebrews’ explicit interpretation. Contrast this with Rahab’s quoted confession and the resource-sharing reported in Luke 8:1–3.
Communal voices: Who interprets the turning point in Ruth 4?
Distinguish the people and elders at the gate (4:11–12), the women who speak to Naomi and name Obed (4:14–17), and the narrator who connects Obed to David (4:17–22). How does the ending enlarge a story that begins in the days of the judges?
Connections: Where does a woman's word become the text's own word?
Work through the "Women's Words" section: naming speeches, Hagar's name for God, Proverbs 31:1, the women's song in 1 Samuel 18, Daughter Zion, Esther's decree, the Bride's "Come."
Assembly: Which covenant and Torah-reading texts name women explicitly, and at what moments in Israel's story?
Moab (Deut 29), the sabbatical reading (Deut 31), Ebal (Josh 8), Jehoshaphat's fast (2 Chr 20), the Water Gate (Neh 8), the sealing of the covenant (Neh 10). Ask what these moments have in common.
