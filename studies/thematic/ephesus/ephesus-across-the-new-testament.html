01 / THE STUDY

A church encountered at different moments

Who is here? What are they facing? What responsibility has been entrusted to them? What are they being told about Jesus?

This study visits Ephesus at several moments preserved in the New Testament. Acts introduces the people around Paul’s extended ministry and records his farewell to the elders. Ephesians gives us instruction and prayer for the letter’s readers. First and Second Timothy show Paul addressing his coworker’s responsibilities amid particular problems. Revelation records Jesus’ assessment of the church at a later moment.

The value lies in returning to the community and seeing it again. Some people and responsibilities are explicitly connected across the records. Other circumstances are known only from a particular passage. These are snapshots of a church’s life, not a sequence of measured stages of maturity or a reconstruction of one uninterrupted controversy.

Each snapshot includes what the passage says about Jesus: the Lord proclaimed in Acts, the crucified and exalted Christ in Ephesians, the Christ confessed in the household instructions, the risen Messiah remembered by Timothy, and the living Lord who addresses Ephesus in Revelation.

Related work

This study draws on Resurrection as Formation and 1 Timothy: A Literary–Literal Edition, especially the place of the confession in 1 Timothy 3:14–16. Here the organizing subject is the church, its people, and its circumstances through time.

02 / SIX SNAPSHOTS

Six snapshots, one church

The dates below follow the traditional Pauline chronology for Ephesians and the letters to Timothy, with Revelation placed in the AD 90s. They locate the settings of the records, not stages of the church’s maturity.

Acts 18:18–19:41 (AD 50s): The community takes shape: teaching in the hall of Tyrannus, the Spirit, the renunciation of magic, and the conflict over Artemis. Paul later describes the stay as three years (20:31).
Acts 20:17–38 (later AD 50s): Paul summons the Ephesian elders to Miletus and warns of destructive leadership rising from within.
Ephesians (early AD 60s, traditionally): Christ’s cosmic lordship, Jew–Gentile reconciliation, and the life of his body.
1 Timothy (early to mid-AD 60s, traditionally): Timothy explicitly remains in Ephesus (1:3) to confront different teaching and order God’s household.
2 Timothy (mid- to late AD 60s, traditionally): Paul faces death; Timothy must guard and transmit the gospel. An Ephesian destination is probable, not explicit.
Revelation 2:1–7 (around AD 95, under the common late dating): The living Jesus commends endurance and discernment but commands a return to love.
Dates, Timothy’s location, and the destination of Ephesians

Pauline authorship and the dates of Ephesians and the Pastoral Letters are disputed. This study adopts the traditional setting as a working chronology; later-authorship proposals produce a different historical reconstruction. The dates attached to Acts refer to the events narrated, not the composition of Acts.

Timothy’s location in 2 Timothy

Timothy is probably still serving in Ephesus. The letter recalls Onesiphorus’s Ephesian service, reports sending Tychicus there, and sends greetings to Prisca, Aquila, and Onesiphorus’s household (1:18; 4:12, 19). Unlike 1 Timothy 1:3, it does not explicitly locate Timothy in the city. Paul’s Roman custody is indicated by 1:16–17. Tychicus providing coverage for Timothy is a reasonable proposal, not a stated assignment.

Ephesians and its readers

Important early manuscripts omit “in Ephesus” from 1:1, Tertullian reports that Marcion’s copy carried the title “to the Laodiceans,” and the letter is less personally specific than one might expect after Paul’s long stay. Many interpreters understand it as a circular letter. Ephesians can therefore contribute its instruction and prayers without every exhortation being treated as evidence of a uniquely Ephesian crisis.

An alternative placement

N. T. Wright proposes an Ephesian imprisonment and places the prison letters within that period, rather than the later Roman imprisonment. That would move Ephesians into Paul’s Ephesian years. The proposal is discussed alongside Paul’s testimony of danger.

03 / PAUL’S YEARS

Paul’s Ephesian years: work, relationships, and opposition

Before the riot there are years of relationships and repeated work. Acts supplies the public account; 1 Corinthians, written from Ephesus, adds coworkers, visitors, and a household gathering; 2 Corinthians recalls severe affliction in Asia. Together these records let us see more than the dramatic disturbance.

Settling into the work

The story begins with a brief visit. Paul arrives with Priscilla and Aquila, speaks in the synagogue, declines a request to stay longer, and promises to return if God wills; they remain when he leaves (Acts 18:18–21). Other people are already part of the story before he returns. Apollos arrives with a strong knowledge of the Scriptures and an incomplete understanding of baptism; Priscilla and Aquila explain the way of God to him more accurately; the brothers then encourage his journey to Achaia and write ahead to secure his welcome (18:24–28). When Paul returns, Apollos is in Corinth, and Paul finds disciples whose understanding is associated with John’s baptism; they are directed toward Jesus, baptized, and receive the Holy Spirit (19:1–7). That is one reported encounter with about twelve men, not a census of the church.

The initial pattern already combines proclamation and formation. Some people need fuller instruction; others need to renounce practices incompatible with their new allegiance. Apollos’s incomplete understanding is not depicted in the same way as the later teachers’ destructive distortion. The distinction matters: faithful teaching includes patient clarification, not merely identifying opponents.

Acts gives the stay a shape in time: three months of synagogue discussion, two years of daily teaching in the hall of Tyrannus, and a further stay in Asia (19:8–10, 22). At Miletus Paul recalls three years of admonishing people night and day with tears (20:31). He also recalls teaching publicly and in homes and working with his hands to meet his own needs and those of his companions (20:20, 34). Luke describes Jews and Greeks throughout the Roman province of Asia hearing the word (19:10). Ephesus is a base for sustained work with a regional reach.

Why he stayed is not left to a biographer to guess. Writing from the city, Paul tells the Corinthians he will remain in Ephesus until Pentecost because a wide door for effective work has opened to him, and there are many adversaries (1 Cor. 16:8–9). Opportunity and opposition are named in the same breath, and the second is not a reason to leave.

The letter also shows what Paul was carrying while he lived in Ephesus. He has heard from Chloe’s people about divisions in Corinth (1 Cor. 1:11), he answers matters the Corinthians wrote to him about (7:1), and he arranges a collection, approved representatives, and travel (16:1–7), including the possibility of wintering with the Corinthians after passing through Macedonia (16:6). That is a glimpse of his wider stewardship, and it carries a distinction this study keeps throughout: a problem Paul was dealing with while in Ephesus was not necessarily a problem in the Ephesian church. Neither passage locates Chloe or reconstructs the messengers’ route. Nor does the record describe a fixed staff. Timothy is visible as Paul’s helper here well before his explicit Ephesian assignment (Acts 19:22; 1 Tim. 1:3); the two references do not tell us he held one office continuously in between.

The name of Jesus is not a religious technique

The sons of Sceva attempt to invoke the Jesus proclaimed by Paul without possessing authority over the evil spirit they confront. Their failure contributes to public reverence for Jesus’ name: Luke says the name was magnified, ἐμεγαλύνετο τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ (19:17). Believers then confess their practices, and practitioners burn magical books valued at fifty thousand pieces of silver (19:11–20). The episode requires attention to who does what: God works through Paul, the exorcists attempt to use the name, and believers acknowledge and renounce their own practices. It is not an account of Paul organizing the seizure and destruction of other people’s books.

The contrast is not between weak magic and stronger magic. Jesus is not another power to manipulate. Luke’s account joins reverence for his name with confession and the renunciation of costly practices.

Conversion has economic consequences

Demetrius, a silversmith, links the growth of Paul’s message with danger to the shrine trade and to Artemis’s public honor. The resulting disturbance gathers in the theater (19:23–41). Luke’s account connects religion, livelihood, and civic loyalty, but it does not depict every official or resident as hostile: the crowd seizes Gaius and Aristarchus rather than Paul; the disciples will not let Paul go in; some provincial officials, the Asiarchs, are his friends and send him a warning; an Alexander put forward by the Jews is shouted down; and the city clerk disperses the crowd and points grievances toward the courts (19:29–41). At this moment other people are protecting Paul rather than waiting for him to protect them.

The public response is mixed: craftsmen fear for their livelihood and the goddess’s honor, while friends try to protect Paul and the clerk directs complaints toward legal proceedings. The same episode reveals opposition, vulnerability, and practical help.

In this snapshot the economic conflict concerns the shrine trade and Artemis’s public honor. The later charge that some treat godliness as a means of gain belongs to First Timothy’s own setting (1 Tim. 6:3–10).

Paul’s own testimony of danger

Paul’s own letters add the personal cost. Writing from Ephesus, he says that, humanly speaking, he fought with beasts there (1 Cor. 15:32). Looking back on affliction in Asia, he recalls a burden beyond his strength, despair of life, and reliance on God who raises the dead (2 Cor. 1:8–11). He also recalls a painful visit to Corinth and a letter written in anguish and tears (2 Cor. 1:15–2:4).

The severity of the distress is explicit. The precise Asian event is not identified. Some of these burdens concern Paul’s wider responsibilities rather than the condition of the Ephesian church.

These years contain sustained opportunity and real vulnerability. Paul teaches and sends coworkers, but he also depends on friends, visitors, and people who intervene to protect him.

In discussing what kept Paul at the work, Wright points to the love of the Messiah, expressed in Paul’s own language about Christ’s love compelling him and the Son of God loving him and giving himself for him (2 Cor. 5:14; Gal. 2:20). For Paul’s stated reason to remain in Ephesus, the open door and many opponents of 1 Corinthians 16:8–9 remain central. Wright on Paul’s motivation

Was Paul imprisoned in Ephesus?

N. T. Wright argues that Paul’s severe affliction in Asia included an imprisonment in Ephesus. He regards the crisis as a major personal ordeal, while acknowledging that the charge and duration of the proposed imprisonment are unknown. Acts narrates the opposition and riot but does not narrate such an imprisonment. Wright interview

What changes under this proposal?

Ephesians would fall within Paul’s Ephesian years, rather than the later Roman setting used in the timeline. The familiar locations and references are being arranged differently; the proposal should not be added to the traditional chronology as though both were one sequence.

Paul as a person within the story

Wright gives the Ephesian period two chapters, “Ephesus I” and “Ephesus II,” in Paul: A Biography. In interviews he stresses the relation between the work, the Corinthian crisis, and Paul’s own distress. His suggestion that deprivation and imprisonment shaped the period is biographical reconstruction. The letters themselves attest both Paul’s intense distress and his reliance on God.

This discussion follows Wright’s published interviews about the biography, listed in the bibliography; the fuller treatment is the biography’s two Ephesus chapters.

Jesus in this record: Acts presents his name as authoritative and his message as life-changing. Paul’s own testimony directs attention to the God who raises the dead, on whom he depends in severe affliction.

04 / ACTS 20

The elders at Miletus: a farewell and a charge

After the disturbance Paul encourages the disciples and leaves for Macedonia (Acts 20:1). Months later, hurrying toward Jerusalem with a delegation that includes the two Asians Tychicus and Trophimus (20:4), he sails past Ephesus to avoid delay and summons its elders to Miletus (20:16–17). The farewell is a later meeting with Ephesian leaders, not the closing speech of the riot. Paul appeals to what they had watched him do: humility, tears, trials, teaching in public and in homes, a message of repentance toward God and faith in Jesus, a ministry received from the Lord (20:18–24).

Paul warns that destructive teachers will enter the community and that men from the elders’ own group will arise, distort the truth, and draw disciples after themselves (20:28–31). Luke calls these leaders elders (πρεσβύτεροι, 20:17) and overseers (ἐπίσκοποι, 20:28). Their responsibility is to care for the flock entrusted to them.

The contrast is Christ-centered. The flock belongs to God and has been acquired through Christ’s blood; leaders do not own it. Paul recalls his humility, tears, teaching, labor, and support for the weak. He leaves the elders not simply with defensive instructions but with the word of grace and the example of Jesus’ generosity (20:18–21, 28, 32–35). Translations differ over the precise wording of 20:28, but the sacrificial basis of the church’s belonging is clear.

Read the farewell alongside the letters to Timothy

Read alongside the later letters to Timothy, the farewell offers useful comparisons in care, teaching, generosity, and Paul’s description of his course. Similarities in wording and subject are noted below.

The speech is more than a warning about wolves. Paul remembers teaching, work, tears, and help for the weak. He commends the elders to God and to the word of grace, then kneels with them in prayer.

A parting between people who know one another, and a gap the records leave

The scene ends with kneeling prayer, weeping, embraces, and grief at Paul’s statement that they will not see his face again (20:25, 36–38). The elders are not an anonymous audience receiving a timeless leadership lecture; Luke depicts people facing the loss of a familiar presence, and he records their sorrow without a biography of any elder or a report of their later decisions.

First Timothy later recalls a charge to remain in Ephesus while Paul was going to Macedonia (1:3). The shared people and entrusted responsibility are explicit. The full intervening itinerary is not.

What this snapshot reveals about Jesus: His self-giving establishes both the value of the church and the pattern of its leadership.
The departure recalled in 1 Timothy 1:3

Acts 19:22 sends Timothy and Erastus ahead to Macedonia before Paul leaves after the disturbance (20:1). First Timothy 1:3 describes Timothy remaining in Ephesus while Paul goes toward Macedonia. These references should not be combined into one event without argument. The traditional reconstruction places the pastoral assignment after a release from Roman custody; other readings place it earlier.

05 / EPHESIANS

The mystery is a new humanity in the exalted Christ

This record: instruction and prayer for the letter’s readers, possibly across several churches. Its vision of life in Christ is not a report that the Ephesian congregation had already achieved that maturity.

Ephesians explains the significance of what the gospel brings into being. God’s purpose is to sum up all things in Christ, ἀνακεφαλαιώσασθαι τὰ πάντα ἐν τῷ Χριστῷ (1:9–10). The risen Jesus is exalted above rule and authority and given as head to the church (1:20–23). The letter addresses how its readers are to understand and live that identity.

A revealed purpose, not privileged secret knowledge

The “mystery” is not something believers are supposed to keep obscure. It is God’s purpose, once hidden and now disclosed. Ephesians 3:6 gives it concrete communal content: Gentiles share the inheritance, belong to the same body, and share the promise in Christ through the gospel. The church displays God’s wisdom even to heavenly powers (3:3–10). The word μυστήριον runs through the letter (1:9; 3:3, 4, 9; 5:32; 6:19) before 1 Timothy uses it twice for the faith the church confesses (3:9, 16).

Acts describes Jews and Greeks hearing the message together. Ephesians unfolds the theological significance: through the cross, Christ reconciles both to God in one body. He does not merely permit different groups to occupy the same room. He creates a shared life in which inherited hostility no longer governs belonging (2:11–18).

God’s dwelling is a reconciled people

This new community is described as God’s household and a holy temple, with Christ as its cornerstone and God dwelling in it by the Spirit (2:19–22). Its members are οἰκεῖοι τοῦ θεοῦ, members of God’s household; 1 Timothy 3:15 will speak of conduct ἐν οἴκῳ θεοῦ, in God’s house. That is a direct conceptual and verbal connection. It also supplies a striking contrast with a city identified in Acts with the guardianship of Artemis’s temple.

The primary scriptural background, however, remains Israel’s story of God dwelling among his people. The temple imagery should not be reduced to a clever local reference to Ephesian architecture. The contrast is that God’s dwelling is being constituted through Christ’s work in human relationships, not secured by the city’s prestige or monumental buildings.

Tim Mackie’s BibleProject classroom notes propose a symmetrical arrangement of Ephesians 1–3: praise and prayer frame the accounts of rescue and Paul’s ministry, with the new household and temple at the center.

Read this way the prayers matter as much as the argument. Paul prays that the community will grasp the power that raised and exalted Jesus (1:19–23), and then that it will be strengthened to know the Messiah’s love with all the saints (3:17–19). The powerful Christ of chapter 1 cannot be detached from the loving Christ of chapter 3, and comprehending the revelation is not the accumulation of information; the letter moves its hearers into gratitude, worship, and a shared life.

Maturity is both doctrinal and loving

Ephesians 4 connects Christ’s gifts to the church with growth beyond deceptive teaching. Yet that maturity is inseparable from truth practiced in love, ἀληθεύοντες ἐν ἀγάπῃ, and from a body building itself up in love (4:11–16). Love is not added after doctrinal maturity; it helps define it. The exhortation to imitate Christ’s self-giving follows naturally (5:1–2).

The second half of the letter shows what that looks like in ordinary practice. Falsehood gives way to truth because believers are members of one another; work becomes a means of sharing with someone in need; corrosive speech is replaced by words that build up; bitterness and vengeance give way to kindness and forgiveness, with Christ’s self-giving as the pattern (4:25–5:2). Those are the same instincts 1 Timothy will require of the household: hospitality, trustworthy speech, care for widows, impartial accountability, generosity. In both letters the practices are not administrative distractions from the mystery; they are what the revelation of Christ looks like when it has a body.

The closing armor passage calls the readers to stand against spiritual powers rather than flesh and blood (6:10–20). Its images draw on Isaiah: the belt of righteousness (Isa. 11:5), the breastplate and helmet (Isa. 59:17), and the good news of peace (Isa. 52:7). The classroom notes read these powers as bound up with, though not reducible to, forces that divide and distort human life. In that reading the community’s resistance includes truthfulness, peace, and faithfulness.

What this snapshot reveals about Jesus: The exalted Lord is also the crucified peacemaker, the head of a united body, and the source of its growth in love.

06 / 1 TIMOTHY

The confession of Christ must become household life

First Timothy directly names Ephesus as Timothy’s assignment: he is to remain there, προσμεῖναι ἐν Ἐφέσῳ (1:3). He must prevent different teaching, including preoccupation with myths and genealogies and a misuse of the law (1:3–7). Further passages identify imposed restrictions on marriage and food, quarrels, and the pursuit of gain (4:1–5; 6:3–10).

Nevertheless, the aim is not merely to produce a better-defended institution. The opening charge already defines its goal as love (1:5). Timothy’s task is to restore the kind of teaching that forms faithful, loving people, not to make argument itself the church’s vocation.

The letter describes teachers whose controversies produce envy, friction, slander, and suspicion and who regard godliness as a means of gain (6:3–5). Timothy’s work also includes prayer, the character of leaders, care for widows, fair treatment of elders, and instruction for wealthy believers.

The edition displays adjacent pairs, an earth–heaven sequence, and concentric pairings. These are proposed reading arrangements. The confession is often understood as hymnic or creedal; its original liturgical setting is not known. See Hymns of the New Covenant.

The confession moves across flesh and spirit, angels and nations, the world and glory. It is larger than a list of church rules because the church’s life arises from the identity and work of Christ. Its ordering is not a simple chronology: worldwide proclamation appears before the final reference to being taken up in glory. The focus is the scope and exaltation of Christ’s story.

“Vindicated in spirit” is read in this edition in the light of resurrection and exaltation. The wording is not identical to the explicit “raised from the dead” in 2 Timothy 2:8.

The practical instructions are not a departure from Christ

The surrounding material shows how the confession becomes visible. The one mediator who gives himself for all grounds prayer for all people, including rulers (2:1–6). Leaders must display self-control, hospitality, gentleness, and freedom from the love of money (3:1–13). God’s good gifts are to be received with gratitude rather than rejected as forbidden (4:1–5). Widows require real care; elders require both appropriate support and fair accountability (5:3–22). Wealthy believers must use their resources generously (6:17–19).

These passages do not all develop their reasoning in identical ways, and some raise interpretive questions beyond this study’s scope. The shared point is that the household claiming allegiance to Jesus must become recognizable by practices consistent with its confession. The letter even ends on that scale: its last word of grace is addressed to ὑμῶν, you all (6:21), a plural benediction on a letter written to one man, a sign that the assembly was in view throughout.

Here Acts helps in three particular ways. The monetary sacrifices of Acts 19 illuminate the cost of changed allegiance. The contrast between rival objects of public confession is theologically useful. It should not be presented as a proven deliberate echo of the riot chant.
What this snapshot reveals about Jesus: The incarnate, vindicated, proclaimed, and exalted Christ is the content of the church’s confession and the ground of its public life.

07 / 2 TIMOTHY

The risen Jesus remains central when ministry becomes costly

Paul writes from Roman custody and expects death. Timothy is probably still serving in Ephesus. Some associates have abandoned Paul; others continue to help, and he testifies that the Lord has stood with him (1:15–18; 4:6–18). The letter addresses Timothy’s teaching responsibility and Paul’s urgent desire for his company.

The three movements follow BibleProject’s 2 Timothy overview; adopted here as a teaching design, not a claim about the letter’s composition.

The calling is relational before it is administrative

Paul begins with Timothy’s grandmother Lois and mother Eunice (1:5). The appeal grows out of shared formation, not a detached succession plan. The first charge is not to be ashamed of the testimony about the Lord or of Paul his prisoner (1:8). The overview explains the desertion of Phygelus and Hermogenes (1:15) through the stigma of association with an accused criminal, a plausible reading the letter does not spell out. Coming to Paul may itself be costly, so the command is to be strengthened in the grace that is in Christ Jesus (2:1): the soldier, athlete, and farmer (2:3–7) each give themselves to something larger than their own ease, and the highest instance is Jesus himself, whose fidelity to the Father led through crucifixion.

The command in 2:8 brings the entire situation back to a person: Μνημόνευε Ἰησοῦν Χριστὸν ἐγηγερμένον ἐκ νεκρῶν, ἐκ σπέρματος Δαυίδ, remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, of David’s seed. The Davidic reference identifies the risen one with Israel’s messianic promise. Paul’s imprisonment does not refute Jesus’ kingship. It creates the setting in which Timothy must learn what fidelity to that king can cost.

πιστὸς ὁ λόγος·Trustworthy is the saying: εἰ γὰρ συναπεθάνομεν, καὶ συζήσομεν·if we died with him, we will also live with him; εἰ ὑπομένομεν, καὶ συμβασιλεύσομεν·if we endure, we will also reign with him; εἰ ἀρνησόμεθα, κἀκεῖνος ἀρνήσεται ἡμᾶς·if we deny him, he also will deny us; εἰ ἀπιστοῦμεν, ἐκεῖνος πιστὸς μένει, ἀρνήσασθαι γὰρ ἑαυτὸν οὐ δύναται.if we are faithless, he remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself. 2 Timothy 2:11–13

Three σύν-verbs bind the hearers’ lives to Christ’s: died-with, live-with, reign-with. The saying holds promise and warning together; its last line must not be read as cancelling the third. Where 1 Timothy 3:16 names the revealed and exalted Christ, this saying asks how his people share his life and remain faithful. That is a theological comparison, not evidence that the two passages had the same liturgical use.

Christ’s resurrection is not an escape from embodied hope

Hymenaeus and Philetus teach that the resurrection has already occurred, and their teaching is upsetting some believers’ faith (2:17–18). Paul’s instruction returns Timothy to Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, and to faithful teaching and patient correction.

What lay behind the resurrection claim?

The letter reports the claim but does not explain its full system. BibleProject’s overview suggests either a philosophical rejection of bodily resurrection or a distortion of Paul’s teaching about resurrection life already begun through the Spirit. Its description of an overly private spirituality is an interpretation of the error, not an additional statement from the letter.

This creates an especially fruitful comparison with Ephesians. Ephesians says believers have already been raised and seated with Christ (2:5–6), yet also speaks of the future redemption of God’s possession and a coming day of redemption (1:13–14; 4:30). The two ages overlap; the age to come has not exhausted its future. Participation in Christ’s risen life now is not the assertion that the whole resurrection hope is exhausted. Second Timothy protects that hope in circumstances where bodies still suffer and death still approaches, just as 1 Timothy protected created goods against teachers who forbade marriage and food (1 Tim. 4:3–5). The two concerns can be read together without asserting that every opponent belonged to one reconstructed movement.

That is why the resurrection-centered approach of the earlier studies is so valuable: resurrection concerns the present shape of faithfulness as well as the future beyond death. It does not justify contempt for ordinary life or imply that a suffering apostle has failed.

Guarding the deposit includes gentleness, and Scripture

Timothy must entrust the message to reliable teachers who can teach others (2:2). He must also reject foolish disputes and correct opponents with patience and gentleness, seeking their recovery rather than their humiliation (2:23–26). The manner of defending the gospel must not contradict its purpose. The vocabulary of guarding is the vocabulary of trust: the good deposit, τὴν καλὴν παραθήκην, is guarded through the Holy Spirit who dwells in us (1:14), and what Paul entrusted to Timothy is to be handed on, not hoarded (2:2).

Paul directs Timothy to the Scriptures he has known from childhood. They can make him wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus and are God-breathed, useful for teaching, reproof, correction, and training in righteousness, equipping the person of God for every good work (3:14–17). The focus here is the role of Scripture in Timothy’s teaching and conduct.

The coat and the books belong in the theological account

Paul asks Timothy to come before winter, to bring the cloak left at Troas and the books, especially the parchments, and to beware Alexander the coppersmith (4:13–15, 21). These details keep the account personal: he wants companionship, warmth, and the materials of his work. At his first defense no one stood by him, but the Lord did; Paul trusts that the Lord will bring him safely into the heavenly kingdom (4:16–18).

The strongest continuity is with Miletus. Before the Ephesian elders Paul spoke of finishing his course (Acts 20:24); to Timothy he can say the course is finished (4:7). His charge is inseparable from the example he has lived.

The closing greetings and travel notices bring familiar names into view. Luke is with Paul; Mark is to come; Tychicus has been sent to Ephesus; Erastus has remained at Corinth; Trophimus has been left ill at Miletus; Prisca, Aquila, and the household of Onesiphorus receive greetings (4:9–21). Several names recur in earlier records, allowing us to recognize relationships without reconstructing every intervening journey.

Identifying names across the records

Hymenaeus in 1 Timothy 1:20 and 2 Timothy 2:17 is plausibly the same opponent. The Alexanders in Acts 19:33, 1 Timothy 1:20, and 2 Timothy 4:14 are not merged into one biography. The two Gaius references are distinguished because Acts 19:29 identifies a Macedonian while Acts 20:4 names a man from Derbe. Revelation’s false apostles and Nicolaitans are not identified with Timothy’s opponents.

BibleProject’s suggestions that Paul was arrested at Troas, that Alexander caused the arrest, and that the women targeted in 2 Timothy were a particular group from 1 Timothy remain historical proposals.

What this snapshot reveals about Jesus: He is the risen Davidic Messiah, the giver of strength, and the faithful Lord who remains present in weakness. His resurrection anchors a hope that can sustain faithful service without making safety the test of his presence.

08 / REVELATION 2

The church that tests teachers is itself examined by Jesus

Revelation announces itself in three forms: an apocalypse, an unveiling of heaven’s perspective on history (1:1); a prophecy, a word from God to his people (1:3); and a letter to seven actual churches in the Roman province of Asia (1:4). BibleProject’s overview draws the consequence this study also adopts: the book’s meaning must first be anchored in John’s time, place, and audience and in the Old Testament imagery it reuses, not in a predictive code. It leaves the precise identity of John open, and so does this page.

John sees the risen Jesus among seven lampstands representing the churches (1:12–20). Ephesus is addressed first. The one walking among the lampstands knows its work and speaks to its present condition. Each of the seven messages addresses a particular church; their problems should not be assigned wholesale to Ephesus.

Jesus commends the Ephesians’ work and endurance. They refuse evil, test false claims to apostolic authority, and persist for his name without giving up (2:2–3). These are genuine features of the church’s life in this assessment.

The warning concerns love, not insufficient activity

Despite that record, Christ indicts the abandonment of their earlier love: τὴν ἀγάπην σου τὴν πρώτην ἀφῆκες (2:4). The object of that love is not specified in the verse. Love for Christ, love for fellow believers, and outward-looking love are closely related possibilities. It is too narrow to make the verse mean only fading religious emotion or only the loss of one particular activity. One earlier record may bear on it: Ephesians 1:15 credits its readers with faith in the Lord Jesus and, in many manuscripts, love toward all the saints, but the earliest witnesses lack the words for love, and the NA28 prints them in brackets. Whether that letter once commended the love Revelation says was abandoned is a question the text leaves open.

The commendation and rebuke stand together. The church’s resistance to error does not cancel the charge that it has abandoned its earlier love. Jesus calls for remembrance, repentance, and the earlier works; he does not explain how the loss of love came about.

Discernment and endurance are recognized, and repentance is still required. The passage does not say that testing teachers caused the loss of love.

Remembering must become repentance and renewed practice

Christ calls the church to remember, repent, and return to its earlier deeds (2:5). The remedy is neither mere nostalgia nor increased feeling. Whatever the exact range of “first love,” the demanded response includes concrete action: τὰ πρῶτα ἔργα ποίησον, do the first works.

The threat to remove the lampstand is severe. Since the lampstand represents the church, this is more than a threat to diminish its public reputation. Christ places its continuing standing as his church under judgment. Its apostolic history does not grant immunity from his present authority. Yet the warning is conditional, and the message ends with a promise to the one who conquers: access to the tree of life (2:7).

The church is commended for enduring for Jesus’ name (2:3). Its subsequent response to his call is not recorded in this message.

Read the message within Revelation’s wider vision

The Lamb defines what conquering means

Each church message ends with a promise to the one who conquers. Revelation 5 presents the Lion of Judah and Root of David as a Lamb standing as though slain. BibleProject’s overview reads this as the key to victory through faithful, self-giving witness. It also reads the numbered people heard in 7:4 and the multitude seen in 7:9 as two portraits of the Lamb’s people. These are interpretive readings of the wider vision, not further reports of events in Ephesus.

What they settle is this: resisting false teaching while abandoning love cannot exhaust the meaning of conquering, because the Lamb conquered by self-giving love. The point illuminates the rebuke of 2:4 without explaining its cause.

The day that examines God’s own people

Revelation reaches its conclusion through repeated depictions of the day of the Lord, the prophets’ name for God’s decisive intervention against evil (Isaiah 2; Joel 2), which the overview reads as three overlapping cycles rather than a linear timetable, a preference this page reports rather than adopts. What matters for Ephesus is a warning older than John. Amos told a people eager for that day that it would be darkness and not light, because Israel had come to reproduce the injustice God opposed (Amos 5:18–24). Belonging to God has never made a community immune to his examination. Acts 20 warns of destructive leadership arising from within; 1 Timothy confronts teachers who treat godliness as gain; Revelation 2 examines a church with a real record of endurance. None of these texts says that Ephesus became Babylon. The comparison concerns accountability within the people of God.

A further comparison concerns trade and allegiance. Craftsmen fear for their livelihood in Acts 19; merchants lament Babylon’s fall in Revelation 18. Both passages use the language of crafts and commerce, including the same word for craftsman (τεχνίτης, Acts 19:24; Rev. 18:22). This permits theological comparison without establishing that John is retelling the Ephesian riot.

What this snapshot reveals about Jesus: The living Lord is present with his people, knows their works, judges them truthfully, calls them back, and promises life. The Lamb who gave himself for them also holds their future, and his victory defines the shape of theirs.

09 / READING TOGETHER

Returning to the church through its records

The records become illuminating when each is allowed to show its own moment. Paul’s sustained work and the elders’ farewell are not the same occasion. Timothy’s first assignment and the circumstances of the second letter are not interchangeable. Jesus’ assessment in Revelation includes both endurance and an urgent call to repent.

The Spirit’s activity

An additional shared feature is the Spirit’s work: forming believers in Acts 19:1–7, appointing overseers in Acts 20:28, constituting God’s dwelling in Ephesians 2:22, sustaining Timothy’s guardianship of the gospel in 2 Timothy 1:14, and speaking to the churches in Revelation 2:7. Revelation and confession

Acts magnifies Jesus’ name; Ephesians explains the mystery; 1 Timothy confesses Christ; 2 Timothy calls for shared fidelity; Revelation discloses the living Lamb.

A people made one

The flock belongs to God (Acts 20); the new humanity is his dwelling (Eph. 2–3); the household must live fittingly (1 Tim. 3); the Lamb’s people include the nations (Rev. 7).

Truth and love

Watchfulness, healthy teaching, Scripture-shaped service, and the call back to love belong together. Discernment serves fidelity to Christ; it does not replace it.

Victory through apparent weakness

Paul’s imprisonment and completed course meet the Lamb’s self-giving pattern. Suffering is not by itself a sign of Jesus’ absence (2 Tim. 2; 4; Rev. 5; 11–12).

The Spirit and formed conduct

The Spirit’s gift, God’s indwelling, the guarded deposit, and God-breathed Scripture support a visible life of service (Acts 19–20; Eph. 2; 2 Tim. 1; 3).

Accountability and future hope

God’s people remain answerable to him. Present resurrection life leads toward vindication, the tree of life, and renewed creation, not escape from ordinary responsibility.

10 / READING NOTES

The gaps in the record, and a later witness

Ignatius of Antioch later addresses the Ephesian church, names its bishop Onesimus, commends its faith and love, and discusses unity and resistance to false teaching (Ephesians 1, 4, 9, 12, 14). His letter is another witness to a living community associated with Paul. It does not document how the church responded to Revelation’s warning or establish that its Onesimus is the man named in Philemon.

How historical and literary proposals are used

Artemis is important to the city’s setting, but First Timothy does not name her. Claims about converted priestesses or the precise origins of an opponent’s ideas need evidence beyond a shared setting.

The study uses a traditional Pauline chronology, identified near the timeline. Proposed literary structures and interpretations of Revelation are attributed where they are discussed. Neither a repeated word nor an attractive parallel establishes direct literary dependence or a chain of historical causes.

The BibleProject materials offer teaching outlines and interpretations. Wright’s proposed imprisonment offers a biographical reconstruction. These resources help us ask questions of the records without replacing what the records actually say.
