01 / THE CLAIM

Six disclosures of one gospel

The church must guard the truth about Jesus in a way that preserves the love, holiness, unity, endurance, and witness that the truth about Jesus is meant to produce.

Read together, Acts 18–20, Ephesians, 1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, and Revelation 2:1–7 offer connected but distinct portraits of a Christian community learning what allegiance to Jesus requires. Acts depicts the gospel establishing a people and disturbing established loyalties. Ephesians explains the new humanity God creates in Christ. First Timothy addresses how that people must live as God’s household. Second Timothy insists that the risen Jesus remains the center when ministry brings suffering, desertion, and approaching death. Revelation presents that same living Lord examining a church that has resisted deception but abandoned its earlier love.

These are not successive versions of Jesus, nor a complete record of one congregation’s uninterrupted development. They are different pastoral disclosures of the implications of the same gospel. Jesus is not replaced by different versions of himself; his identity and work address changing circumstances: conversion, reconciliation, leadership, money, suffering, and the danger of outward faithfulness without love. The governing question is whether the community that confesses Jesus continues to live as the reconciled, Spirit-formed people his death and resurrection create.

The clearest compressed expression of the thesis is 1 Timothy 1:5. Paul has just told Timothy to stop the different teaching, and before he says anything else about the teachers he states what the whole charge is for:

τὸ δὲ τέλος τῆς παραγγελίας ἐστὶν ἀγάπη ἐκ καθαρᾶς καρδίας καὶ συνειδήσεως ἀγαθῆς καὶ πίστεως ἀνυποκρίτου 1 Timothy 1:5

The goal (τέλος) of the charge is love, arising from a pure heart, a good conscience, and sincere faith. Everything else in the letter serves that goal.

Where this study sits

This page develops the central insight of Resurrection as Revelation, Part 3: Resurrection as Formation: the confession of Christ in 1 Timothy 3:16 belongs at the heart of the letter’s practical instructions, not in a separate theological compartment. That study connects the confession with sound doctrine, qualified leaders, household relationships, and public witness. The thirteen-movement reading of 1 Timothy in the print edition, 1 Timothy: A Literary–Literal Edition, is useful as a proposed literary arrangement; the present argument does not depend on proving that particular chiasm. What this page adds is the horizontal view: the same gospel meeting the same city across five books and roughly forty years. Where it borrows an interpretation from BibleProject’s Ephesians classroom notes or its 2 Timothy, Acts, and Revelation overviews, it says so; connections between the books are this study’s own synthesis unless shared vocabulary is shown.

02 / SIX WITNESSES

Six witnesses, one church

A chronological reading is useful, provided that dates and locations are not made more certain than the evidence allows. This study follows the traditional Pauline framework for Ephesians and the Pastoral Letters while acknowledging that their authorship and dating are disputed in scholarship. Under later-authorship proposals these books remain important witnesses to Pauline tradition and church life, but they cannot simply be treated as direct reports from successive years of Paul’s ministry. The dates below concern the events or the traditional letter settings; the Acts entries are not dates for the composition of Acts.

Acts 18:18–19:41 (AD 50s): The community takes shape: teaching in the hall of Tyrannus, the Spirit, the renunciation of magic, and the conflict over Artemis. Paul later describes the stay as three years (20:31).
Acts 20:17–38 (later AD 50s): Paul summons the Ephesian elders to Miletus and warns of destructive leadership rising from within.
Ephesians (early AD 60s, traditionally): Christ’s cosmic lordship, Jew–Gentile reconciliation, and the life of his body.
1 Timothy (early to mid-AD 60s, traditionally): Timothy explicitly remains in Ephesus (1:3) to confront different teaching and order God’s household.
2 Timothy (mid- to late AD 60s, traditionally): Paul faces death; Timothy must guard and transmit the gospel. An Ephesian destination is probable, not explicit.
Revelation 2:1–7 (around AD 95, under the common late dating): The living Jesus commends endurance and discernment but commands a return to love.

Dates are approximate and locate the settings, not stages of maturity. “Traditionally” marks the traditional Pauline chronology; a mid-90s setting for Revelation is the common late dating, not the only proposal.

One row has a live alternative that matters for this study. N. T. Wright places Paul’s Ephesian years around AD 53–56 and argues for an imprisonment in Ephesus during them; on that reconstruction Ephesians, together with Philippians, Colossians, and Philemon, was written from an Ephesian cell in the mid-50s rather than from Rome in the early 60s, which would put the letter inside the first snapshot rather than after the second. This page keeps the traditional setting on the rail and treats Wright’s proposal as an identified alternative, discussed below; the argument of the page does not depend on either date.

Was Timothy still in Ephesus when 2 Timothy was written?

That is the usual traditional reconstruction, and it has substantial support. Nevertheless, 2 Timothy does not repeat the explicit location statement of 1 Timothy 1:3. Its evidence is cumulative: Timothy knows the service Onesiphorus rendered in Ephesus (1:18); Paul has sent Tychicus there (4:12); and Timothy is to greet Prisca, Aquila, and the household of Onesiphorus (4:19). Paul’s request that Timothy come to him (4:9, 21) helps explain the proposal that Tychicus would provide coverage in Timothy’s absence, but the letter does not say that was his assignment.

Prisca and Aquila strengthen the inference. Acts introduces them as refugees from Claudius’s Rome (18:2), settles them in Ephesus with Paul (18:18–19, 26), and 1 Corinthians, written from Ephesus, greets from their house church there (16:8, 19). Romans 16:3–5 finds them back in Rome hosting another house church, if that chapter was addressed to Rome as most hold. A greeting to them in 2 Timothy 4:19 therefore fits an Ephesian destination well, though it cannot by itself prove one: the couple moved.

The appropriate wording

Paul writes to Timothy, probably still serving in Ephesus. Paul’s own Roman imprisonment is more directly indicated, especially by 1:16–17. The claim of a second Roman imprisonment belongs to the traditional reconstruction of his movements; BibleProject’s 2 Timothy overview likewise leaves open whether the letter comes from the house arrest of Acts 28 or from a later arrest, and its proposal that Paul was seized at Troas, where the cloak and books were left (4:13), is a reconstruction rather than something the letter states.

Was Ephesians a circular letter?

Ephesians has a broader, less personally specific character than one might expect from a letter to people Paul knew closely. Important early manuscripts omit “in Ephesus” at 1:1, and Tertullian reports that Marcion’s copy carried the title “to the Laodiceans” (Against Marcion 5.11; 5.17). Accordingly, many interpreters understand it as a circular letter for several churches in Asia Minor. That remains a reconstruction, not a settled identification of the complete original audience.

It can therefore illuminate Ephesian Christianity without every instruction being treated as evidence of a uniquely Ephesian crisis. Similarly, “the church in Ephesus” should not evoke one later-style church building. The house-church greeting in 1 Corinthians 16:19 is the best glimpse we have of its actual setting.

03 / PAUL’S YEARS

Paul’s Ephesian years: the gospel establishes a people

Before the riot there are years of relationships and repeated work, and a letter Paul wrote while he lived there. Acts supplies the public account; 1 Corinthians, written from Ephesus, puts names, visitors, and a house church beside it; 2 Corinthians remembers what those years cost him. Read together they show a period of a life, not only a dramatic episode.

Settling into the work

The story begins with a brief visit. Paul arrives with Priscilla and Aquila, speaks in the synagogue, declines a request to stay longer, and promises to return if God wills; they remain when he leaves (Acts 18:18–21). Other people are already part of the story before he returns. Apollos arrives with a strong knowledge of the Scriptures and an incomplete understanding of baptism; Priscilla and Aquila explain the way of God to him more accurately; the brothers then encourage his journey to Achaia and write ahead to secure his welcome (18:24–28). When Paul returns, Apollos is in Corinth, and Paul finds disciples whose understanding is associated with John’s baptism; they are directed toward Jesus, baptized, and receive the Holy Spirit (19:1–7). That is one reported encounter with about twelve men, not a census of the church.

The initial pattern already combines proclamation and formation. Some people need fuller instruction; others need to renounce practices incompatible with their new allegiance. Apollos’s incomplete understanding is not depicted in the same way as the later teachers’ destructive distortion. The distinction matters: faithful teaching includes patient clarification, not merely identifying opponents.

Acts also gives the stay a shape in time. Three months of synagogue discussion give way, after opposition, to two years of daily teaching in the hall of Tyrannus, followed by a further stay in Asia (19:8–10, 22); Paul’s farewell later recalls the whole as three years of admonishing people night and day with tears (20:31). The three years is not a further period to add to the others. His own recollection fills in what the summary leaves out: teaching in public and from house to house, and working with his hands to meet his own needs and those of his companions (20:20, 34), the trade Acts had identified at Corinth (18:3). Luke describes the word reaching Jews and Greeks throughout the province of Asia (19:10); “Asia” is the Roman province, not the modern continent. Luke names no daughter churches, but the seven churches addressed in Revelation 2–3 all lie in that province, and Ephesus stands first in the list (Rev. 1:11). Ephesus can fairly be called a base for regional work, provided that remains a description of the activities Luke reports rather than a claim to know who founded every nearby congregation.

Why he stayed is not left to a biographer to guess. Writing from the city, Paul tells the Corinthians he will remain in Ephesus until Pentecost because a wide door for effective work has opened to him, and there are many adversaries (1 Cor. 16:8–9). Opportunity and opposition are named in the same breath, and the second is not a reason to leave.

The letter also shows what Paul was carrying while he lived in Ephesus. He has heard from Chloe’s people about divisions in Corinth (1 Cor. 1:11), he answers matters the Corinthians wrote to him about (7:1), and he arranges a collection, approved representatives, and travel (16:1–7), including the possibility of wintering with the Corinthians after passing through Macedonia (16:6). That is a glimpse of his wider stewardship, and it carries a distinction this study keeps throughout: a problem Paul was dealing with while in Ephesus was not necessarily a problem in the Ephesian church. Neither passage locates Chloe or reconstructs the messengers’ route. Nor does the record describe a fixed staff. Timothy is visible as Paul’s helper here well before his explicit Ephesian assignment (Acts 19:22; 1 Tim. 1:3); the two references do not tell us he held one office continuously in between.

The name of Jesus is not a religious technique

The sons of Sceva attempt to invoke the Jesus proclaimed by Paul without possessing authority over the evil spirit they confront. Their failure contributes to public reverence for Jesus’ name: Luke says the name was magnified, ἐμεγαλύνετο τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ (19:17). Believers then confess their practices, and practitioners burn magical books valued at fifty thousand pieces of silver (19:11–20). The episode requires attention to who does what: God works through Paul, the exorcists attempt to use the name, and believers acknowledge and renounce their own practices. It is not an account of Paul organizing the seizure and destruction of other people’s books.

The theological contrast is not between weak magic and stronger magic. Jesus is not another power to manipulate. The narrative presents his authority as demanding allegiance and a changed life. A later letter’s concern for sound teaching therefore belongs to a world where religious language, claimed spiritual power, and actual fidelity could already come apart.

Conversion has economic consequences

Demetrius, a silversmith, links the growth of Paul’s message with danger to the shrine trade and to Artemis’s public honor. The resulting disturbance gathers in the theater (19:23–41). Luke’s account connects religion, livelihood, and civic loyalty, but it does not depict every official or resident as hostile: the crowd seizes Gaius and Aristarchus rather than Paul; the disciples will not let Paul go in; some provincial officials, the Asiarchs, are his friends and send him a warning; an Alexander put forward by the Jews is shouted down; and the city clerk disperses the crowd and points grievances toward the courts (19:29–41). At this moment other people are protecting Paul rather than waiting for him to protect them.

The scene fits a pattern BibleProject’s Acts overview traces across Philippi, Athens, and Ephesus: the announcement that Jesus is king unsettles the civic and religious order, Paul is accused of subverting the Roman way of life, and the officials who examine the matter find no crime to prosecute.

This prepares an important question for 1 Timothy: once believers have rejected an idol, have they also rejected the habit of making religion serve wealth and status? Acts shows threatened income outside the church; 1 Timothy will diagnose the treatment of godliness as financial gain within it (1 Tim. 6:3–10). The texts support this thematic comparison without proving that the later teachers consciously copied Artemis’s institutions.

Paul’s own testimony of danger

Acts is not the only record of what these years cost. From Ephesus Paul writes that, humanly speaking, he fought with beasts there (1 Cor. 15:32); whether the beasts are literal or a figure for human opponents the letter does not say. Looking back from Macedonia he recalls an affliction in Asia so far beyond his strength that he despaired of life itself and felt he had received the sentence of death, which taught him to rely not on himself but on God who raises the dead; he was delivered, and he credits the prayers of others (2 Cor. 1:8–11). That is strong evidence of profound distress in the region. It does not say the event happened in Ephesus, occurred during the riot, or consisted of imprisonment. The same letter remembers a painful visit to Corinth and a letter written out of much affliction and anguish, with many tears (2 Cor. 1:15–2:4), burdens that surrounded the Ephesian period without dating every visit or letter.

The secure distinction is between Paul’s wider personal burdens and the condition of the Ephesian church itself. The early portrait includes sustained opportunity and real vulnerability; the sources do not require a choice between an effective ministry and a deeply distressed minister.

What this snapshot reveals about Jesus: He is the authoritative Lord whose gospel creates a people, defeats rival claims, and reorders concrete loyalties, and the God who raises the dead is the one on whom his most effective worker learns to rely.

04 / WRIGHT

What N. T. Wright adds to the portrait

Wright gives Ephesus two consecutive chapters of Paul: A Biography, “Ephesus I” and “Ephesus II,” places the years around AD 53–56, and reads them as the period that did the most to Paul himself: substantial missionary success joined to personal danger and distress, with the Corinthian crisis running through the same years. He also reads Ephesians as probably a circular letter to several churches in the region, which supports the caution in the timeline above. His contribution here is separated into what the texts state and what he reconstructs from them.

An Ephesian imprisonment, as a reconstruction

Wright’s most distinctive proposal is that Paul was imprisoned in Ephesus and faced a potentially fatal crisis there. He states the proposal with confidence while acknowledging that the charge and the duration are unknown. The clues he builds on are real: the affliction in Asia under a sentence of death (2 Cor. 1:8–10), the beasts at Ephesus (1 Cor. 15:32), and the guest room Paul asks Philemon to prepare (Philemon is usually placed at Colossae, a short journey from Ephesus but a long one from Rome, from which Paul was planning to travel west to Spain; Philem. 22; Rom. 15:24, 28). Acts 19 records opposition and a riot, not an imprisonment. The wording this page uses is therefore: Wright reconstructs an Ephesian imprisonment as the setting for Paul’s severe affliction in Asia. It does not say that Paul was imprisoned in Ephesus as Acts records.

What moves if the proposal is accepted

The reconstruction is consequential rather than decorative. Wright places Philippians, Philemon, Colossians, and Ephesians in the proposed Ephesian imprisonment rather than in the later Roman one. Under his proposal, Ephesians is written from within Paul’s Ephesian years to a wider regional audience, not sent back to Ephesus from Rome years later, and the early-60s row on the rail becomes one option of two. This page does not combine his chronology with the traditional one quietly; it names the proposal, notes what depends on it, and leaves the rail as it stands. The portrait of Paul’s work and relationships in the preceding section does not depend on accepting an Ephesian imprisonment.

Distress taken seriously

Wright does not read 2 Corinthians 1 as a routine remark that ministry is hard. He sees profound distress following a period in which much had appeared to go well, and he considers how imprisonment, physical deprivation, and dependence on others might have contributed, presenting those circumstances as his reconstruction. He then proposes that Paul’s established habits of prayer carried him into a deeper apprehension of the God he already trusted, and he connects that possibility with the concentrated presentations of Christ in Philippians 2 and Colossians 1, which on his dating come from this period. That is Wright’s account of how the experience unfolded; the letters give Paul’s testimony of distress and reliance on God, not a diary confirming the sequence. What the proposal usefully protects is the person doing the work: the steward is living through changing circumstances too, and Paul should not be treated as a fixed narrator standing outside the church’s story, unaffected by opposition, disappointment, or physical danger.

Why he kept going

Asked what kept Paul getting up and continuing, Wright answers with the love of the Messiah, pointing to Paul’s own language: the love of Christ compels us (2 Cor. 5:14), and the Son of God loved me and gave himself for me (Gal. 2:20). That is Wright’s account of Paul’s underlying motivation, not a claim about the Ephesian congregation’s development, and for the narrower question of why Paul stayed in this city, 1 Corinthians 16:8–9 remains the direct evidence. Wright’s stated aim for the biography is close to the method of this page: to hear each letter as what Paul needed to say to a particular congregation at a particular moment, rather than as a detached essay.

What the biography does not do

It makes the Paul-era snapshots more detailed and more human: the duration of the stay, the coworkers, the responsibilities beyond Ephesus, and Paul’s own experience of danger and dependence. It does not establish what happened under Timothy or explain Jesus’ later assessment in Revelation. Those remain separate records to examine, and no later Ephesian problem is tied back to these years on Wright’s authority.

05 / ACTS 20

Paul’s farewell becomes a framework for pastoral care

After the disturbance Paul encourages the disciples and leaves for Macedonia (Acts 20:1). Months later, hurrying toward Jerusalem with a delegation that includes the two Asians Tychicus and Trophimus (20:4), he sails past Ephesus to avoid delay and summons its elders to Miletus (20:16–17). The farewell is a later meeting with Ephesian leaders, not the closing speech of the riot. Paul appeals to what they had watched him do: humility, tears, trials, teaching in public and in homes, a message of repentance toward God and faith in Jesus, a ministry received from the Lord (20:18–24).

Paul’s warning occurs at Miletus, where he summons the elders from Ephesus. It is not merely a prediction that outsiders will attack the church. Destructive teachers may arise from within the elders’ own circle, redirecting disciples toward themselves (Acts 20:17, 28–31). Luke calls the same men elders (πρεσβύτεροι, 20:17) and overseers (ἐπίσκοποι, 20:28), the same overlap the letters to Timothy and Titus assume (1 Tim. 3:1–2; 5:17; Titus 1:5–7).

The contrast is Christ-centered. The flock belongs to God and has been acquired through Christ’s blood; leaders do not own it. Paul recalls his humility, tears, teaching, labor, and support for the weak. He leaves the elders not simply with defensive instructions but with the word of grace and the example of Jesus’ generosity (20:18–21, 28, 32–35). Translations differ over the precise wording of 20:28, but the sacrificial basis of the church’s belonging is clear.

Acts 20 consequently should not be reduced to “watch for wolves.” It also asks what a shepherd looks like: one who tells the truth, refuses exploitation, weeps over people, and gives rather than consumes. That description anticipates Revelation’s refusal to separate discernment from love.

A parting between people who know one another, and a gap the records leave

The scene ends with kneeling prayer, weeping, embraces, and grief at Paul’s statement that they will not see his face again (20:25, 36–38). The elders are not an anonymous audience receiving a timeless leadership lecture; Luke depicts people facing the loss of a familiar presence, and he records their sorrow without a biography of any elder or a report of their later decisions.

That statement also leaves a gap this page keeps open. First Timothy 1:3 has Paul urging Timothy to remain in Ephesus as Paul himself was going to Macedonia, a setting that matches no moment in Acts and sits uneasily beside the farewell’s expectation. The traditional reconstruction fills the gap with a release from the Roman custody of Acts 28 and a later return to the region; a different reading fits 1:3 to an earlier departure. The records themselves do not say. What they do state is the direct link of person, place, and entrusted responsibility: the elders charged at Miletus, and Timothy later left in the same city with a charge of his own (1 Tim. 1:3, 18).

What this snapshot reveals about Jesus: His self-giving establishes both the value of the church and the pattern of its leadership.

06 / EPHESIANS

The mystery is a new humanity in the exalted Christ

Ephesians explains the significance of what the gospel has brought into being. God’s purpose is to sum up all things in Christ, ἀνακεφαλαιώσασθαι τὰ πάντα ἐν τῷ Χριστῷ (1:9–10). The risen Jesus is exalted above every rival rule and authority and given as head to the church (1:20–23). In an Ephesian setting the language resonates with the struggle over spiritual allegiance in Acts 19, and the letter’s closing picture of a conflict with rulers, authorities, and cosmic powers (6:12) reads naturally in a city where the name of Jesus had just been tested against spirits and magic. The significance of that language is not limited to one city, however.

A revealed purpose, not privileged secret knowledge

The “mystery” is not something believers are supposed to keep obscure. It is God’s purpose, once hidden and now disclosed. Ephesians 3:6 gives it concrete communal content: Gentiles share the inheritance, belong to the same body, and share the promise in Christ through the gospel. The church displays God’s wisdom even to heavenly powers (3:3–10). The word μυστήριον runs through the letter (1:9; 3:3, 4, 9; 5:32; 6:19) before 1 Timothy uses it twice for the faith the church confesses (3:9, 16).

Acts describes Jews and Greeks hearing the message together. Ephesians unfolds the theological significance: through the cross, Christ reconciles both to God in one body. He does not merely permit different groups to occupy the same room. He creates a shared life in which inherited hostility no longer governs belonging (2:11–18).

God’s dwelling is a reconciled people

This new community is described as God’s household and a holy temple, with Christ as its cornerstone and God dwelling in it by the Spirit (2:19–22). Its members are οἰκεῖοι τοῦ θεοῦ, members of God’s household; 1 Timothy 3:15 will speak of conduct ἐν οἴκῳ θεοῦ, in God’s house. That is a direct conceptual and verbal connection. It also supplies a striking contrast with a city identified in Acts with the guardianship of Artemis’s temple.

The primary scriptural background, however, remains Israel’s story of God dwelling among his people. The temple imagery should not be reduced to a clever local reference to Ephesian architecture. The contrast is that God’s dwelling is being constituted through Christ’s work in human relationships, not secured by the city’s prestige or monumental buildings.

The letter’s own design supports reading 2:19–22 as a center rather than an aside. BibleProject’s Ephesians classroom notes (Tim Mackie) lay out chapters 1–3 as a symmetry in which praise surrounds prayer, the prayers surround the accounts of rescue and of Paul’s ministry, and the new household and temple stand in the middle. It is a proposed literary reading, not an additional event in Ephesian history, and the argument here does not depend on it; but it shows why the question of who this people is in Christ arrives at God’s dwelling.

Maturity is both doctrinal and loving

Ephesians 4 connects Christ’s gifts to the church with growth beyond deceptive teaching. Yet that maturity is inseparable from truth practiced in love, ἀληθεύοντες ἐν ἀγάπῃ, and from a body building itself up in love (4:11–16). Love is not added after doctrinal maturity; it helps define it. The exhortation to imitate Christ’s self-giving follows naturally (5:1–2).

The second half of the letter shows what that looks like in ordinary practice. Falsehood gives way to truth because believers are members of one another; work becomes a means of sharing with someone in need; corrosive speech is replaced by words that build up; bitterness and vengeance give way to kindness and forgiveness, with Christ’s self-giving as the pattern (4:25–5:2). Those are the same instincts 1 Timothy will require of the household: hospitality, trustworthy speech, care for widows, impartial accountability, generosity. In both letters the practices are not administrative distractions from the mystery; they are what the revelation of Christ looks like when it has a body.

The closing armor belongs to the same picture (6:10–20). Its equipment is drawn from Isaiah’s portrayal of God and his anointed: righteousness as a belt (Isa. 11:5), the breastplate of righteousness and helmet of salvation (Isa. 59:17), feet fitted with the good news of peace (Isa. 52:7). The classroom notes read the powers behind the conflict as bound up with, though not reducible to, the social forces that divide and distort human life. On that reading the battle in Ephesus did not end with the burned books of Acts 19; it continues in whether the reconciled people remain truthful, peaceful, and united. Other people are not the enemy, which is why a hostile manner of defending the gospel, the very thing 2 Timothy 2:24–25 forbids, is a defeat in this armor rather than a use of it.

What this snapshot reveals about Jesus: The exalted Lord is also the crucified peacemaker, the head of a united body, and the source of its growth in love.

07 / 1 TIMOTHY

The confession of Christ must become household life

First Timothy directly names Ephesus as Timothy’s assignment: he is to remain there, προσμεῖναι ἐν Ἐφέσῳ (1:3). He must prevent different teaching, including preoccupation with myths and genealogies and a misuse of the law (1:3–7). Further passages identify imposed restrictions on marriage and food, quarrels, and the pursuit of gain (4:1–5; 6:3–10).

Nevertheless, the aim is not merely to produce a better-defended institution. The opening charge already defines its goal as love (1:5). Timothy’s task is to restore the kind of teaching that forms faithful, loving people, not to make argument itself the church’s vocation.

Why 3:14–16 belongs at the center

The purpose statement concerns conduct in God’s household, the church of the living God, pillar and buttress of the truth (3:14–15). The confession about Christ follows immediately. Its six clauses are given here in the Greek with the English of 1 Timothy: A Literary–Literal Edition, so that the wording matches the print and web editions of the letter.

The practical instructions are not a departure from Christ

The surrounding material shows how the confession becomes visible. The one mediator who gives himself for all grounds prayer for all people, including rulers (2:1–6). Leaders must display self-control, hospitality, gentleness, and freedom from the love of money (3:1–13). God’s good gifts are to be received with gratitude rather than rejected as forbidden (4:1–5). Widows require real care; elders require both appropriate support and fair accountability (5:3–22). Wealthy believers must use their resources generously (6:17–19).

These passages do not all develop their reasoning in identical ways, and some raise interpretive questions beyond this study’s scope. The shared point is that the household claiming allegiance to Jesus must become recognizable by practices consistent with its confession. The letter even ends on that scale: its last word of grace is addressed to ὑμῶν, you all (6:21), a plural benediction on a letter written to one man, because the assembly was always in view.

08 / 2 TIMOTHY

The risen Jesus remains central when ministry becomes costly

Second Timothy changes the immediate setting without changing the gospel. Paul is imprisoned in Rome and expects death. Some have abandoned him; others continue to serve, and the Lord has not abandoned him (1:15–18; 4:6–18). His statement that those in Asia have turned away from him should not be inflated into the claim that every Asian Christian has renounced Jesus. BibleProject’s overview calls it Paul’s final and most personal letter and reads its design in three movements; the outline is its teaching design, adopted here because it keeps the personal and the pastoral together.

The calling is relational before it is administrative

Paul begins with Timothy’s grandmother Lois and mother Eunice (1:5). The appeal grows out of shared formation, not a detached succession plan. The first charge is not to be ashamed of the testimony about the Lord or of Paul his prisoner (1:8). The overview explains the desertion of Phygelus and Hermogenes (1:15) through the stigma of association with an accused criminal, a plausible reading the letter does not spell out. Coming to Paul may itself be costly, so the command is to be strengthened in the grace that is in Christ Jesus (2:1): the soldier, athlete, and farmer (2:3–7) each give themselves to something larger than their own ease, and the highest instance is Jesus himself, whose fidelity to the Father led through crucifixion.

The command in 2:8 brings the entire situation back to a person: Μνημόνευε Ἰησοῦν Χριστὸν ἐγηγερμένον ἐκ νεκρῶν, ἐκ σπέρματος Δαυίδ, remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, of David’s seed. The Davidic reference identifies the risen one with Israel’s messianic promise. Paul’s imprisonment does not refute Jesus’ kingship. It creates the setting in which Timothy must learn what fidelity to that king can cost.

Three σύν-verbs bind the hearers’ lives to Christ’s: died-with, live-with, reign-with. The saying holds promise and warning together; its last line must not be read as cancelling the third. Where 1 Timothy 3:16 names the revealed and exalted Christ, this saying asks how his people share his life and remain faithful. That is a theological comparison, not evidence that the two passages had the same liturgical use.

Christ’s resurrection is not an escape from embodied hope

Hymenaeus and Philetus say the resurrection has already occurred and thereby unsettle others’ faith (2:17–18). The precise system behind their claim is not explained. The overview offers two possibilities without deciding: a Greek-philosophical rejection of bodily resurrection in favor of spiritual experience, or a distortion of Paul’s own teaching that resurrection life begins now through the Spirit. On either reading the future hope of resurrection and new creation is being displaced by a privatized spirituality detached from daily life. That reconstruction must remain distinguished from the assertion the text actually reports.

This creates an especially fruitful comparison with Ephesians. Ephesians says believers have already been raised and seated with Christ (2:5–6), yet also speaks of the future redemption of God’s possession and a coming day of redemption (1:13–14; 4:30). The two ages overlap; the age to come has not exhausted its future. Participation in Christ’s risen life now is not the assertion that the whole resurrection hope is exhausted. Second Timothy protects that hope in circumstances where bodies still suffer and death still approaches, just as 1 Timothy protected created goods against teachers who forbade marriage and food (1 Tim. 4:3–5). The two concerns can be read together without asserting that every opponent belonged to one reconstructed movement.

That is why the resurrection-centered approach of the earlier studies is so valuable: resurrection concerns the present shape of faithfulness as well as the future beyond death. It does not justify contempt for ordinary life or imply that a suffering apostle has failed.

Guarding the deposit includes gentleness, and Scripture

Timothy must entrust the message to reliable teachers who can teach others (2:2). He must also reject foolish disputes and correct opponents with patience and gentleness, seeking their recovery rather than their humiliation (2:23–26). The manner of defending the gospel must not contradict its purpose. The vocabulary of guarding is the vocabulary of trust: the good deposit, τὴν καλὴν παραθήκην, is guarded through the Holy Spirit who dwells in us (1:14), and what Paul entrusted to Timothy is to be handed on, not hoarded (2:2).

The positive alternative to word-battles is the Scriptures Timothy has known from childhood, the Scriptures of Israel in his setting. Paul distinguishes their nature from their purpose. They are θεόπνευστος, God-breathed (3:16), the Spirit’s guidance of the writers; and they are able to make one wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus (3:15) and useful for teaching, reproof, correction, and training in righteousness so that God’s people are equipped for every good work (3:16–17). The passage is not used here to settle the later boundaries of the canon. Its point in the letter is formative: Scripture leads to Jesus and prepares people to do good, which is exactly the outcome 1 Timothy 1:5 required of teaching.

The coat and the books belong in the theological account

Paul asks Timothy to come before winter (4:21), to bring the cloak he left at Troas, and the books, above all the parchments (4:13), and to beware Alexander the coppersmith (4:14–15). The overview’s suggestions that the arrest happened at Troas and that Alexander was responsible remain proposals. But the details themselves keep the letter from becoming an abstract celebration of suffering: the messenger still needs warmth, friends, and the materials of his work. At his first defense no one stood by him, yet the Lord stood by him and strengthened him (4:16–17), and he expects to be brought safely into the heavenly kingdom, not necessarily out of the prison (4:18). Risk, sacrifice, and tension are not signs of Jesus’ absence; in the overview’s phrase, it is precisely in those moments that his love and faithfulness become tangible.

The strongest continuity is with Miletus. Before the Ephesian elders Paul spoke of finishing his course (Acts 20:24); to Timothy he can say the course is finished (4:7). His charge is inseparable from the example he has lived.

The closing register of names (4:9–21) is the richest list of people in any of the six records, and several of them tie this last letter back to the first snapshot. Demas has deserted, in love with the present age; Crescens and Titus have gone elsewhere; only Luke is with Paul; Mark is to be brought; Tychicus, one of the two Asians on the Jerusalem delegation (Acts 20:4) and the carrier of Ephesians (6:21–22), has been sent to Ephesus (4:12); Erastus, sent from Ephesus to Macedonia with Timothy years before (Acts 19:22), has stayed at Corinth, and Trophimus the Ephesian (Acts 20:4; 21:29) has been left ill at Miletus, the place of the farewell (4:20); Prisca and Aquila and the household of Onesiphorus are greeted (4:19). The list does not make these people interchangeable or supply their later stories. It does show that the network of Paul’s Ephesian years is still the network of his last letter.

09 / REVELATION 2

The church that tests teachers is itself examined by Jesus

Revelation announces itself in three forms: an apocalypse, an unveiling of heaven’s perspective on history (1:1); a prophecy, a word from God to his people (1:3); and a letter to seven actual churches in the Roman province of Asia (1:4). BibleProject’s overview draws the consequence this study also adopts: the book’s meaning must first be anchored in John’s time, place, and audience and in the Old Testament imagery it reuses, not in a predictive code. It leaves the precise identity of John open, and so does this page.

John sees the risen Jesus among seven lampstands, which Revelation identifies with the churches (1:12–20; 2:1). Ephesus is addressed first. Jesus is not merely the subject of earlier preaching or the center of a remembered confession; he now speaks as the one present among the lamps. A community Ephesians had called God’s dwelling is visited by the Lord who walks among the lampstands. The overview’s summary of the seven churches’ pressures (affluence and apathy, moral compromise, harassment, persecution) describes the whole setting; Ephesus’s own condition must be read from 2:1–7.

Jesus commends substantial faithfulness. The Ephesians work, endure, refuse evil, and test people who falsely claim apostolic authority. They have suffered for his name without giving up (2:2–3). Read alongside Acts 20 and the letters to Timothy, this suggests that concern for discerning true from false teaching has remained an important part of the community’s life. The texts do not establish an uninterrupted institutional history or prove that the same people were involved throughout.

The warning concerns love, not insufficient activity

Despite that record, Christ indicts the abandonment of their earlier love: τὴν ἀγάπην σου τὴν πρώτην ἀφῆκες (2:4). The object of that love is not specified in the verse. Love for Christ, love for fellow believers, and outward-looking love are closely related possibilities. It is too narrow to make the verse mean only fading religious emotion or only the loss of one particular activity.

This is where the earlier texts sharpen the diagnosis. Ephesians connected resistance to deception with growth in love (4:14–16) and prayed for strength to know Christ’s love together (3:17–19). First Timothy made love the purpose of the charge (1:5). Second Timothy required love and gentleness in guarding and teaching the gospel (1:13; 2:22–26). Revelation does not introduce love as a replacement for doctrinal care. It confronts a church whose doctrinal care no longer suffices as evidence of complete faithfulness, and it exposes a failure within an existing calling, not a newly introduced requirement.

We should not claim to know why the Ephesians abandoned their first love. The passage does not say that exposing false teachers caused it. The theological warning is nevertheless clear: successful resistance to error is not, by itself, the whole of faithfulness to Jesus.

Remembering must become repentance and renewed practice

Christ calls the church to remember, repent, and return to its earlier deeds (2:5). The remedy is neither mere nostalgia nor increased feeling. Whatever the exact range of “first love,” the demanded response includes concrete action: τὰ πρῶτα ἔργα ποίησον, do the first works.

The threat to remove the lampstand is severe. Since the lampstand represents the church, this is more than a threat to diminish its public reputation. Christ places its continuing standing as his church under judgment. Its apostolic history does not grant immunity from his present authority. Yet the warning is conditional, and the message ends with a promise to the one who conquers: access to the tree of life (2:7).

There is a final continuity in Jesus’ name. In Acts 19:17 the name is magnified; in Revelation 2:3 the church endures διὰ τὸ ὄνομά μου, for that name. Jesus affirms that endurance while insisting that bearing his name must still include the love appropriate to him.

The Lamb defines what conquering means

Every message to the seven churches ends with a promise “to the one who conquers,” and the overview asks the right question: why is faithfulness described as conquering at all? The rest of the book is the answer. John hears of the Lion of Judah and the Root of David who has conquered, and turns to see a Lamb standing as though slain (5:5–6). Hearing and seeing diverge again in chapter 7: he hears the number of a sealed Israelite army and sees a multitude from every nation (7:4–9). The Old Testament’s conquering king is disclosed as the crucified and risen Messiah, and his army as the nations redeemed by his blood, whose conquest is faithful witness rather than the killing of enemies.

The day that examines God’s own people

Revelation reaches its ends through repeated depictions of the day of the Lord, the prophets’ name for God’s decisive intervention against evil (Isaiah 2; Joel 2), which the overview reads as three overlapping cycles rather than a linear timetable, a preference this page reports rather than adopts. What matters for Ephesus is a warning older than John. Amos told a people eager for that day that it would be darkness and not light, because Israel had come to reproduce the injustice God opposed (Amos 5:18–24). Belonging to God has never made a community immune to his examination. Acts 20 warns of destructive leadership arising from within; 1 Timothy confronts teachers who treat godliness as gain; Revelation 2 examines a church with a real record of endurance. None of these texts says that Ephesus became Babylon. The comparison concerns accountability within the people of God.

Babylon does supply one striking echo. Revelation’s Babylon is the nation that exalts its own economic and military security into a god (17–18), and when it falls its merchants mourn and no craftsman of any craft will be found in it again (18:11–22). In Ephesus it was the craftsmen, τεχνῖται, whose trade Demetrius said the gospel endangered (Acts 19:24–27); the word for craftsman, τεχνίτης, recurs in the dirge over Babylon (18:22). That is a thematic echo in shared vocabulary, not a claim that John had Acts 19 in mind. It does show that the question Acts raised, whether religion may serve wealth, is the question Revelation presses on every human kingdom.

What this snapshot reveals about Jesus: The living Lord is present with his people, knows their works, judges them truthfully, calls them back, and promises life. The Lamb who gave himself for them also holds their future, and his victory defines the shape of theirs.

10 / ONE PORTRAIT

The same gospel, changing pressures

This is not a simple story of decline. Acts already includes misunderstanding and conflict; First Timothy already insists on love; Revelation still recognizes genuine endurance. A more faithful synthesis is that the same gospel must continually form the same kinds of fidelity under changing pressures.

The Spirit’s activity

An additional shared feature is the Spirit’s work: forming believers in Acts 19:1–7, appointing overseers in Acts 20:28, constituting God’s dwelling in Ephesians 2:22, sustaining Timothy’s guardianship of the gospel in 2 Timothy 1:14, and speaking to the churches in Revelation 2:7. The church is not left to preserve its inheritance through vigilance alone.

Six through lines

Revelation and confession

Acts magnifies Jesus’ name; Ephesians explains the mystery; 1 Timothy confesses Christ; 2 Timothy calls for shared fidelity; Revelation discloses the living Lamb.

A people made one

The flock belongs to God (Acts 20); the new humanity is his dwelling (Eph. 2–3); the household must live fittingly (1 Tim. 3); the Lamb’s people include the nations (Rev. 7).

Truth and love

Watchfulness, healthy teaching, Scripture-shaped service, and the call back to love belong together. Discernment serves fidelity to Christ; it does not replace it.

Victory through apparent weakness

Paul’s imprisonment and completed course meet the Lamb’s self-giving pattern. Suffering is not by itself a sign of Jesus’ absence (2 Tim. 2; 4; Rev. 5; 11–12).

The Spirit and formed conduct

The Spirit’s gift, God’s indwelling, the guarded deposit, and God-breathed Scripture support a visible life of service (Acts 19–20; Eph. 2; 2 Tim. 1; 3).

Accountability and future hope

God’s people remain answerable to him. Present resurrection life leads toward vindication, the tree of life, and renewed creation, not escape from ordinary responsibility.

11 / CAUTIONS

What the evidence does and does not say

Revelation’s warning should not be turned into an undocumented claim that the Ephesian church immediately disappeared. Roughly a decade or two later, Ignatius of Antioch addresses a church in Ephesus, praises its faith and love, and discusses its resistance to false teaching and its need for unity. He names its bishop, Onesimus (Ephesians 1), reports that travelling teachers with false doctrine were refused a hearing (9), and calls the Ephesians fellow-initiates of Paul, who remembers them in his letters (12). His letter does not prove that Revelation’s warning produced a recovery, and it does not identify his Onesimus with the slave of Philemon, but it prevents an overly neat story ending in immediate extinction.

Artemis also provides context, not a universal explanation. First Timothy never names the goddess. Claims about converted priestesses, the origins of the dietary restrictions, or the identity of particular opponents require additional evidence. Demonstrable textual connections should remain distinct from attractive historical reconstructions.

The BibleProject materials used on this page are teaching overviews, and they are cited as such. They do not settle the authorship debate, prove the cause of Ephesus’s lost love, or document its response to the warning; their proposed structures (the design of Ephesians 1–3, the three movements of 2 Timothy, the three cycles of Revelation) are readings, and their historical suggestions (an arrest at Troas, Alexander’s responsibility, wealthy women as the teachers’ targets) are reconstructions. Where a reading is attributed to them, the attribution is the point.

The final synthesis is therefore both Christological and pastoral. Jesus does not merely supply information for the church to defend. He creates it through the gospel, reconciles its people through the cross, gives it life through his resurrection, sustains its witnesses in suffering, and retains the right to call it to repentance.

Ephesus’s continuing question is whether the community that knows what to say about Jesus still lives in a way that makes his character visible. Its calling is not to choose between truth and love. It is to remain faithful to the Christ in whom truth and love belong together.
