TORAH / VISUAL STUDY

The TabernacleThree zones, one direction

Sacred space · Sin offering · Day of Atonement

An ordinary Israelite's sin offering (Lev 4:27–35) stops at the bronze altar. On the Day of Atonement (Lev 16) the high priest alone carries blood through every zone to the mercy seat. The tabernacle has one court (Ex 27:9–19); multiple courts belong to the later temples.

01 / WORSHIPER’S WALK

The worshiper's walk: bringing a sin offering

Lev 4:27–35 with 1:11; 3:14–16; 6:24–30. Gray steps are yours; blue steps are the priest's. You never pass the altar. Select a numbered station to highlight its explanation.

1In the camp. The sin "is made known to him" (4:28). He chooses a female goat or ewe lamb without defect. He must be ritually clean to enter.
2Through the east gate. Leading the animal past the embroidered screen into the court, "before the LORD" (4:4 pattern).
3North of the altar. He lays his hand on its head and kills it himself where burnt offerings are killed (4:29; 1:11). The priest catches the blood.
4Blood. Priest dips a finger and puts blood on the four horns; the rest is poured at the altar's base (4:30). The blood stops here.
5Fat. Fat over the entrails, the two kidneys with their fat and the lobe of the liver are burned on the altar (4:31; 3:14–15).
6Forgiven. "The priest shall make atonement for him, and he shall be forgiven" (4:31). No part of this offering is his; the text does not say when he leaves.
7–9The priest's cleanup. The meat is "most holy": eaten by male priests only, only in the court (6:26, 29). Earthen pot broken, bronze pot scoured and rinsed, spattered garments washed in a holy place (6:27–28). Ashes carried outside the camp (6:10–11). Hide: unassigned for this offering (cf. 7:8). If the blood had gone inside the tent, nothing is eaten and the carcass is burned outside the camp (6:30; 4:11–12).

02 / TWO OFFERINGS

Two sin offerings side by side

Same court, same altar, same word (ḥaṭṭaʾt). What changes is who sinned, and therefore how deep the blood goes and where the carcass ends up.

1 Blood on the altar's horns, the rest at its base (4:30).
2 Fat burned on the altar (4:31).
3 Meat eaten by the priests in the court (6:26). The meat stays in the court; only the ashes go out, to a clean place outside the camp (6:10–11).
1 Blood carried into the tent: sprinkled seven times before the veil and put on the horns of the incense altar (4:6–7, 17–18).
2 The rest poured at the base of the bronze altar; fat burned there (4:7, 10).
3 Hide, flesh and dung carried outside the camp and burned in a clean place (4:11–12, 21). Nothing is eaten (6:30).
Rule of thumb: From Lev 4 and 6:30: the further in the blood goes, the further out the body goes, and the priests eat only what stayed in the court. The Day of Atonement is the extreme case of the right-hand pattern: blood to the mercy seat, carcasses burned outside the camp (16:14–15, 27).

03 / INNER ROOM

Inside the Holy of Holies: what the high priest would see

Built from the construction texts. A gold room, hung with cherubim, with no lamp.

Eden patterns. Cherubim on every soft surface: the ceiling curtains (Ex 26:1), the veil (26:31) and the mercy seat (25:18–20). Cherubim guard the way back into Eden (Gen 3:24); here they stand at the veil and over the ark, and the way in is from the east (Morales 2012; Beale 2004; Stordalen 2000). Gold everywhere (26:29). The trees, flowers and gourds carved on the walls belong to Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 6:29–35), not the tent; the tent's tree is the lampstand in the Holy Place (Ex 25:31–36).
No lamp. The lampstand stands outside the veil (Ex 27:21). Under four layers of coverings the inner room would be dark; the text does not describe its brightness. What is prescribed is a censer of burning coals from the altar and a cloud of incense that covers the mercy seat, "for I will appear in the cloud over the mercy seat" (Lev 16:2, 12–13). Dwelling in thick darkness is God's choice (Ex 20:21; 1 Kgs 8:12).
Between Days of Atonement. No one enters (16:2). Maintenance of the space is the yearly blood rite itself (16:16, 33). When the camp moves, Aaron and his sons go in, take down the veil and wrap the ark in it, then goatskin, then a blue cloth; poles are inserted and the Kohathites carry it without touching or seeing it (Num 4:5–6, 15, 20). Gershonites carry the curtains, Merarites the frames (Num 4:24–33); Levites set it up again (Num 1:51).

04 / CONSTRUCTION

Three things up close

The bronze altar, the inner curtains, and what actually formed the courtyard boundary. Drawn to the measurements in Exodus 26–27 and 38.

Acacia wood boxed with bronze, about 7½ ft square and 4½ ft high, hollow (27:8). Four horns of one piece with it at the corners, where the sin-offering blood goes (Lev 4:30). A bronze grating hangs under a ledge that runs around it halfway up, with four rings at its corners for the carrying poles (27:4–7). Its tools are all bronze: ash pots, shovels, basins, forks, fire pans (27:3). No steps (Ex 20:26). The fire is never allowed to go out (Lev 6:12–13).
Ten linen curtains, each about 42 × 6 ft, with cherubim woven in blue, purple and scarlet yarn "skillfully worked" (26:1). Sewn into two sets of five; fifty blue loops on each edge and fifty gold clasps join them, so the whole is one tent (26:3–6). Draped over the gold frames, they form the ceiling of both rooms and hang down the outside; from inside the Holy of Holies you look up into cherubim. The veil is the same fabric and pattern (26:31). Over them, unseen: goats' hair, rams' skins and an outer hide the ESV renders "goatskins" (Hebrew taḥash, meaning uncertain) (26:7–14).
Not a wall: a fence of fine twisted linen hangings about 7½ ft high, hung from silver hooks on 60 pillars (material not stated) set 5 cubits apart in bronze bases; the pillar tops are capped with silver and linked by silver bands (27:10–11, 17; 38:17, 19). Cords and bronze pegs stake them out (27:19; 35:18). South and north sides 100 cubits with 20 pillars each; west 50 cubits with 10; east 50 cubits with 15 cubits of hangings on 3 pillars either side of a 20-cubit gate screen in blue, purple and scarlet on 4 pillars (27:12–16). No Israelite could see in; only the tent top showed above the linen.

05 / CEILING

The ceiling over the Holy of Holies

Worked out from Ex 26:1–6 and 26:33. Left: how the one joined sheet of ten curtains lies over the frames. Right: what you would see looking up from beside the ark.

What the text fixes. Ten curtains of fine twisted linen with blue, purple and scarlet, "with cherubim skillfully worked," each 28 × 4 cubits (26:1–2). Five sewn to five; fifty blue loops on each joining edge and fifty gold clasps join the two halves into one tabernacle (26:3–6). The joined sheet is 40 × 28 cubits; the frames are 30 long, 10 wide, 10 high (26:16–25).

So the geometry follows

The first five curtains cover the 20‑cubit Holy Place; the clasp line falls exactly at 20 cubits, and the veil is hung "under the clasps" (26:33). The second five cover the 10‑cubit Holy of Holies and fold 10 cubits down the west wall. Across the width, 28 cubits over a 10 + 10 + 10 path leaves the curtain 1 cubit off the ground outside. From inside the Holy of Holies the ceiling shows two seams and a row of gold clasps along the veil.

What it leaves open

The text gives no size, number, pose or spacing for the cherubim, and does not say whether the linen was also visible between the gold frames. The figures here follow ancient Near Eastern art, where cherub-like guardians are winged, human-faced lions set in facing pairs beside a stylized palm; Solomon's temple used the same pairing, "cherubim and palm trees" (1 Kgs 6:29; Ezek 41:18–19). Exodus itself gives only "cherubim"; upright winged human figures are the other form scholars propose, and the palms are borrowed from the temple, not prescribed for the tent. The seams, colors, clasps and proportions are from the text; the motif is a reconstruction (Eichler, “What Kind of Creatures Are the Cherubim?”).

06 / BLOOD DEPTH

Six zones, and how far the blood goes

The full gradient from wilderness to mercy seat, west on the left. Each bar is one kind of sin offering: it starts where the sinner stands and ends where the blood is applied. Whoever stands closer to the center pollutes deeper, so the blood must reach deeper.

where the sinner stands blood applied carcass burned outside the camp live goat sent out
The pattern. Lev 4 grades the offering by the sinner: a commoner or leader brings a goat and the blood stays on the bronze altar; the anointed priest or the whole assembly brings a bull and the blood is carried in to the veil. Only the yearly rite reaches the mercy seat. The graded pattern is the priestly logic Milgrom (1991) and Sklar (2005) describe: impurity generated closer to the center reaches deeper into the sanctuary, so the blood must reach deeper to purge it. The scale runs the other way for what is unclean: lepers live outside the camp (13:46), the scapegoat is released there (16:22), and the burned carcasses go there too.

07 / THREE TIERS

Three tiers, three places

The tent's zones repeat a map the Torah has already drawn twice. Eden is a land, a garden inside it, and a tree at the center (Gen 2:8–9). Sinai is a base, a midpoint, and a summit (Ex 19; 24). BibleProject reads the tabernacle as the same map laid flat: the nearer the center, the fewer who may go and the more it costs to enter.

One direction, one door. In all three the movement runs inward toward a single point, and in all three the way is guarded: cherubim at the garden (Gen 3:24), fire and a boundary at Sinai (Ex 19:12; 24:17), cherubim on the veil and over the ark (Ex 25:18–20; 26:31). BibleProject's Exodus episodes read the tent as Eden brought down from the mountain into the camp, built after the pattern (tavnit) Moses is shown (Ex 25:9, 40), so that the presence that met him at the summit now rests among the people (40:34–35).

Where the text stops

Exodus never says the tent “is” Eden or Sinai. The correspondences are in shared vocabulary and layout: cherubim, gold and onyx (Gen 2:11–12; Ex 25:7), the eastward entrance, “work and keep” (Gen 2:15; Num 3:7–8), and seven divine speeches (Ex 25:1–31:17) that end in Sabbath (31:12–17). The three-tier map is a reading of that evidence, and it is marked here as one.

11 / FURNITURE

Why the furniture matters

Each object is specified twice, as instruction (Ex 25–30) and as build report (Ex 36–40), with a material, a measurement and a position, and almost never with an explanation. Each card below keeps the two apart: what the text fixes, then the reading BibleProject and the temple-theology literature draw from it, in which the furniture is at once a set of Eden and creation images and the machinery for keeping a holy presence in a camp of people who fail.

Ark and mercy seatʾaron · kapporet

TextAcacia chest, 2½ × 1½ × 1½ cubits, gold inside and out, poles never withdrawn (25:10–15). Solid gold cover with two cherubim of one piece, wings spread, faces toward it (25:17–20). The testimony inside (25:16); God speaks “from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim” (25:22).

ReadingA throne with no image on it. Where a neighbouring shrine set a statue on its box, this box holds words, and the LORD “is enthroned on the cherubim” (1 Sam 4:4; Ps 99:1). The lid is named for the rite that will be done on it (Lev 16:14). Mackie notes the Torah specifies two boxes in the same order, material then dimensions then fittings: Noah’s tebah and this ʾaron, both vessels for preserving life, and the pitching of the first (kafar, Gen 6:14) shares its root with the atoning done on the second.

Table of the bread of the Presenceshulḥan · leḥem panim

TextAcacia overlaid with gold, 2 × 1 × 1½ cubits, with a molding and rim and gold dishes and bowls; bread set “before me regularly” (25:23–30). North side of the Holy Place (26:35). Twelve loaves in two rows, renewed every Sabbath and eaten by the priests (Lev 24:5–9).

ReadingTwelve loaves for twelve tribes, kept always fresh and always “before the LORD.” Leviticus names the lamps and the bread in one breath (24:2–8), so Israel sits on the table under the light. BibleProject reads it as Eden’s perpetual food: a people held in God’s presence, provided for, and lit.

Lampstandmenorah

TextOne talent of hammered gold: a shaft and six branches, cups shaped like almond blossoms, seven lamps (25:31–40). South side, opposite the table (26:35). Tended evening and morning so it burns “regularly” (27:20–21; 30:7–8; Lev 24:2–4).

ReadingThe tent’s tree, blossoming in gold: the one plant form inside the tabernacle, where Solomon’s temple later carved whole gardens (1 Kgs 6:29). BibleProject reads the seven lamps as the lights of creation kept “evening and morning” in Genesis 1’s rhythm, a sevenfold light that never goes out.

Altar of incensemizbaḥ haqqetoret

TextAcacia overlaid with gold, 1 × 1 × 2 cubits, with horns, set before the veil (30:1–6). Incense morning and evening, with the lamps (30:7–8); blood on its horns once a year (30:10); a recipe reserved for it alone (30:34–38).

ReadingA cloud that never clears in the room before the veil. The Psalms make the image explicit, “let my prayer be counted as incense before you” (Ps 141:2), and on the Day of Atonement the censer’s cloud covers the mercy seat “so that he does not die” (Lev 16:12–13).

Bronze altarmizbaḥ haʿolah

TextAcacia boarded with bronze, 5 × 5 × 3 cubits, horns of one piece, a grating and poles, hollow (27:1–8). Its fire is never allowed to go out (Lev 6:12–13). The tamid: a lamb every morning and every evening (29:38–42).

ReadingThe threshold. No one goes in without a life surrendered here first, and the ordinary worshiper’s blood never goes further than its horns (Lev 4:30). BibleProject reads the daily ʿolah, wholly burned and offered for no one in particular, as perpetual surrender, its wording linked to Moriah (Gen 22) and Passover (Ex 12).

Basinkiyyor

TextBronze, made from the mirrors of the women who served at the entrance (38:8), set between the tent and the altar; priests wash hands and feet before entering or approaching the altar “so that they do not die” (30:17–21; 40:30–32).

ReadingWater for the priests’ bodies, blood for the sanctuary’s surfaces: two different cleanings. BibleProject borrows Milgrom’s picture of blood as a ritual detergent for the pollution that human failure leaves on holy things, and the basin keeps the ones who handle it clean enough to approach.

The tent, its coverings and its veilmishkan · parokhet

TextTen cherubim curtains, eleven of goats’ hair, rams’ skins, and taḥash hides (26:1–14) over gold-covered frames (26:15–30). A cherubim veil on four gold pillars; an embroidered screen on five at the door (26:31–37).

ReadingFour layers between the presence and the sun, and cherubim at each threshold, as at the garden (Gen 3:24). BibleProject reads the veil’s blue as sky, so that the high priest passes, in effect, through the heavens to stand before the throne; the text gives the colors and the guardians, and leaves the sky to the reader.

Built in sevens

Seven times “the LORD said to Moses” (Ex 25:1; 30:11, 17, 22, 34; 31:1, 12). The first speech lists seven things to make: the materials, the ark, the table, the lampstand, the tent, the altar, the court. Between the lists sit the priestly garments (28:4, 39–43), a seven-day ordination (29:35), and lamps and a lamb kept “evening and morning” (27:20–21; 29:38–42); the seventh speech is the Sabbath (31:12–17). BibleProject, adapting David Dorsey’s outline, reads the design as creation’s rhythm: an ordered space kept by human images of God.

God moves in, not away

The tent overturns a common half-truth, that a holy God keeps his distance from sin. The blueprints begin with the opposite intention, “that I may dwell in their midst” (Ex 25:8), and the whole apparatus of altar, basin, blood and veil exists so that the presence can stay in a compromised camp and the camp can come near on God’s terms (29:45–46; Lev 16:16). That is BibleProject’s summary of the section, and it is also the plain grammar of Exodus 25.

13 / SERVICE

The rhythm of service

What kept the tent running between one Day of Atonement and the next.

Twice daily
Lamps trimmed at evening and morning (Ex 27:20–21; 30:7–8). Incense on the golden altar (30:7–8). The tamid: a lamb with grain and drink offering, morning and twilight (Ex 29:38–42). Ashes cleared, fire kept burning (Lev 6:9–13).
Every Sabbath
Twelve fresh loaves set on the table; the old bread eaten by the priests in a holy place (Lev 24:5–9). Double daily offering (Num 28:9–10).
New moons and feasts
Extra offerings at each new moon (Num 28:11–15). Passover and Unleavened Bread, Weeks, Trumpets, Booths (Lev 23; Num 28–29). The court fills; the tent does not.
Once a year
Day of Atonement, 10th of the 7th month. The sanctuary itself is purged and the veil is passed, the only time (Lev 16:29–34; 23:26–32). Israel fasts and rests.
When the cloud lifts
Aaron's sons wrap the ark in the veil and cover every piece (Num 4:5–14). Kohathites carry the furniture, Gershonites the curtains, Merarites the frames (4:15–33). Levites set it up again where the cloud settles (Num 1:51; 9:15–23).
